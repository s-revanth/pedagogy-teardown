# OverSimplified: how it teaches

Counts use the 11 videos with reliable captions. fo2Rb9h788s (WW2 Part 2, Albanian machine-translated captions) is excluded from counts and used only as weak, unverified support for pacing and gag-as-chunk-marker. "n/12" in the brief becomes n/11 below.

## 1. Bottom line
OverSimplified teaches causation, not content: every country, class or institution becomes a character with one consistent want, and each causal hinge is performed as a short dialogue skit rather than stated as a date or a term. Structure comes from repetition: one template (tax, boycott, retreat; Missouri, then California; Italy, then Germany) is replayed until the viewer predicts the next turn, and a planted line or gag is paid off 5 to 20 minutes later. It has no notation and no checks for understanding. Comedy does the work that ramped difficulty does elsewhere, so it trades nuance for memorability and says so aloud.

## 2. Signature arc
1. Sponsor or admin cold-open (10/11), often before the hook.
2. Cheeky modern hook that turns the subject into a relatable scene: breakfast skit (Prohibition 0:15), toddler Henry (0:47), "Let's have a war" (WW1 0:02), Jimmy the tourist (Russian Rev 0:00).
3. A baseline or misconception set up and flipped: "Everyone must love this guy, right, wrong" (Russian Rev 2:48), "the issue of slavery is solved" (Civil War 3:51), Columbus (American Rev 0:47). Present in 6/11, partial in most others.
4. Roadmap or one-line cause chain (7/11): WW2 5:29, Napoleonic 12:03, WW1 1:58.
5. Template scene acted, then replayed with new actors (Civil War 3:35 then 4:57; American Rev 3:16 then 4:20; French Rev 4:50-6:46 then 8:34-9:20).
6. Chronology of escalating events, each hinge as skit, with a running gag or character as a timeline marker (McLean, Hoover/Miles x3 at 26:34/28:41/31:02, Pierre).
7. Tonal pivot or honest caveat when stakes are real (Civil War 8:08, Cold War 2 5:48).
8. Callback to the hook (10/11): Henry 25:44, French Rev 16:24, Civil War 26:43-28:52.
9. Teaser for next part, sponsor, or mock-moral shrug (Prohibition 33:01).

## 5. New principles / theories
1. **Template-replay lets the learner induce the rule (replay-with-new-actors).** Missouri then California (Civil War 3:35, 4:57); Italy then Germany (WW2 P1 2:48, 3:36); tax cycle twice (American Rev 3:48-4:53). Falsifiable: if a mechanism is shown with identical script structure twice with different actors before it is named, learners state the rule unprompted more often than when it is named after one instance.
2. **Characterise by a single want.** Each actor gets one stable desire and voice (Pierre, Louis, France and Austria in Napoleonic 5:58; "I want that thing" in WW2 6:01; three tsars in Russian Rev 4:08). Falsifiable: if abstract actors (institutions, variables) have one fixed want, learners predict their next move better than when roles vary scene to scene.
3. **Plant-and-pay-off as spaced retrieval without a quiz.** A throwaway line returns 5 to 20 minutes later (Henry 3:15 to 18:05; American Rev 8:34 to 11:37; McLean 18:27 to 26:43). Falsifiable: if a memorable detail is planted and later invoked, learners recall the causal link at delay better than when it is stated once.
4. **Flag the fabrication, then use it.** Declaring a made-up detail (WW2 P1 3:36) and compression (Cold War 2 5:48, 14:51; Civil War 8:08) buys trust, so the comic register can remain without misleading. Falsifiable: if invented illustrations are labelled at point of use, learners keep the gist and do not misattribute the invention.
5. **Tonal pivot signals a change of epistemic mode.** Civil War 8:08 strips the jokes to say what slavery meant, then quotes primary sources (12:13). Falsifiable: if a tone shift is announced when moving from structure to stakes, learners rate that content as higher-confidence and remember it as "the real part".
6. **Recurring pressure point makes the climax pre-understood.** Berlin is introduced at 6:27, 13:25 and 14:16 in Cold War 1 before the wall; Prohibition's loophole list pays off bootlegging. Falsifiable: if the climax object appears in smaller form earlier, learners predict the climax before the reveal.

## 6. What it does badly or never does
- No notation, no interface, no problem the learner can solve; zero inline quizzes beyond rhetorical asks and two games (Spot the Vietcong 5:16; Spot the French soldier 3:16).
- No ramp: starts at scale (WW2 P1, Prohibition). Difficulty is flat; humor equalises Mussolini and the Ardennes.
- Analogies are never broken or refined; countries as people hides economics and mass movements.
- No recap-as-test, no examples that change the learner's model; many videos end mid-story (WW1, WW2 P1).
- Nuance is traded for jokes; "no lesson" shrug ending (Prohibition 33:01).

## 7. Velocity translation
- **Scene builder:** a "skit" scene primitive: two or three labelled actors, a speech-bubble script, and a replay slot that swaps actors but keeps the script. Use it for any process (market, protocol, algorithm step).
- **Voice/beat script:** each actor gets one fixed want and a voice tag; the beat script marks plant tokens and scheduled pay-offs at 5, 10 and 20 minutes.
- **Orchestrator:** rule "template twice, then name": show the same mechanism with different actors before the label; delay naming until the learner predicts the third run (ghost hand pauses and asks).
- **Playbooks:** a "causal chain" playbook (baseline, flipped misconception, template scene, replay, pivot, callback) for history and social science; a spaced-callback scheduler.
- **Learner model:** record which plants were paid off and whether the learner predicted a replay; use that as the signal to compress or expand.
- **Ghost hands:** replays highlight the changed actor only.
- **Evals:** test for label-after-replay and flagged-fabrication; measure callback recall at delay; add the missing step OverSimplified lacks, a real prediction check.
