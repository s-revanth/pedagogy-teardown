## 2. Full narration script (written in the channel's voice)

**[0:00] Hook.**
Mathematics is supposed to be the one place where we can be sure. In science, you test an idea and it can be overturned by the next experiment. In maths, once something is proved, it is proved, forever. Two plus two is four today, and it will be four in a billion years. That is the promise. But about a hundred years ago, a handful of people discovered that something is wrong at the foundation. Not a small error. Something built in.

**[0:45] The question.**
So here is the question I want to ask. Is every true statement in mathematics provable? In other words, if something is true, is there always, somewhere, a proof of it, even if nobody has found it yet? Most people say yes. And a lot of the best mathematicians of the early twentieth century said yes. I want you to commit to an answer before we go on. Yes, or no. Write it down, or type it in the comments. And hold on to it, because by the end of this video, I think you may not be sure what the question even means.

**[1:30] Roadmap.**
Here is where we are going. We start with a dream: that all of mathematics could be put on a perfectly solid foundation, so that every question has an answer and no contradiction can ever sneak in. Then we will watch three attempts to build that foundation, a paradox that smashed the first one, a young logician who proved something shocking about the second one, and a machine that finished the job. By the end, you will see why the flaw is not an accident.

**[2:10] Hilbert.**
The story starts in 1900, at a conference in Paris, with a German mathematician called David Hilbert. He was probably the most influential mathematician alive. He stood up and read out a list of twenty-three problems that he thought would drive maths for the next century. And underneath this list was a belief. Every problem on it has a solution. Maybe it is hard, maybe nobody has found it yet, but it exists. There is no such thing as a question in mathematics that cannot be answered.

**[3:20] Foundations.**
To see why that sounds reasonable, you need to think about how maths is built. The model for everything was Euclid, two thousand years earlier. Euclid does not just state that the angles of a triangle add up to a hundred and eighty degrees. He starts with a few simple assumptions, called axioms, such as that you can join any two points with a straight line, and then he derives everything else from those, one step at a time. It is like a building on a foundation. If the foundation is solid and every step is valid, the whole thing stands.

**[4:30] The first crack.**
But even Euclid had a weak spot. One of his axioms, the parallel postulate, always looked a bit clumsy to people, and for centuries they tried to prove it from the others. They failed. And in the nineteenth century, mathematicians worked out why. You can throw the axiom away, or replace it, and get a different geometry, one where the angles of a triangle add up to more than a hundred and eighty degrees, like triangles drawn on a ball. And that geometry is just as consistent as Euclid's. So maths was not describing a single fixed truth about space. It was a game that depended on which rules you chose. And that raises a worrying question: if you can choose the rules, how do you know your rules will never contradict themselves?

**[5:50] Three properties.**
Let me define the three things Hilbert wanted. An axiom is a statement you accept without proof. A proof is a chain of steps, each following by a rule from the axioms or earlier steps. Now, he wanted a system of axioms that was, first, consistent: you can never prove both a statement and its opposite. Second, complete: for every statement, either it or its opposite can be proved. And third, decidable: there is a mechanical method that can tell you, for any statement, which one. Consistent, complete, decidable. That was the dream, and I will come back to this list.

**[7:00] Sets.**
The most ambitious attempt to build everything from scratch was based on sets. A set is just a collection of things. A bag of marbles. A set of even numbers. Mathematicians in the late nineteenth century, Cantor and then Frege, showed that you could build numbers, and from numbers nearly everything else, out of nothing but sets. It was so elegant that it felt like the foundation had been found.

**[8:10] Bags that hold bags.**
Now, think about a bag. Can a bag contain other bags? Sure. A bag of bags. Can a bag contain itself? Strange, but nothing says it cannot. So take the following. Some bags contain themselves and some do not. Gather together every bag that does not contain itself and put them in one big bag. Call it R. Now ask a simple question: does R contain itself?

**[9:10] The paradox.**
Suppose it does. Then it is a bag that contains itself, but R was only supposed to hold the bags that do not. So it cannot. Fine, suppose it does not contain itself. Then it is a bag that does not contain itself, and so it should be in R, so it does. Either way, contradiction. This is Russell's paradox, and it means the notion of a set that mathematicians had been using was inconsistent. Not wrong in some far-off corner, but wrong in a way you could state in a few lines.

**[10:10] The barber.**
There is a version of this that people tell with a barber. A barber in a village shaves everyone who does not shave themselves, and nobody else. Who shaves the barber? If he shaves himself, he should not. If he does not, he should. The barber cannot exist. And in maths the problem was that the thing the barber stood for, the bag R, looked like it should exist, because the rules said it could be built.

**[11:10] Frege.**
Frege had just finished the second volume of his life's work, built on sets, when he received a short letter from a young Bertrand Russell pointing out the paradox. He added an appendix to the book admitting that the foundation had collapsed just as the work was finished. Imagine finishing a book and finding out the first page is wrong.

**[12:20] Rescue one.**
So people tried to fix it. The first rescue was Russell's own. Do not allow a set to refer to itself at all, by sorting things into levels: things, collections of things, collections of collections. It does block the paradox. But it is heavy. Russell and Whitehead wrote a three-volume book, the Principia Mathematica, and it takes them hundreds of pages before they prove that one plus one equals two. It works, but it is so cumbersome that nobody would want to do maths in it.

**[13:40] Rescue two.**
The second rescue was to tighten the rules for building sets so that the bag R simply cannot be built. And that worked as far as anyone could tell. But "as far as anyone could tell" is not what Hilbert wanted. He wanted a proof that the rules would never lead to a contradiction. Not that nobody had found one yet. And that is a very different thing to ask for.

**[14:40] Hilbert's confidence.**
In 1930, Hilbert gave a speech. He had retired, and he told an audience that there is no such thing as an unsolvable problem. He ended with a line that was later put on his gravestone: we must know, we will know. It is a wonderful sentence. And it is possibly the high point of confidence in the history of maths. Because the day before that speech, at a conference in the same city, a young man had quietly announced something that said the opposite.

**[15:30] Sponsor.**
Sponsor read, around a minute.

**[16:20] Gödel.**
That young man was Kurt Gödel. He was twenty-four, shy, and he was doing his PhD work on this very question. And he realised that you could take an old paradox, the liar, and turn it into a tool. The liar sentence is "this sentence is false." If it is true, then it is false. If it is false, then it is true. Nobody can assign it a truth value. Gödel's idea was to change one word.

**[17:30] The notch.**
Replace "false" with "unprovable". Now the sentence says: "this sentence cannot be proved." Think about that. If it can be proved, then the system has proved something false, because the sentence says it cannot be. So the system would be inconsistent. But if it cannot be proved, then what it says is exactly right: it is true, and it cannot be proved. And so if the system is consistent, there is a true sentence that the system cannot prove.

**[18:40] Numbers that talk about maths.**
There is an obvious objection. Maths is about numbers, not about sentences. How can a statement in arithmetic talk about itself? Gödel's answer was to give every symbol a number. Let's do that with a small alphabet. Plus is one, equals is two, zero is three, and so on. A formula is a string of symbols, so it becomes a string of numbers, and you can turn that string into one big number by using prime numbers. First prime to the power of the first number, second prime to the power of the second, and so on. Each formula now has its own number, and from the number you can read the formula back.

**[20:00] The compact version.**
So now a statement about formulas becomes a statement about numbers. "This is a valid proof of that formula" turns into a plain arithmetic relation between two numbers. The statement we are building can be written, roughly, as: there is no number which is the code of a proof of the formula with this code. And it does not matter yet that it looks dense. What matters is that it is an ordinary statement about whole numbers.

**[21:00] Building it.**
Here is how you make the formula talk about itself. Step one, write a formula with a gap in it that says: the formula with code x is not provable. Step two, take its own code, call it g, and plug that number into the gap. Step three, you now have a statement that says: the formula with code g is not provable, and the formula with code g is this very formula. You can try it with a short formula first, and check that plugging its code into itself gives a new, longer one, and that the same recipe works however long the formula is. The recipe never needs to be invented again. Once you have it, it applies to any system that can do arithmetic.

**[22:20] The result.**
So here is what Gödel proved. In any consistent system of axioms strong enough to do basic arithmetic, there are true statements that cannot be proved within the system. This is the first incompleteness theorem. And look at what that does to the old belief. True and provable were supposed to be the same thing. They are not. Provable is a smaller circle inside true, and no amount of adding axioms makes the circle fill the whole thing. If you add the missing sentence as an axiom, the new system has its own new unprovable sentence.

**[23:30] What it does not say.**
I should be careful here. This is not saying maths is unreliable. It does not say that we cannot know anything. It only applies to systems strong enough for arithmetic, and the sentence Gödel built is a rather artificial one. Mathematicians do not trip over it in their daily work. What it does say is that no single list of rules can capture all of arithmetic.

**[24:30] The meaning for Hilbert.**
The same argument also says something about the second property: a system cannot prove its own consistency. So of the three things Hilbert wanted, he cannot have consistent and complete, and he cannot even get the guarantee of consistency from inside. And that was when many mathematicians accepted that the dream was in trouble. But there was one thing left: decidability. Maybe some systems have unprovable statements, but could there still be a mechanical method that tells you which statements are provable and which are not?

**[25:30] Turing.**
That is where a British student called Alan Turing comes in. He was twenty-three. To answer this, he needed a precise idea of what a mechanical method is. So he invented one. Not a physical machine, but an idea, a stand-in for a person with a pencil and paper.

**[26:30] The machine.**
It is very simple. There is a long tape divided into squares, each can hold a symbol. A head reads one square at a time. And there is a small table of rules that says: if you see this symbol, in this state, write that, move left or right, and change state. That is all. Yet any computation that any computer can do, can be done by a machine like this. And one more point. The rule table of a machine can be written as symbols on a tape. So a machine can be given another machine as input. That is exactly what we did with Gödel numbers, and it is the key to everything that follows.

**[27:30] Halting.**
Now ask about a single question: given a program and an input, will the program eventually stop, or will it run forever? Take two tiny programs. One counts from one to ten and stops. The other keeps adding one and never stops. You can look at them and tell. But what about a program that checks whether every even number is the sum of two primes and stops if it finds a counterexample? Does it ever stop? To answer that, you would have to solve a famous open problem.

**[28:30] Can you write a checker?**
So here is a challenge. Could you write a program, call it H, that takes any other program and its input and tells you correctly whether it halts? Think about how you would try. The obvious thing is to run the program and watch. If it halts, you say yes. But if it does not halt, you are still waiting. At no point can you say, it has gone on long enough. So brute force fails, and what you need is a way to tell without running forever.

**[29:30] The diagonal trick.**
Here is the argument, and it is the same trick as the liar and the bag. Suppose H exists. Build a new program D that takes a program as input and asks H what it will do when it is fed itself. If H says it halts, D deliberately loops forever. If H says it loops, D stops straight away. D does the opposite of whatever H predicts. Now feed D to itself. If H says D halts, then D loops, so H was wrong. If H says D loops, then D halts, so H was wrong again. The only way out is that H does not exist.

**[30:50] Consequences.**
So no program can decide halting. And that means no mechanical method can decide which statements of maths are provable, because if it could, you could use it to solve halting. Decidability falls. And this has an unsettling consequence. Many open problems, like the Goldbach conjecture or Collatz, can be phrased as will this program ever stop. It is possible that some of them are true and have no proof at all.

**[31:50] What mathematicians do now.**
It sounds like the end, but it is not quite. We now know of statements that really are independent of the standard axioms. The continuum hypothesis, about the sizes of infinity, is one of them: you can add it or its opposite and nothing breaks. So mathematicians treat axioms more as a menu. Choose some, see what you can build, and be honest about it.

**[32:40] Callback.**
So, back to the question I asked at the beginning. Is every true statement provable? The answer is no. And it is a proved no, a proof of the limits of proof. I find that comforting, in a strange way. Maths is still certain. A proof is still a proof. It is just that truth is bigger than any set of rules we can write down. And Hilbert's line on the gravestone is still true as a sentiment. We must know. It is the second half, we will know, that turned out to be false.

**[33:30] Outro.**
Thanks, patreon, and a pointer to the next video.
