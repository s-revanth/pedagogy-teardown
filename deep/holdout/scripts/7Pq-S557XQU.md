## 2. Full narration script

(about 2,300 words, targeting roughly 155-165 wpm, flat and fast)

You are a horse. The year is 1900, and you have had a good career. You pull a cart, you pull a plow, you pull a carriage full of people who are late. Your whole job is to pull things, and nothing about that job seems to be going anywhere. Sure, there are these new engines wheezing around town, but they are loud, they break down, and they are obviously a toy for people with too much money. So you go back to pulling. And then, within a few decades, the number of horses in the country drops off a cliff.

Now, here is the story you probably believe when you hear that. Machines are tools. Tools make workers more productive. A more productive worker is a more valuable worker. So the horse, equipped with a better tool, should have done fine. That is the comfortable story, and it is the one the whole video is about breaking. So go ahead, nod along. You will be asked to give it up in a few minutes.

To see why it is wrong, we need a tiny model, and it takes three rules. Rule one: work gets done by whatever does it cheapest. Not best, cheapest. Rule two: a horse costs money to exist. Oats, a stable, a vet, a person to look after it. It costs this whether or not there is any work to do. Rule three: a horse can pull. That is the entire skill list. Three rules. We will call the point where those rules bite the oats line. It is the place where doing the work with a machine costs less than keeping the horse alive to do it.

Now, a farm. Ten horses, one tractor for sale. Do not worry about any other number yet. The tractor does not have to be good. It does not need to be elegant or beautiful or have the horse's charm. It has to do an hour of pulling for less than an hour of horse. When it does, the farmer does not feel cruel. He just does the arithmetic. One horse leaves, then three, and then, because the stable and the farrier and the feed store no longer have enough business, the cost of keeping each remaining horse goes up, which pushes the oats line the wrong way for the ones that are left.

Notice what did not happen to the horse. It did not get a bad deal. It got no deal. A few horses found new work, racing and pets and ceremonies, but the number of those jobs was tiny next to the number of jobs that vanished. The tool did not make the horse more valuable. The tool made the horse's one skill unnecessary, and one skill is all a horse had.

Which brings up the obvious objection, and I want to give it its due. You are not a horse. You can retrain. You can learn to do a different job. Of the three rules, that is the one place where horses and people differ. So the question for the rest of this video is not whether the story fits horses. It does. The question is whether rule three holds for you.

Let us run the same rules on people, starting with the simplest kind of job: muscle. A factory floor used to be full of people who lifted, welded and screwed things together. Replace the horse with a worker, replace the tractor with a robot arm, and check. Can the machine do the task? Is it cheaper than the worker's upkeep, which is a wage, a break room, and a person who gets sick? Yes and yes, in more and more of the building. Same cause again. The oats line.

Second setting: driving. Think about one truck for one day. A person sits in a seat, watches a road, and turns a wheel. Now think about a driverless truck for that same day. It watches the road, turns the wheel, and does not need a lunch break or a night's sleep. Millions of jobs in the world are really this one skill. Taxi driver, bus driver, delivery driver, truck driver. Different uniforms, same task. If the machine drops below the oats line for that one task, it does not take one job. It takes a category. You, the driver, are not a special case. You are a horse with a nicer hat.

Third setting, and this is the one where people start to get uncomfortable: thinking jobs. Look at a desk. Someone sorts a list. Someone checks a number against another number. Someone looks something up, copies it, and files it. Someone reads a form and decides which pile it belongs in. That is a lot of what sits behind the word office. Count them up with me. Sort. Check. Look up. File. Decide a pile. That is five tasks, and each of them is something a program can do for close to nothing. When you count your own days honestly, how many of your hours are in that list?

Now, I can feel you thinking of the fix, so here it is. You say: fine. Machines take the dull jobs, people retrain, and human beings move on to whatever the machines cannot do. New jobs, always. That is what has happened every time before. That sounds like a good ending, so let us stop here and take it.

Except no. Here is the problem. Look at the new job. A new job is also a task, or a handful of tasks. And the machine that learned to drive did not learn one trade. It learned how to learn a trade. Rule three said a horse can pull and nothing else, and that was why the horse had nowhere to go. But the new machine is not a tractor. A tractor is a one-trick horse replacement. This is a machine that can be pointed at the next job, and the next one, and then at the job you retrained into. That is a different thing from anything the horse faced, and it is the reason rule three suddenly looks shaky for you.

So run the check once more on the jobs that feel the most human. Writing, designing, deciding who to hire. People say those cannot be automated because they need creativity. Notice that nobody is using the oats line when they say that. They are using a feeling. The oats line asks one boring question: can the machine produce something close enough, for much less? If the answer is yes for enough of the work, it does not matter whether the machine is creative. It matters whether the output is cheaper than you. Same cause, again.

Now, a place where I should be careful, because the horse comparison breaks, and it breaks in a way that matters. Horses did not vote. They did not buy anything. They did not write laws. They could not decide that the economy should work differently. You can. So the horse story is not a prediction of what society will do. It is a description of what the arithmetic does when nobody changes the rules.

And with that, we are into speculation time. Everything before this was mechanism, and I stand by it. Everything in this next minute is a guess. How fast this happens, whether entire new categories of human work appear, whether people end up working fewer hours or fewer people end up working at all: I do not know, and anyone who tells you they do is selling something. Keep the fence between those two things in your head. The mechanism is not the guess.

So, here are the rules again. Rule one: the cheapest way to do the work wins. Rule two: whatever is doing the work costs money to keep alive. Rule three: a worker is only safe if there is somewhere else for them to go. And a rule zero that sits on top of all three: none of this is about being good at the job. It is about cost. Try it on a job I did not mention. Pick one. Ask whether the machine can do the task, ask whether it is below the oats line, and ask whether there is a next door to walk through. If you can answer those three questions, you did not need me.

Which leaves us back where we started, in 1900, with a horse standing in a street. That horse was not stupid, and it was not lazy. It was very good at the one thing it did. It simply was not in the room when the arithmetic was decided. The cliff on the chart did not come from a villain. It came from a line being crossed.

So the thing worth asking yourself is not whether you are better than a horse. You probably are. The question is whether you are better than a machine that costs less than your oats.

Good luck out there.
