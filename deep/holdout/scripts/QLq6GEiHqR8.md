## 2. Full narration script

[SPONSOR, about 45 s, admin]

First, a quick word from today's sponsor. Have you ever wanted something to arrive at your house without having to leave it? Of course you have. That is what this service does, and it works, and I used it, and as far as I can tell it did not start a war with another nation, which is more than I can say for today's topic. There is a link in the description. Now let's go.

[HOOK]

Alright, so picture this. You are in a quiet neighborhood, and your neighbor's pig has been eating your garden. Again. You are tired, you are annoyed, and you grab something that makes a loud bang. And that, my friends, is how two of the strongest countries in the world nearly started a war.

In 1859, a man shot a pig on a small island in the Pacific Northwest, and within weeks, thousands of soldiers, five warships, and a couple of governors were staring at each other down the barrels of cannons. And the pig is the only casualty in the entire conflict. This is the Pig War.

[MISCONCEPTION FLIP]

Now, you might think: a war over a pig, that is so silly that it must have been a joke. Some bored people with too many guns, a funny animal, everyone goes home. And it is a good story. But, no. The pig did not cause the war. The pig was a very tiny spark in a very large barrel of gunpowder. The real problem was a map, and a sloppy treaty, and a lot of people who wanted the same piece of land. So let's talk about the real cause.

[ROADMAP]

Here is the plan. First, I will show you how two countries ended up sharing a giant chunk of land. Then, how a lazy treaty left a gap big enough to drive a warship through. Then, how settlers on both sides moved onto one small island. Then comes the pig. Then comes the escalation, where each side sends more soldiers, then more, then more, until a single sensible person finally says "this is dumb." And at the end, I will tell you how it finally got resolved, which took twelve years and a German emperor. Let's start at the beginning.

[BASELINE]

In the early 1800s, the area now called the Pacific Northwest, which covers what is today Oregon, Washington, and parts of Canada, is a vast wilderness called the Oregon Country. Two countries want it: the United States and Great Britain. And they have been fighting over it politely, in the way that two people can fight over a parking space without technically yelling. So they come to an arrangement: it is shared. Joint occupation. Basically a shared fridge, where no one has labeled anything.

And the British side is run, effectively, by a company: the Hudson's Bay Company. It is the biggest fur-trading business in the area, and a fur hat was, in those days, the equivalent of owning a nice car. The company is not exactly a government, but it acts like one. It has forts, it has rules, and it has a very polite manager type who says things like "we shall see about that."

Hudson's Bay manager: "We have been trading beavers for decades. We rather like this arrangement."

[AMERICANS ARRIVE]

But the Americans have a different idea. In the 1840s, wagons start rolling along a long trail from the east, packed with families who want farmland. They are not interested in beavers. They want fields. And if you are an American in the 1840s, you have a very particular belief that the entire continent is your destiny. They even have a name for it: manifest destiny, which is a fancy way of saying "I would like that, please."

American settler: "Is this land taken?"

Hudson's Bay manager: "Well, we have been here for a while..."

American settler: "Great, so it is free."

That is not what the manager said, but it is how the settler heard it. And as more wagons arrive, the number of Americans in the region grows, and the Hudson's Bay people start to feel like guests at their own dinner.

[POLK]

Now we need a president, and in 1844 the Americans elect James K. Polk, and he runs on a very loud and very simple promise: take the entire Oregon Country, all the way up to the Russian border. His slogan is basically a number and a threat: fifty-four forty or fight. Which, honestly, is a great slogan for a toddler, and an awful one for a president.

But here is the problem. Right after he takes office, he picks a fight with Mexico, a war that is going to take most of the army, and then he looks at the other map and realizes that he does not want a second war with Great Britain at the same time.

Polk: "Remember how I said we would fight for all of it?"

Advisor: "Yes?"

Polk: "How about half?"

Meanwhile, the British are looking at their own list. They are not enthusiastic either. Beavers are going out of fashion, their hats are less popular, and the area is far away. So both sides sit down and agree on a compromise.

[THE TREATY]

The Oregon Treaty of 1846 is clean, simple, and almost completely sensible. The border will follow the forty-ninth parallel, a straight line, from the Rocky Mountains to the ocean. Then, because the southern tip of Vancouver Island dips below that line, the treaty says that the border will go down to the middle of the channel that separates the island from the mainland, then south through the channel, then out to the ocean.

Do you see the problem? Read it again: "the channel."

Because here is the thing about the sea between Vancouver Island and the mainland: there is not one channel. There are two. And they are not close to each other. And between them sits a cluster of islands called the San Juan Islands.

[TWO CHANNELS, REPLAY]

So let's see what each side said, and I want you to notice that it is the same argument twice.

The British look at the map and say, "Well, obviously the border goes through the channel on the east, a thing called Rosario Strait. Which makes all the islands British. Great."

British diplomat: "I am looking at the map and it is very clear. The channel is the one over here."

American diplomat: "No, it is the channel over here, to the west, a thing called Haro Strait. Which makes all the islands American. Great."

American diplomat: "I am looking at the map and it is very clear. The channel is the one over here."

Same sentence, same confidence, completely different channel. And since both sides had perfectly reasonable arguments, and both sides had a map with the border drawn in the convenient place, nobody was willing to give in. And because the two countries had just avoided a big war over the whole region, nobody wanted to start a new one over a bunch of rocks. So they did the most British thing and the most American thing: they ignored it.

[SAN JUAN ISLAND]

And at first, the plan works fine. The islands are small and nobody lives there. But one island in particular, San Juan Island, has good grass and a very sunny climate. The Hudson's Bay Company, being smart and having no government telling them otherwise, sets up a sheep farm on the island and calls it Belle Vue, which sounds very elegant for a place covered in sheep. They bring in a few thousand sheep. They bring in workers. And as far as the company is concerned, this is British land.

Hudson's Bay manager: "Our sheep. Our grass. Our island."

[TAX LOOP]

Then, American settlers arrive, and a new problem appears: tax. In 1853, the American territory of Washington is organized, and the first thing a new government does is figure out whom to tax. And the American tax collector looks at the island and notices that there are thousands of sheep, none of them taxed.

American tax collector: "Hello, I am here to collect tax on your sheep."

Hudson's Bay manager: "This is British land. We do not pay your taxes."

American tax collector: "This is American land. And you do."

Hudson's Bay manager: "Take it up with London."

American tax collector: "Take it up with Washington."

And the collector leaves and comes back. And the same argument happens again. And again. Each side says the same thing. Neither side moves. It is like arguing with someone about whose turn it is to do the dishes, except there are sheep.

And I want you to notice this for later: this is the shape of the whole story. A problem arises. Each side claims the land. Each side refuses to move. Each side adds a bit more force to its claim. We are about to see this happen over and over.

[GOLD RUSH]

Now things speed up. In 1858, gold is found on the Fraser River on the mainland, which is basically the 1850s version of an internet trend. Tens of thousands of people rush in, many of them Americans. And a lot of them pass through the islands and decide that the islands are pretty good. So some of them stay.

By 1859, there are around twenty-five American settlers on San Juan Island, some of them squatting on land that the Hudson's Bay Company is using. They have no real legal claim to it. They are just standing there. And among them is a man named Lyman Cutlar.

[THE PIG]

Let me tell you about Lyman Cutlar. He is a farmer. He is from Kentucky, and he has staked a claim on a patch of ground in the middle of the island, a spot he has decided to call his own, even though the company thinks it is theirs. And he has planted potatoes. Now, I will be honest, I do not know how big this potato patch is, how many potatoes there were, or how emotionally attached he was to them. But I am going to say, for the sake of storytelling, that these are the most important potatoes in the world.

And on the morning of June 15, 1859, Cutlar steps out of his cabin, and he sees something in his garden.

Cutlar: "Is that... a pig?"

Pig: "Oink."

Cutlar: "Is it eating my potatoes?"

Pig: "Oink oink."

And this is not the first time. The pig, we are told, has a habit of wandering through the garden. It belongs to Charles Griffin, who runs the Hudson's Bay sheep farm, and the pig is a big, healthy one, a Berkshire, which, I am told, is a fancy kind. It has a taste for American vegetables.

Cutlar loses it. He grabs his gun, and he shoots the pig. A very dead pig.

And then, because he is not a monster, he feels a little bad, and he goes to the neighbor to apologize and to offer to pay. And this is where it goes wrong.

Cutlar: "Look, I shot your pig. I am sorry. Here is ten dollars."

Griffin: "Ten dollars? That is a prize pig. It is worth a hundred."

Cutlar: "A hundred? For a pig that was eating my potatoes?"

Griffin: "That is what I said."

And I have to point something out, which is that a hundred dollars was a very large amount of money in 1859. It is more than a farm worker would earn in a month or two, and it was ten times what Cutlar offered. They both knew that the number was inflated, which is why it became a fight, and not a conversation.

[THREAT AND PANIC]

And then, the Hudson's Bay Company gets involved. The company officials say they will arrest Cutlar and take him to the British colony on Vancouver Island for trial. Which, if you think about it, is quite a thing to say to an American citizen who is, in his own mind, on American soil.

Company official: "We will have to take you to Victoria for trial."

Cutlar: "You will do no such thing."

And the American settlers panic. They know that the British have a navy, and they know that they have nobody. So they write to the closest American military commander, and they say: please help.

Let's pause there. Look at the pattern: someone makes a claim, someone refuses, someone threatens, and the person threatened asks for bigger help. This is the loop. And we are about to run it with soldiers.

[HARNEY]

The closest commander is Brigadier General William Harney, in charge of the American army in that area. And Harney is, let us say, not a cautious man. He has a famous temper, he has a long history of aggression, and, crucially, he is a man who likes the idea of taking the islands. He reads the letters, and he sees an opportunity.

Harney: "An American is being threatened by the British? On American soil? We will send soldiers."

Advisor: "Sir, are you sure? We do not know for certain that it is American soil."

Harney: "It is now."

So he sends a captain named George Pickett, a man with long hair and a strong personality, and a few dozen soldiers, to the island, to protect the Americans. And I would just like to mention that this same George Pickett is going to become very famous in a few years, for something else. We will get to that.

[PICKETT LANDS]

In July, Pickett lands on San Juan Island with around sixty soldiers, and he announces that the island is American and that no British laws apply. He sets up a camp near the water.

Pickett: "By order of General Harney, this land is United States territory."

Hudson's Bay official: "That is unusual, because I have a map that says otherwise."

Pickett: "My map says I am right."

Now, on the other side, the governor of the British colony on Vancouver Island, James Douglas, is furious. Douglas is not exactly a stranger to this land. He used to work for the Hudson's Bay Company, and he is not about to let some American officer take their island. So he does the same thing Harney did: he asks for backup.

[ESCALATION LOOP]

And now we get to the part where the pig is long forgotten, and we are into a straight arm-wrestling match between two empires. And I want to show it to you in a loop, because that is how it happened.

Round one: the British send a warship, HMS Tribune, under a captain who is calm and reasonable. The ship anchors off the island. It has guns. They are pointed at the Americans.

Round two: the Americans see the warship, and Pickett asks for more men. Harney sends more. Now there are over four hundred American soldiers on the island, with cannons.

Round three: the British see this, and they send more ships. Soon there are five warships in the water, with something over two thousand men on board, and a lot more guns than the Americans have.

Round four: the Americans, in their camp on the beach, look at five warships and say, we will stay anyway.

You see? Same shape each time. Somebody blinks, but then somebody else blinks louder. And the result is that, in the space of a few weeks, an island with twenty-five people, a few hundred sheep and one dead pig has more firepower around it than most countries.

[STANDOFF]

Now at this point you are probably thinking: surely somebody will have the sense to stop this. And, in fact, some people did, but they were not the ones in charge.

On the beach, American soldiers and British sailors can see each other. And they do what soldiers on both sides always do in a boring standoff: they glare. The American captain, Pickett, tells his men to dig in. A British captain, a very reasonable man, comes ashore to talk, and he asks Pickett to join the British in occupying the island together. And Pickett says no.

British captain: "Perhaps we could share the island, just until we hear from London?"

Pickett: "No."

British captain: "I am being very polite."

Pickett: "Still no."

And according to the stories, Pickett says something along the lines of: "If you try to land, we will fight you, and we will make it a Bunker Hill." Which is a dramatic reference to an American battle where the British won but lost loads of men. So, in other words: we will lose, but we will make you pay.

I am flagging that this is a paraphrase of a legend, and I do not know the exact words. But the point is that a few hundred men on a beach were ready to fight a fleet.

[THE SENSIBLE ADMIRAL]

Back in Victoria, Governor Douglas is out of patience. He tells the senior naval officer, Rear Admiral Robert Baynes, to land marines on the island and kick the Americans out. And Baynes, who has just sailed in, looks at the map, looks at the situation, and says no.

Douglas: "Admiral, I have given you an order."

Baynes: "Governor, I am not going to start a war between two great nations over a pig."

I will admit that I made up the exact line, but that is basically what he said: he did not think a squabble about a pig was a good reason for his country to go to war, and he did not think that was what his government wanted. And so, in a feat of maturity that is rare in this story, the admiral refuses a direct order from the governor, and nobody fires.

And that is one adult in the room, in a story about one pig and about four hundred and sixty thoroughly overexcited men.

[WASHINGTON REACTS]

Meanwhile, back in Washington, the president reads the news and asks the obvious question: who sent an army to an island over a pig? The answer is Harney, and the president is not pleased. He sends someone older and calmer: General Winfield Scott, the most senior officer in the American army, a man who is famous for being both a war hero and a peacemaker.

Scott: "Gentlemen, I have come to clean this up."

Harney: "But, sir..."

Scott: "No."

And Scott arrives in the fall, sits down with Governor Douglas, and they agree on the most reasonable solution: nobody gets the island. Not yet. Instead, each side will keep a small garrison there, around a hundred soldiers apiece, until the border can be figured out for good. This is called joint occupation. It is basically a shared fridge again, but with guns.

[TWELVE YEARS OF PEACE]

And this works amazingly well. The British put their camp at the north end of the island, with a fancy garden and a proper little village. The Americans put theirs at the south end, on a windy prairie. And for twelve years, the two camps basically throw parties for each other. They celebrate the Fourth of July together. They celebrate Queen Victoria's birthday together. They have sports. They have dinners. At one point, a British officer is said to have grown the nicest garden on the island, and an American officer said it was the second nicest, behind his own.

I am not sure about that last bit, but it is in keeping with the mood. For twelve years, the "war" over the island is the most gentle one in history.

[TONAL PIVOT]

Now, a serious note. While this is going on, the United States is falling apart. In 1861, the Civil War begins, and most of the officers who were on the island leave to fight. Including, yes, Captain Pickett, who joined the Confederacy, and who is now remembered for a famous charge at Gettysburg that ended in disaster. So the man who confidently said he would hold the island against a fleet is going to have a much worse day, a few years later.

It is a reminder that the thing I have been joking about was, on the surface, a small and lucky escape. Because if one commander had decided to fire, then those sailors and soldiers would be dead, and the two nations would have been at war, right as the United States was about to tear itself apart.

[ARBITRATION]

Okay, so how does this end? In 1871, the United States and Great Britain, after the Civil War, sit down and sign a big treaty to clear up a lot of old arguments, and one of the issues is the island dispute. And they do something wonderfully modern: they hire a referee. They pick the German emperor, Kaiser Wilhelm the First, to decide which channel is the right one.

Kaiser Wilhelm: "So, which channel, again?"

The Kaiser asks a commission of three experts to look at the maps and the arguments. And, after a year of study, they say the American channel, Haro Strait, is the correct one. And that means, in 1872, the border is fixed, the San Juan Islands become American, and the British soldiers quietly pack up and go home.

And so the islands are American today, though British Columbia is right next door, and if you ever take a ferry through them, you will go through exactly the channel that everyone argued about.

[CALLBACK AND ENDING]

And that is the Pig War. Twelve years of occupation, five warships, a few hundred soldiers, a lot of ego, and a single casualty. A pig.

So, let's go back to the fence. The neighbor, the garden, and the thing that went bang. In the end, nothing about the pig mattered, except that it was a very good excuse. The real problem was a sentence in a treaty that said "the channel" when there were two. And the real lesson, if you want one, is that the most dangerous place in the world is a border nobody has drawn properly, with people on both sides who are very tired of waiting.

Or maybe the real lesson is: keep your pigs on your own side of the fence. I do not know. I will let you decide.
