## 2. Full narration script

(about 1,700 words, targeting roughly 160-170 wpm)

Congratulations. You are eleventh in line to be president of the United States. It is not a job you wanted, and it is not a job you applied for, and today it is a job you are about to get, because everyone ahead of you is in one room, and something has gone very wrong with that room. What is your plan? Well, your country already has one. It is called the line of succession, and the strange thing is not that it is bad. The strange thing is that nobody ever sat down and designed it.

Now, you probably think the opposite. Of all the things a country must get right, the loss of its leader is the most predictable. Presidents die. Presidents get sick. Presidents get shot. So surely this is a careful, engineered list, built by serious people with clipboards. Hold on to that belief, because the next ten minutes will not be kind to it.

Here is the model, in three rules. Rule one: the Constitution names exactly one backup, the vice president, and then waves its hand and says Congress will sort out the rest. Rule two: Congress did sort it out, by writing a list of offices in order. Rule three: whoever is next must actually be allowed to be president, which means old enough, born here, and a resident long enough. If they are not, you skip them and move down. Those three rules, and one cause that explains almost everything strange in this video: the list was written by whoever happened to be in the room.

Start with the simple case, because it works. The president dies. The vice president takes over. Nobody is confused, nobody argues, and the country goes on. That is the version of the plan that people imagine when they say the plan is fine. Now remove the vice president. Now you need the list. And this is where it starts to look like something other than engineering.

Imagine you are asked to design the list yourself, the dumb way. You do not start with a principle. You look around for people who already have offices and some authority, and you line them up. That is, roughly, what happened, a layer at a time, over a couple of centuries. Each new law fixed the last disaster and brought in a new disagreement. So do not read this as a plan. Read it as an archaeological site.

Link one is the Speaker of the House. This sounds reasonable until you realize the Speaker is a leader of the party that holds the House, which may not be the party that holds the White House. So the same event that kills a president could also flip which party runs the executive branch, without a single voter being asked. Rule two put the Speaker there, and the cause, a list written by whoever was in the room, explains why.

Link two is the senator who presides over the Senate, the president pro tempore. By tradition, this is the longest-serving member of the majority party. That means the second person in line behind the Speaker is chosen by how long they have been around, not how ready they are. Same cause again. Nobody chose this as a criterion for running a country. It is just what happened to be sitting there.

After that, the cabinet. And the order is a gift to anyone who likes trivia. The cabinet is lined up by the date each department was created. State first, Treasury, Defense, Justice, and so on down, ending with the newest departments. The order of who steps up in a crisis is the order in which the buildings were founded. Same cause again. If you ever wanted to know who leads in a national emergency, the honest answer is whoever's department is oldest.

Now rule three starts to bite. Remember that every person in the line must be eligible. But cabinet members are chosen for other reasons, and a few of them, on any given day, may not meet the requirements. Some were born abroad. And there is a deeper question: are members of Congress even the kind of person the Constitution means when it says an officer can act as president? Lawyers argue about this, and I will fence it off right here: the arguments are real and unsettled, and I am not going to pretend that I know how a court would rule. The mess is a fact. The resolution is a guess.

I can feel you reaching for the safety net, so here it is. Designated survivor. Every year, when the whole government gathers in one place for a speech, one cabinet member is taken somewhere else, just in case. That sounds like a fix. And, to be fair, it is the closest thing to engineering in this whole system. But look at what it actually says. It says that nobody trusts the list. A single person, kept in a car for one night a year, is a patch on a design that has no plan for the other 364 nights.

So what would a good plan look like? Bracket it. The worst case is what you have: a list ordered by accident, with party risk, age risk and eligibility risk all stacked up. The ideal is short, chosen for readiness, with people spread across places and parties, and confirmed for this exact purpose. The real design is the compromise, because every person in that list holds some power, and nobody who holds power is eager to give it up for a better list.

So here are the rules again. One: the Constitution names only the vice president. Two: Congress added a list. Three: every link must be eligible. And rule zero, on top of the others: it was never designed as a system. Run it on the line from the Speaker to the cabinet and watch the same cause light up at every step.

Which brings us back to you, eleventh in line. You are looking at that list, and the list is looking at you, and neither of you is particularly sure of the other. The plan was not written for your sake. It was written for whoever was in the room. Good luck.
