## 2. Full narration script (about 1,840 words; runs a little short of the 2,200 needed at 165 wpm, so the real pace of ideas is likely higher or the real video has more tangents)

Hey, Vsauce. Michael here. How fast is dark? Not "how dark is fast", that one is easy, it is the colour of a cheetah at midnight. I mean how fast does dark itself move, from one place to another. Is it quick? Is it lazy? Is it just very, very late to every party and always shows up after the lights?

Here is where most people land first. They say dark is the absence of light, and an absence cannot go anywhere. You cannot ship a hole. Fair. Then somebody else says, wait, turn off a lamp and the room is dark everywhere basically instantly, so dark must travel at the speed of light, because light is the thing that stopped. Two answers, both sensible, and they cannot both be right. By the end of this video I think we can say which one was fooling us, and I suspect the surprise is that the trouble is in the question.

So let us start with a definition that is small enough to carry around. Dark is a place where very few photons are arriving. That is it. Not a substance, not a fluid. A place where the number of particles of light hitting your eye is low. I will tighten that later, but hold on to it, because we are about to push on it from three directions: a lamp, a flashlight, and a pair of scissors.

First, the lamp. Picture a room with a bulb in the middle, and you flick the switch. What happens? The bulb stops sending out new light. But the light that already left is still travelling, flying outward toward the walls. So for a tiny moment, a thin shell of light is still crossing the room, and everything inside the shell, the bulb side, is dark. If you stood near a wall, you would keep seeing light until that last shell reached you, and then you would be in the dark. So the front of dark, the boundary between light and no light, runs outward at the speed of light, because it is literally the back edge of the last light. Sounds like a point for team "dark moves at light speed". But notice what dark is doing there. It is not travelling. It is being left behind. Dark is what is behind the last photon, and the photon is what is moving.

Now let us make you the experimenter. You have a flashlight. Point it at a wall three metres away and give your wrist a quick flick, sweeping the beam from left to right. A small bright spot slides across the wall. Fine. Now back up to thirty metres, a big hall, same flick, same wrist. The spot moves ten times faster across the wall, because the same swing of angle now covers ten times as much wall. Notice what is true. The beam did not have to be pushed harder. The farther the wall, the faster the spot goes. Nothing about your wrist changed.

So take the wall to its limit. Aim at the Moon. It is about three hundred and eighty thousand kilometres away. Flick your wrist so the beam swings by a degree in a tenth of a second. That is a slow, lazy flick. The spot on the Moon covers about six and a half kilometres in that tenth of a second. That is sixty-five kilometres per second. Not fast, so far. But make the flick a bit more energetic, a full half turn in a second. Now the spot on the Moon is racing along at over a million kilometres per second. And light only goes about three hundred thousand kilometres per second. So the spot is moving more than three times the speed of light.

Do you see why I did not want to give you a number first? Because the moment the number is faster than light, your brain says either I did something wrong or physics broke. Neither is true, and the reason is the spot is not a thing.

Here is the rule, said the way I would first say it out loud, and then the way it would look written. The speed of the spot equals how quickly you swing the beam, times how far away the wall is. In letters, v equals omega times r. That is all it is. And look at it: there is no speed limit written anywhere in it. If r gets big enough, v gets as big as you like.

Now there is an obvious analogy, and I like it because you have probably been part of it. A stadium wave. People stand up, then sit down. Nobody runs around the stadium. Every person only moves up and down, a metre at most. But the wave, the pattern, travels all the way around the whole ring of the stadium, and it can go much faster than any one person could run. Because the wave is not a thing. It is a pattern of who is standing. No one needs to carry anything from seat to seat.

Same with the spot on the Moon. No photon in the spot is moving along the surface. Each photon is going straight out from your flashlight, at light speed, and landing. A photon lands here, then another lands a bit further along, then another. The spot is the wave. You are the stadium announcer deciding who stands up.

I want to run the same pattern in a couple more costumes so you can feel how general it is. A cloud passes in front of the sun. The shadow of the cloud races across a field, and with a high enough cloud and a strong enough wind, the edge of that shadow outruns the cloud itself. Why? Because the angle of the sun and the ground lets the shadow edge sweep. And hold your hand in front of a lamp and tilt it. The edge of the shadow on the wall slides much faster than your hand does. Again the shadow is not carried. It is created, again and again, on whatever the light happens to reach.

And one more, the scissors. Open a pair of long scissors, a really long pair. Imagine blades a light-year long, if you like. When you close them even slowly, the point where the blades cross, the cutting point, runs along the blade. If the blades are nearly parallel, that crossing point moves incredibly quickly. Faster than you pushing the handles, much faster. It could, in principle, be faster than light. But the crossing point is not a piece of metal. It is where two moving pieces of metal happen to meet. It is a pattern again, and nothing is going from one end of the scissors to the other except the story of two lines intersecting.

By now it feels like I am saying you can break the speed limit of the universe with a flashlight. So let me try to cash that in. Suppose you want to send a message to someone on the Moon faster than light. You sweep the beam, and the spot hits a sensor on the Moon, then a second sensor further along. Then you say, look, the signal got from the first sensor to the second faster than light. But the sensors did not talk to each other. The spot did not carry a message from the first to the second. Each one got its own separate hit from you, travelling at light speed from your hand. To make the second sensor know what the first got, someone has to send a signal between them, and that signal obeys the limit. The only way to make use of the spot is for the Moon crew to already know what you will do. That is not communication. That is a rehearsal.

That tells us something about how to cut the world. There is the speed of a pattern, which can be anything, even infinite, and there is the speed of a signal, a thing that carries cause and effect, which has a limit. Physicists have names for this in more advanced settings, phase velocity and group velocity, and the same distinction keeps showing up in waves of all kinds. But I want you to notice that we arrived at the name after we had the picture, not the other way round. The shape of the idea is: pattern speed has no limit, signal speed does.

Now back to dark. When I said the dark front in the room travels at light speed, the honest version is more careful. The last bit of light travels at light speed. Dark was the backdrop. If dark is just the place where the photons are not, then asking how fast dark moves is like asking how fast the gap in a line of ants moves. The gap can drift, and speed up, and slow down, and disappear, but the gap never carried an ant anywhere.

I should be honest about where this simple picture stops working. Real shadows are not perfectly crisp. There is a fuzzy edge, the penumbra, where the light is partly blocked, and it matters when you try to say exactly where dark begins. Also, I have been treating light as little balls. In the full picture, light is a field, and dark is not nothing; the field is still there, with its own fluctuations, even in a perfect vacuum. So the clean line I gave you, dark is a place with no photons, is the best thing I could tell you with a lamp, a flashlight and a pair of scissors, and not the last word.

So, what is the speed of dark? Here is my answer, and you can argue with it. It depends on what you decide counts as an object. If dark is a pattern, the edge of where light is not, then like every pattern it can move at nearly any speed you like, because it is not carrying anything. If dark is supposed to be a thing that sets off from one place and arrives at another, then there is no such thing to measure, and the question was like asking how fast the smell of nothing travels. Dark is not slow, and it is not fast. It is not the kind of thing that has a speed in that sense.

And here is the part I like. Take your own shadow, right now. You are a thing that blocks light, standing in front of a source, and what you see on the ground is a map of the shape of you, drawn by whatever light managed to get around you. That map can slide, stretch, shrink, and race away across the world, and none of it is a thing travelling. You are a creator of patterns, and your shadow is the proof that the best answers sometimes come from realising the question was a bit wrong.

And as always, thanks for watching.
