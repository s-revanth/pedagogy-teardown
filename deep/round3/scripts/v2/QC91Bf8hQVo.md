Can five coin flips tell you who's cheating? That's the question at the heart of this game, and it's a fair one to ask before you play. Here's the partial answer up front. Five flips can flag a suspicious blob, but they can't prove anything. Some innocent blobs will look guilty, and some cheats will slip through. The game is about how much of each you're willing to live with.

Here's the setting. In the nation of blobs, there's a popular pastime built on coin flips. Each blob brings its own coin, and players take turns flipping. Heads makes the flipper happy and the other player sad. That's the whole pastime.

Rumour has it that some blobs are using trick coins that land heads more often than half the time. That isn't fair, so the blob detectives want to catch the cheaters. Our job is to decide, from a handful of flips, who's using a fair coin and who isn't. Let's warm up with the obvious approach, which is to flip five times and look.

Each blob flips its coin five times. The results run from zero heads all the way up to five heads in a row. Some blobs got a messy mix, and some got a streak. Look at the one that came up five heads out of five. It looks suspicious, and that instinct is exactly what the game is built to test. Is a streak like that enough to accuse a blob?

Here's a useful number. With a fair coin, five heads in a row happens about one time in 32. So a fair blob will look like this roughly 3 percent of the time. That isn't zero, which means a five-heads rule will sometimes accuse an innocent blob.
Now the game itself. This is a game from Primer. You're the detective. Each blob comes with a coin you can't see, and you only see the flips. You judge the blob, and the game tells you how you did. Your job is to pick a rule, use it on the blobs, and watch what happens to your mistakes. Let's look at the two kinds of mistakes you can make.

A false accusation is when you call a fair blob a cheater. A missed cheat is when a trick coin gets away with it. You can't drive both to zero with five flips. Pushing one down pushes the other up. So the rule you choose is a trade-off, and the game makes you pick where to stand.
Let's set a rule and see what it does. Accuse a blob only if it gets five heads out of five. That's strict, so it won't often accuse fair blobs. But a trick coin that lands heads three quarters of the time will hit five in a row only about one time in four. So the strict rule misses most of the cheats. That's the trade-off in one example.

Here's the arithmetic, worked out. A coin that lands heads 75 percent of the time gives five heads in a row with probability 0.75 multiplied by itself five times. That's about 24 percent. So a cheater gets caught about a quarter of the time, and roughly three quarters of cheats slip through. The strict rule protects fair players, but as a detector it's weak.

Here's your call. A blob has come up five heads out of five. Do you accuse it, or let it go? Make your choice before the reveal, and think about the two ways you could be wrong. You could accuse an innocent blob, or you could let a cheat walk away. Pick one, and hold on to it.

Here's the reveal, with the same numbers. If you accused every five-heads blob, about 3 in 100 fair blobs would be wrongly accused, and about a quarter of the cheats would be caught. Whether that's a good decision depends on how much you care about wrongly accusing an innocent blob, compared with missing a cheat. Neither answer is free. Keep that trade-off in view, because it comes back.

Now try the tuning run yourself. Here are a thousand blobs, half using fair coins and half using trick coins. Set a rule that asks for more flips. Try ten flips, and accuse at seven or more heads. Watch the tally as you tune. You'll see that more flips sharpen the test, and the trade-off becomes easier to see with every change you make.

That's the lesson the tally keeps repeating. More flips give you a sharper test, but they cost more coins and more time, and nobody wants to be flipped forever. So there's always a budget. You can lower false accusations, raise the catch rate, or cut the number of flips, but with a fixed budget you can only fix two of the three.

Now the caveat, and it's the part people forget. Our calculations assumed the trick coins land heads 75 percent of the time. That number was a choice, not a fact. If the real cheats use a different bias, the rule will behave differently from what we predicted. A rule can look fair on paper and still miss badly, because the assumption about the trick coin was off. So write your assumptions down, and check them against what you see.

Write down your rule and your guess about the trick coin before you look at the tally. If the tally surprises you, the surprise is about your assumption, not the blobs.

So here's where the game lands. Five flips can flag a suspicious blob, but they can't convict it. Fair blobs will sometimes look guilty, and cheats will often look fine. A rule is a choice about which mistakes you're willing to make. Now it's your turn. Judge the next blob, and see whether your rule holds up.
