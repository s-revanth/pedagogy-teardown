Start with a small question. What is two cubed? You know this one. Two times two times two is eight. Three numbers are involved here: the base, which is two, the exponent, which is three, and the answer, which is eight. Keep those three corners in mind, because this whole video is about them.

Picture a triangle. Put the base in one corner, the exponent in another, and the result in the third. Then notice that once you know any two corners, the third one is pinned down. That is the entire idea, and the rest is working out which operation fills which gap.

Let's start with the case where you know the base and the exponent and want the result. That's the power itself. Two to the third is eight. Five squared is twenty-five. Nothing new there. Power takes you from the base and exponent to the answer.

Try one more: the base is ten and the exponent is four. Multiply ten by itself four times and you get ten thousand. The triangle gives you the result directly, and you never needed to count zeros one by one.

Now cover up the answer and look at the other two corners. Suppose you know the result is eight and the exponent is three, and you need the base. The number that, cubed, gives eight is two. That operation is the root. The cube root of eight is two. Root takes you from the answer and the exponent back to the base.

Now cover up the exponent. Suppose the base is two, the result is eight, and you need to know which power of two gives eight. This is a different kind of question. You are asking what exponent you need. That operation is the logarithm. The base-two logarithm of eight is three, because two to the third is eight.

So the triangle has three corners and three operations, and each operation fills the gap left by hiding one corner. Power fills in the result. Root fills in the base. Logarithm fills in the exponent. Three operations, one triangle, one fact read three different ways.

Each of those operations has an inverse partner. Power undoes a logarithm, and a logarithm undoes a power. A root undoes a power in the base. Knowing which partner undoes which is the same as knowing which corner is missing. That is why the triangle can be read in any direction.

Before we go further, here is a small guess. What exponent do you put on five to get one hundred twenty-five? Think about five times five, and then five times five times five. Make your guess before I answer.

Five to the third is one hundred twenty-five, because five times five is twenty-five, and twenty-five times five is one hundred twenty-five. So the exponent is three, and the logarithm of one hundred twenty-five in base five is three. Your reasoning was the same as the triangle's: two corners known, the third read off.

Try the same move with another pair. Base three and result twenty-seven: the exponent is three, because three cubed is twenty-seven. Base ten and result one hundred: the exponent is two. Each time you know two corners, the third is forced, and you never need a separate rule for each case.

Now let's push the triangle a little. Take the base nine and the result three. What exponent gives three? The answer is one half, because the square root of nine is three. This is where the triangle gets interesting. A root is a power with a fraction in the exponent. The corners stop being whole numbers, but the triangle still holds.

Here's another case. Suppose the exponent is four and the result is sixteen. What is the base? Two, because two to the fourth is sixteen. But negative two to the fourth is also sixteen, so there are two bases that work. That is a small surprise. It reminds you that roots can have more than one answer when the exponent is even. Notice what happened there. Two corners were known, and the triangle returned a base with two values. That is the triangle warning you that this corner is not one-to-one, which is a useful thing to remember whenever you solve for a root.

Mathematicians write these relationships in several ways, and the variety can make them look like unrelated rules. The letters change from book to book, but the three corners and their three gaps do not. If a formula is hard to remember, draw the triangle and ask which corner is missing.

It helps to say the triangle out loud. The base, raised to the exponent, gives the result. The root of the result, with the exponent as the index, gives the base. The logarithm says which exponent of the base gives the result. Three sentences, one picture, and each sentence answers a different question about the same three numbers.

This is not just a classroom exercise. In compound interest, you may know the starting amount, the rate, and the final amount, and need the time, which is an exponent. In sound, loudness is measured on a logarithmic scale, so a reading is an exponent in disguise. In each case you are walking the same triangle, with different labels on the corners.

A common slip is to read the triangle as a rule about multiplication. It is really about repeated multiplication with a fixed base, and the distinction matters as soon as the exponent is a fraction or a negative number.
