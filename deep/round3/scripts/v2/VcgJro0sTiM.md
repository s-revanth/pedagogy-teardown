<<<NARRATION
Every so often people ask me what else to watch if they liked these videos. So here is a list, and I'll try to be specific about why each one is on it. These are picks from my own viewing, not a ranking, and your taste will certainly differ. Some of you want proofs, some want puzzles, and some want history.

If you are new to this, here is a sensible order. Sample one channel from each category over a week or two, note which one made you want to keep watching, and then go deeper there. Nobody needs to watch everything, and the best channel for you is usually the one you return to without deciding to.

Let's start with channels that focus on visual proofs. These are the ones that teach you to see why a result is true, not just that it is true. If you liked the moments in these videos where a picture makes a formula feel inevitable, this is the category to explore. The payoff is a feeling of looking at a famous result and finally understanding why it had to be that way.

Next, the history side. Some channels spend most of their time on how a concept was discovered, who was wrong about it, and what the argument looked like before anyone polished it. Those are great if you care about the human side of mathematics. The stories are often more surprising than any formula, and they make the ideas easier to remember, because you remember who got stuck and why.

A typical episode might start with a result everyone learns in school, then trace it back to the puzzle that produced it. You see why the formula looks the way it does, and that is much harder to forget than the formula itself. Those episodes also teach you how to ask questions, which is a skill that transfers far better than any single topic.

Then there are the puzzle channels. These pose a problem, give you a few minutes to think, and then show a solution, sometimes with several approaches. The best of them make you do the work. If you have a habit of pausing a video to try a question before the answer appears, this is the kind of channel that rewards it. It is a very good way to practise, because the problems are chosen to make you think about which tool to reach for.

Pause before the solution appears. Even a few minutes of your own attempt makes the reveal more memorable, because you have a version of the answer to compare against, and the comparison is where most of the learning happens.

After that, the programming side. Some creators build their videos by writing code that draws the mathematics on screen. That makes every picture a small experiment. You can watch a parameter change, see the shape respond, and see the mechanism behind it. For anyone who wants to try the ideas themselves, these channels often share code, which turns watching into doing.

Next, a category I'd put in its own corner: channels that explain the bigger picture of numbers. These go wide rather than deep. They cover the surprising places numbers show up, from physics to probability to the structure of primes, and they are a nice way to find the next topic you want to go deep on. Think of them as a map of the territory rather than a guided tour of one road.

And if you want something gentler, there are channels for people returning to mathematics after a long gap. They slow down, define their terms, and assume less background. If the last time you did mathematics was in school and it didn't go well, those are a kind place to start again. You don't need to finish a whole course to get something out of them.

I want to flag a limitation. I'm picking these from what I watch, and my list is shaped by my own interests. It leaves out a lot of excellent work, including channels in other languages and channels focused on applied statistics, which I know much less well. So treat this as a starting point. If a channel you love isn't here, that means the list is incomplete, not that the channel is worse.

There is also a practical tip. Many of these channels point to other creators in their descriptions, comments and pinned posts, so once you find one you like, follow those links. Creators who explain well tend to point you to other creators who explain well, which makes the search easier than it sounds.

One trait I'd look for in any of them is openness about mistakes. Most of the good math channels I know are run by people who are candid about what they got wrong along the way. A creator who shows a first attempt, or admits that a claim needed refining, is teaching you how to think, not just what to think. That, more than any particular topic, is what I'd recommend choosing by.

A second trait is consistency. A channel you can come back to every few weeks will do more for you than one brilliant video you never see again. Look for a steady rhythm, and for series you can follow from start to finish, because a long argument rewards patience. Finally, pay attention to how a channel handles disagreement with experts or with its own past claims. The best ones explain what they changed their mind about, and why. That habit alone makes a channel worth following, even when its topics are far from yours.

So that's the list. If you have a favourite I haven't mentioned, I'd genuinely like to know what it is, and what made you come back to it. For me, the channel I return to most is the one that taught me to stop asking whether a result is true and start asking why it had to be true. That question is where I started, and it's still where I like to be.
