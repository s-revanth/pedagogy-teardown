## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Quick word from our sponsor before we go back to the sea. It's fifteen seconds, and I promise the ships will still be sinking when we return.

Here's a puzzle. Rome is a land power. It has armies, roads and very good infantry, and it has almost no navy to speak of. Then it decides to fight Carthage, the biggest naval power in the western Mediterranean. Rome's answer is to build a fleet. Then it loses the fleet. Then it builds another, and loses that one too, mostly to weather. And then it keeps building. Why would anyone keep building ships that keep sinking? That's the question for this half of the war, and it's a good one. What would you do after losing two fleets? Would you keep going?

Let's start with the map, and I'll be honest, maps are where I always get lost. Sicily sits in the middle, and whoever controls the island controls the grain, the trade and the sea lanes around it. Rome has taken much of the land. Carthage holds the coast and its big fortified harbours. So the war becomes a siege war on land and a sea war at the edges. You'd be surprised how many small towns end up on the front line, changing hands like a bad coin.

Now the cast, because it gets busy. Rome's consuls change every year, and they need a win to look good back home. Carthage's admirals include Adherbal, who commands at the big sea battles. Hamilcar Barca is the general who refuses to surrender a single mountain. Hiero is the Greek ally who sides with whoever looks like winning. Lutatius Catulus will end the war. And the mercenaries are owed money that nobody has. That's more than six names, so I'm going to keep one thread: the ships. Everything else in this half comes back to them.

At Drepana, in 249 BCE, the Roman navy takes a beating. By most accounts the consul in charge is reckless, and the Carthaginian fleet catches him off guard. Rome loses most of its ships in the battle. Then a storm takes what's left. Ships on the bottom of the sea are likely the simplest kind of loss, because nobody gets a second chance to fix a sinking.

Rome lost the fleet, built a new one, and sent it back out.

The cost of a sinking is bigger than it looks, and we should be clear about that. You lose a ship, a crew, and a year's worth of timber and money all at once. And for a state that has never built a serious navy before, that's not a small hit. It's the kind of loss that makes you ask whether the whole project is worth it.

Carthage isn't finished either. Who gives up now? Not Hamilcar. Hamilcar Barca digs in on Mount Eryx, near the western end of Sicily, and holds on for years. He raids, he harasses, and he refuses to come down and fight a decisive battle on Rome's terms. Would you hold a mountain for several years with an army that gets fed by a sea you can't control? That's the problem he faces too, and he solves it by being very stubborn.

Rome's answer, probably, was to put the harbours under pressure and grind the towns down. Blockades, sieges and long, dull months of waiting. Both sides run short of things. Both sides keep promising that the other side is about to crack. And the mountain stays exactly where it is.

So, a quick guess. Rome has lost two fleets. Now the Senate has to decide whether to try a third, paid for by a state that is already bleeding money, or to give up the sea altogether. Pause and make your call. Got it? Good. They build. Not because it was smart, but because there was nowhere else to go, and the other option was to let Carthage own the water.

Ancient sources say Rome raised a new fleet in a surprisingly short time. How do you build that many ships in months? Who pays? Reportedly, reportedly with help from wealthy citizens who paid for ships. The exact numbers are hard to trust, so treat them as rough estimates. What's clear is the message. Rome wasn't going to let the sea go without a fight, and you can admire that or be worried by it, depending on which side of the water you live on.

A quick approximation caveat. Ship numbers from ancient sources are rough. Some totals may be exaggerated, some may be counted differently, and the cost figures are estimates. Treat the scale as real and the exact tallies as careful guesses.

Then comes the decisive battle, at the Aegates Islands, off the western coast of Sicily. Lutatius Catulus leads the new Roman fleet. The ships are better built, the crews are better trained, and the Carthaginian supply fleet is coming in heavy and slow. The Romans catch it. In one battle the sea war is decided, and Carthage has no fleet left to fight with. You can picture the mood in Carthage that evening, and it is not good.

Rome lost the fleet, built a new one, and sent it back out. It worked this time.

Carthage asks for terms, and it gets them, with a bill attached. It leaves Sicily. It pays a large indemnity over a number of years. And it accepts Roman terms. Peace in 241 BCE. I'd say the peace is not really the end of the story, because the peace has a bill, and somebody has to pay it.

The comfortable belief is that the sea war was simply Roman discipline. It's a tidy story about organisation and character, and I think a lot of people like it. I'd say the flip is the more interesting half. The war was won by new ships, repeated losses, and a very stubborn Senate. Discipline helped, but it was not the whole recipe.

Carthage can't pay its own mercenaries. Why would soldiers keep fighting without pay? Good question. Thousands of soldiers who fought for Carthage are owed back pay, and they revolt. This is the Mercenary War, and we have seen versions of that story before. It's brutal on both sides. Hamilcar Barca comes back to crush the revolt. You know the feeling: the people who got you the victory are now the people you have to beat.

While Carthage is busy with its own soldiers, Rome moves in. In 238 BCE it takes Sardinia and Corsica. Carthage protests, but it's in no position to do much. Let's be direct: opportunism is the oldest move in the book, and I'd say it's the most Roman thing in the whole war. A rival that can't defend everything loses the islands it can't reach.

A few decades later, Sicily becomes Rome's first province. Rome is now a sea power, a land power, and a state that thinks in provinces, taxes and the long game. Think about what that costs, and I'll admit it's hard to get my head around: fleets lost, towns starved, mercenaries butchered, and years of a war nobody could price in advance.

So what happened to our puzzle? A land power with almost no navy fought the sea power, lost two fleets to bad luck and bad decisions, and then won at sea. The sea never agreed to the deal, and Rome kept building anyway. A quick caveat on the figures, and then I'll let the ships speak for themselves. Cost and ship numbers are estimates, and the shape of the story is the solid part.

Let me end with a little skit, because the Senate deserves one. A senator asks the admiral what the new ships cost. The admiral answers in timber, bronze and sailors. The senator asks who is paying. The answer, after a long pause, is a tax. Rome lost the fleet, built a new one, and sent it back out. The sea never agreed to the deal, but Rome kept building.
