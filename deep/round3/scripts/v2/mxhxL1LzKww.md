Michael here. How much of the Earth can you see at once? I know you want to answer right away, so don't just yet. Take a guess, because we are going to test it, and the answer is more interesting than most people expect.

I'll start small. Grab a beach ball, or any ball, and pretend it is the Earth. The ball is smaller than the planet, but the shape is the same, and that is all we need for now. Set it on a table and imagine you are standing on its surface.

So what does it mean to see something? For this video, seeing means looking along a straight line. Light travels in straight lines, so the question becomes: which points on the ball can your eye connect to with a straight line that does not pass through the ball itself? Those lines just graze the surface. The edge where they touch is called the horizon.

Stand on a beach with your eyes about 1.8 metres above the sand, and look out over the ocean. The horizon is 4.8 kilometres away. Beyond that, the water keeps curving away from your line of sight, so you cannot see any farther. I used to picture the horizon as a flat line that goes on forever. It is really a circle, and it is closer than you might think.

Think of the horizon as a tangent. A tangent line touches a curve at exactly one point and runs straight on past it. Your sight line is a tangent to the water, and every point it touches is part of your horizon. Move higher and the tangent point moves farther away. That is the whole idea, and everything else in this video follows from it.

Here is a quick check you can do at the shore. Watch a ship sail away. Its hull disappears first, and the masts stay visible last. That happens because the curve of the water hides the lower part of the ship before it hides the top. It is one of the oldest pieces of evidence that the surface is curved.

Now let's climb. The highest point on Earth is the summit of Mount Everest, about 8.85 kilometres above sea level. In 1953, Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay reached that summit. Many people assume that from a mountain top you can see the whole globe. You cannot, and the numbers push back.

From the summit, the horizon is 336 kilometres away. That is a huge distance, but I find it is still a tiny fraction of the planet's surface. A commercial jet cruises at about 10 kilometres, and from there the horizon is 357 kilometres away. Climbing from the beach to the summit, or up to a jet, pushes the horizon out, but only slowly.

Now let's go much higher. The International Space Station orbits at roughly 400 kilometres, and its crew can see far more than any mountaineer. Their horizon is about 2,300 kilometres away. That is, to me, a large slice of a continent, yet it is still a small part of the planet.

Those numbers show the horizon grows, but surprisingly slowly. Doubling your height does not double your view. To see why, we need a formula, and this is the first time we need symbols. Let's call Earth's radius R. Let's call your height above the surface h. And let's call the distance to the horizon d. For the beach, R is about 6,371 kilometres, and h is 0.0018 kilometres.

The formula is d equals the square root of two times R times h. Plug in the beach numbers and you get 4.8 kilometres. Plug in the summit's height and you get 336 kilometres. I find the square root the surprising part, and it is the reason. Height grows, but distance grows only as the square root of height, so every extra step up buys less and less.

Now here is the real question. Distance to the horizon is one thing. How much of the planet can you see? That is a question about area. Imagine the part of the ball you can see from above. It is a cap, like the top of an egg, bounded by the horizon circle. The bigger the cap, the more of the planet you see.

The area of that cap can be written as a fraction of the whole surface. The fraction is h divided by two times the quantity R plus h. Read it aloud: h over two times R plus h. That is the share of the surface you can see from height h.

Two cautions before we test it. This formula assumes a smooth ball. Hills and mountains add a little, and the atmosphere bends light, which lifts the horizon out by a few percent. I should say that those corrections matter for exact surveying, but they do not change the big picture.

Let's test it. On the ground, h is tiny, so the fraction is tiny. On the beach you see far below one percent of the planet. At the summit it is under one tenth of one percent. At the space station, h is about 400 kilometres, and the fraction is about 3 percent.

Most people, when they first hear the station number, are surprised by how small it is. The sphere looks enormous from a plane window, so the view must be enormous too. But the view is always a small cap on the near side of the horizon. The cap grows as you climb, but it climbs slowly.

Now let's go to the Moon, about 384,000 kilometres away. Before I plug that height in, here is where I want you to pause. Stop the video and guess what share of the Earth the Moon can show you at once. Write down a number, then keep watching.

It is just under half. How did you do? Most people guess a tenth or a quarter, which is a very natural mistake. Plug the Moon's height into the same fraction and you get almost exactly half. Even if you could climb infinitely high, the fraction would approach one half and never pass it. That is the ceiling.

That is the wall. No matter how far you go from a single spot, you cannot see more than half of the planet at once, because the other half is always hidden below the horizon from where you stand. The back of the Earth is always somewhere else.

In December 1968, Apollo 8 took the famous picture of the Earth rising over the edge of the Moon. From that distance a thumb held at arm's length can cover the whole planet, which is roughly two degrees of sky. Then, in 1972, Apollo 17 captured a view of the planet from a different angle. Neither photo showed everything at once.

So how do we ever see all of it? We see it over time. A satellite in a low orbit passes over one strip of ground, then the next strip, then the next. The planet turns beneath it. Each pass covers a slice. After enough passes, the whole surface has been seen, even though no single moment ever showed all of it.

People were doing this long before satellites. About 2,200 years ago, the Greek scholar Eratosthenes compared the shadows cast by the sun at two cities in Egypt, Syene and Alexandria, at the same moment. From that difference he estimated the size of the whole planet. He never saw the sphere at once. He saw two shadows, and the curve of the Earth did the rest.

Let's go back to the ball on my table. I would paint its surface in stripes and spin it slowly. Each stripe comes into view, slips over the edge, and a new one appears. You can see the whole ball by rotating it, but no single angle shows you more than half. Hold the ball and check that for yourself, because I think seeing it in your hands is worth more than any sentence I can say.

Keep one more thing in mind. A flat map is not a sphere. Any map that shows the whole planet on one sheet has to stretch something, so the far side is always distorted or hidden. That is another reason a single picture can mislead you about how much you can see at once.

So how much of the Earth can you see at once? From one place, never more than half. From the ground, a tiny fraction. From the space station, about 3 percent. From the Moon, just under half. And over time, all of it.

Given enough time, the answer to how much you can see at once is still the same: less than half, every time. But give it a day, and the answer is all of it.
