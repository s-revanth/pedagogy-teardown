<<<NARRATION
Michael here, and welcome to another IMG. Here is the first picture. It looks like a taco. The shell is curved, the filling is bright, and the whole thing sits on a plate. It is made of LEGO bricks, and it was built by hand. Why does it look like food? Take a second look before reading on, because the reason your brain says taco is more interesting than the taco itself.

Your brain is a pattern hunter. It sees a curve, a colour and a flat base, and it fills in the rest with the most likely object. The builder knew that. They chose the shape and the colours so the bricks did the rest. It is a clever use of expectation, and you walked straight into it.

Shall we try another? Here is a picture of an electrical outlet on a wall. Two small slots, and a round hole below them. Now look again. Many people see a face, complete with eyes and a mouth. The outlet did not change. Your brain simply decided that a face was the most useful guess. Psychologists call this pareidolia, and it is why faces turn up in clouds, toast and the fronts of cars.

There is a deeper reason this happens. Faces matter to us, so the brain is tuned to find them even in noise. Missing a real face could be dangerous, while a false alarm costs only a moment of confusion. Over time, the false alarm wins. I think that trade-off is one of the most fascinating things about how we see.

Next is a photo that looks like it was taken on a set. Two people appear to stand side by side, and one looks much bigger than the other. Nothing is wrong with the camera. The photographer placed the people at very different distances from the lens and lined them up so that the far one looks tiny. This is forced perspective, and it works from one camera position only.

That last part is the honest caveat. Images look convincing from one spot, and that is the whole trick. Step a few paces to the side and the illusion collapses. Anything that depends on a single viewpoint is only true from that viewpoint, and the picture hides the rest.

Here is a satellite view of a desert. Dunes, shadows, and a pattern of ridges that looks like a painting, with swirls and bands of colour. Some of the shapes are so clean that you would swear someone painted them. In fact, the wind carved them over many years. I would say the image is a real measurement of a real surface. The painting comparison is just a shortcut for how orderly nature can look from far away.

Now back to the taco. Look at it again with what we have learned. Your brain filled in the shape, the colour and the bricks. The forced perspective told you one thing, and the satellite told you another. It all comes down to the same trick. We see the most likely thing, not always the thing that is there.

So what do these pictures have in common? Every picture is a choice about where to stand, what to include and what to leave out. The taco is a good example. The builder chose the angle and the lighting. Once you know that, you start seeing the choices everywhere, and the images get more interesting, not less.

Want to try one last thing? Take a photo of your own desk and imagine someone who had never seen a desk looking at it. The most ordinary things look strange when we stop filling in what we already know. Try it on something you see every day.

Thanks for watching, and I will see you in the next IMG.
