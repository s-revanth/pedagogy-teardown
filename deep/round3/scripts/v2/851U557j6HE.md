<<<NARRATION
Here's an integral that looks completely ordinary. Take the function sine of x divided by x. It's a wave that starts at one in the middle and dies down as x grows. Now ask how much area lies under it, from zero out to infinity. Make a guess, and then see whether you're surprised.

The area is pi over two. Not approximately, exactly. That alone is a nice fact, and it is the sort of thing you're happy to accept and move on from.

Now here is where it gets strange. Multiply that function by a second wave, sine of x divided by x over three, which is the same shape stretched out by a factor of three. Integrate the product. The area is still pi over two. Add a third factor, stretched by five. Still pi over two. Add a fourth, stretched by seven. Still pi over two.

By now you might suspect that this is some law of nature, and you would not be wrong about the pattern. It holds for a long run of these factors. At every step, the answer you'd naively expect is still pi over two, and the numbers on the screen agree with it.

So naturally you start to suspect a bug. Maybe the integration is being done wrong. Maybe the pattern is a coincidence that dies out soon. That is exactly the reaction that researchers reported when they first saw this. They thought the result must be a mistake.

Let's test that. A coincidence would break quickly. This pattern does not. You can add factors and watch the value stay at pi over two, right down to every decimal place you can resolve on the screen. A bug would have shown up by now. But a list of matching digits does not explain anything, so let's find the reason.

It helps to be precise about what a coincidence would look like. A coincidence would fail early, when the factors are few and simple, because that is where a random agreement has the least support. Instead the agreement survives every factor you try. Here is a habit I'd recommend when a result looks too clean: check whether the agreement fails where a mistake would fail. A bug in the integration would usually show up in the first few factors, not in the sixth.

Here is a shift in perspective that I think is the real key. Every one of these wave functions is the Fourier transform of a simple box. A box is a flat block of height one over some interval. If you take the Fourier transform of that box, you get a sine over x shape. So each sine-over-x factor is secretly a box, seen through the lens of frequency.

Now go back to the integral. Integrating a product of these waves over all x is the same as taking a convolution of boxes and evaluating it at zero. I don't want to hide behind jargon, so here it is in plain words. Each box is a block with a certain width. When you combine the boxes by sliding one over another and adding up the overlaps, the result is a new shape. The integral is the height of that combined shape at a single point, the point zero.

Here's why that matters. Each factor corresponds to a box with a width, and the widths are one over one, one over three, one over five, and so on. Stack those boxes together. As long as the total width is small enough, the combined shape is perfectly flat across the middle. A flat shape has a height at zero that is exactly what you'd expect, and the integral comes out to pi over two.

So the pattern is not a coincidence. It is a consequence of geometry. For the boxes to produce a flat top at the centre, the widths must add up to less than a particular threshold. Within that threshold, every extra factor leaves the answer alone, because the flat region is still flat.

Before I show you where it breaks, make a guess, since I think you can predict what happens at the edge. If the total width crosses the threshold, what does the combined shape do at the centre? Does it stay flat, bend a little, or fall off a cliff? Commit to one of those.

The answer is that it bends a little. Once the total width passes the threshold, the top of the combined shape stops being perfectly flat near the centre. It starts to dip. That dip is tiny. The integral moves away from pi over two by an amount so small that you would never see it with a normal calculation, a gap far smaller than one part in a billion.

That is the bug that isn't a bug. The pattern holds for the first seven factors, and then the eighth factor pushes the total width just past the threshold, and the answer changes by a sliver. The surprise is not that the pattern breaks. The surprise is that it holds for so long.

Let me say exactly what I'm claiming. The eighth factor does not make the answer dramatically different. It makes the answer different by a margin you could only see with extreme care. So the honest statement is an approximation: the answer is pi over two for a long stretch of factors, and then it is pi over two minus, or plus, an amount that is for practical purposes invisible. Think of the picture as the explanation for why the agreement is so good, not as a promise that it is perfect forever.

So was it a bug? No. It was a boundary, the place where a clean geometric fact stops being exactly true. The researchers were right to be suspicious, and right to dig in. What they found was not a mistake in the calculation. It was a mistake in the expectation that a pattern which holds this well must hold forever. Integrals, like beliefs, can keep their shape for a long time, and still reveal the edge when you push them far enough.
