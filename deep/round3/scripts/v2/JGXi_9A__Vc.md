## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Right now you are standing on a surface that is roughly as thin, compared with its size, as the skin of an apple. That sounds like an exaggeration, but it is close to the truth. Let's use an apple as a model to see why, and then zoom in layer by layer until the whole planet makes sense.

Cut an apple in half, and you see three simple parts. There is a thin skin on the outside, a thick flesh inside, and a core at the centre. Earth has the same basic design, with the skin as the crust, the flesh as the mantle, and the core at the centre. The apple is a model, though, and the real planet is far larger and far more complicated than a piece of fruit.

Earth's average radius is about 6,371 kilometres. That is a rocky ball, and everything we walk on, build on and dig into is a thin layer on its outside. Most of the planet is hidden from view, which is why the layers had to be worked out by careful measurement, one piece at a time.

Why does this matter to us? Because nearly everything we use comes from that thin outer shell. Iron, copper, oil, gas and the water in our taps all sit in or near the crust, so the crust is where human history actually happens. If you have ever dug a garden bed, you have touched the top few centimetres of that shell, which is where plant roots live.

The crust is the thin skin we live on. Depending on where you stand, it is roughly 5 to 70 kilometres thick. Under the oceans it is thinner, often around 7 kilometres, while under the continents it averages several times that. Its thickness is not uniform, and that unevenness is the first reason the apple is only a model. If you have ever flown over a coastline, you have seen how land ends and sea floor begins.

We can make the depth of the crust vivid with four named depths. The tallest mountain, Everest, rises about 8.8 kilometres above sea level. The deepest point of the ocean, in the Mariana Trench, sits about 11 kilometres down. The deepest hole ever drilled reaches about 12 kilometres, a record that has stood for decades. The thickest crust lies beneath large mountain ranges, reaching roughly 70 kilometres.

Here is a guess to make before we go on. Compared with the radius of the whole planet, how much of the Earth do you think the crust takes up? Pause and pick a fraction, a few percent, or something much smaller.

The answer is less than one percent of the radius. Most of the planet lies beneath that skin, so the crust is a very small fraction of the whole. Compared with the apple, the crust is an even thinner shell than most people expect, and that is the lesson the apple is trying to teach. Why does it matter that the shell is so thin? We will see the answer when we reach the mantle.

Under the crust sits the mantle, about 2,900 kilometres of rock. It is solid, but over millions of years it flows very slowly, like a thick liquid. Heat from deep inside drives this slow motion, and it is one of the forces that moves the continents across the surface.

Below the mantle is the outer core. It is liquid iron and nickel, roughly 2,200 kilometres thick, and it is churning all the time. That motion of molten metal creates Earth's magnetic field, which stretches far out into space.

We feel the benefit of that field more than we notice it. It deflects charged particles streaming from the Sun, and where some of them spiral down near the poles, they light up the sky as auroras. Without the field, the atmosphere would be exposed to far more of that stream. If you have ever seen an aurora, you have seen that field at work.

At the very centre sits the inner core. It is solid iron and nickel, about 1,220 kilometres across, and it is held solid by the enormous pressure of everything above it. Its temperature is estimated at more than 5,000 degrees, roughly as hot as the surface of the Sun, although it is buried far beneath our feet. If you could somehow stand near it, you would feel heat that no kitchen could match.

All of these layers are inferred, not seen. Scientists measure how seismic waves from earthquakes bend and slow as they pass through the planet, and they work backwards from those measurements. The figures in this video are rounded and approximate, and nobody has drilled far enough to touch the deep interior directly.

Even the deepest drilling has barely scratched the surface. Engineers can reach a few kilometres with good equipment, and the record of about 12 kilometres is still the deepest hole anyone has made. That is about a fifth of one percent of the way to the centre.

Now let's look at the surface systems. The atmosphere is a thin layer of gas, mostly nitrogen, with about a fifth of it oxygen. Most of that air lies close to the ground, which is why mountain climbers feel the thinning air so quickly. If you have ever climbed a high peak, you have noticed each breath feeling thinner.

The oceans cover most of the surface, and their average depth is several kilometres. Water shapes the climate, carries heat around the globe, and supports most of the life we know. Without it, the surface would look very different and far less welcoming.

The crust itself is not still. Its outer shell is broken into plates, and those plates move a few centimetres a year, about as fast as fingernails grow. Over tens of millions of years, that slow motion builds mountain ranges, opens oceans and reshapes the continents. Why do the plates move at all? Mostly because the mantle below them is slowly convecting, which is the same heat-driven flow we saw earlier.

Sunlight takes about eight minutes to reach us from the Sun, so the light on your face is already old. The same layered design, with a crust, a mantle and a core, shows up on other rocky worlds in our solar system, though the sizes and temperatures differ. Earth is one example of a common pattern, not a unique case.

Do we really understand the core? Not fully. Our best models predict its behaviour well, but the details of how the solid inner core grows over time are still debated, and that is an honest place to stop the science.

So the next time you stand on solid ground, remember the apple. The skin is thinner than it looks, and the planet beneath it is far larger than the surface suggests.
