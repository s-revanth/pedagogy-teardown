## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Earth is a prison in one specific sense, and the bars are physics rather than walls. The exception is that the door is open to anyone with enough energy. That single fact shapes whether we can leave, and how. Let's look at the bars one at a time.

Think of a prison locked by physics rather than by a guard. There is no one you can bribe, and no one to slip past. The only key is energy, and energy is something we have been learning to buy more cheaply.

Here is the picture. Gravity holds everything to the planet, including the air you breathe. Food, water and warmth all depend on the thin layer of conditions near the surface. Those four things are the bars of the cell, and each one has to be dealt with before anyone can leave for good.

Let's start with the first bar, gravity. To leave the planet, a rocket needs to reach a speed of about 11.2 kilometres per second. That is the escape velocity, the speed at which you would keep going and never fall back. It is an enormous speed, and it is the reason rockets are so big. The physics is simple, but the engineering is brutal.

Rung one is low orbit, roughly 400 kilometres up. We have been reaching it for decades, and a station there circles the planet every 90 minutes. That means the astronauts on board see a sunrise about every hour and a half. From there you are not free, because you are still falling around the Earth, but you are no longer stuck on the ground.

Rung two is the Moon, about 384,400 kilometres away. Our spacecraft have reached it, and the trip takes roughly three days. That is the first real step beyond the cell, and it is much easier than most people expect. Why not stop there? The Moon has no air and no water we can use, so it is a waypoint, not a home.

Now the misconception. Most people assume the rocket is the hardest part of leaving. It is not. The rocket is a solved problem, and we know how to build one, but staying alive is the harder one. Air, food, water, radiation and the slow loss of strength all push back against any long journey, and those are the bars that matter most for a settlement. Each of those bars has a known fix on Earth, but none of them is cheap or simple beyond the planet.

Pause here and make a guess. Would you take a one-way ticket to another world, knowing you could not come home? Say yes or no, and keep that answer in mind for what comes next.

The answer depends on the destination, and the best candidate for a settlement is Mars. It is roughly 55 million kilometres away at its closest, so a trip takes months rather than days, and the travel itself is hard on the body. Its gravity is about 38 percent of Earth's, which helps the body in some ways, but it is still far from home. Mars has thin air that is mostly carbon dioxide, almost no magnetic shield, and surface radiation that would be dangerous over long stays. Settling there is possible in principle, but it is far from easy.

Each candidate is a guess, and we should label it as one. Orbital habitats could be built with shielding and spin for gravity, but nobody has built one at scale. Mars could support a small settlement, but the long-term problem of food and air is unsolved. Travel to another star is the most ambitious option, and no one can say yet whether it can be done at all. We can test some of these ideas on the Moon first.

Earth's magnetic field and its atmosphere are the reason the surface is mostly shielded from solar and cosmic radiation. Remove them, and every long stay becomes a risk you have to shield against. We have learned to respect that shield, because every instrument we have sent beyond it has had to be hardened against radiation. That is why a settlement must grow its own food, recycle its own water and reuse its own air, because every kilogram carried from Earth is expensive.

Rung three is Mars, and rung four is the nearest star system, about 4.2 light-years away. Even the fastest probe we have sent beyond the solar system has taken decades to get far from the Sun. At that speed, a trip to the nearest star would take tens of thousands of years, far longer than any human life. So rung four is less a step than a wall, and it marks the edge of the cell with today's technology.

History offers a useful comparison. Crossing the Atlantic once took weeks by sail, then steam cut it to days, and aircraft brought it down to hours. Each leap came from a new solution to an old bar. Nobody crossed the ocean by wishing, and nobody will leave the planet that way either.

The bars do not disappear when you reach a new place. Each new home has its own bars, and each one has to be solved before the next rung makes sense. That is why the prison is better described as a system than as a single barrier.

Let's go back to the first three rungs, because they make a test. The Moon takes about three days, Mars takes months, and the nearest star takes lifetimes. Which of those steps can we realistically repeat? The Moon is already reachable, Mars is a serious plan, and the star is still a dream. That is the test, and it tells us where the door is.

Whether any of this happens in your lifetime depends less on the rocket than on the bars we are willing to solve. The energy problem is slowly getting cheaper, but the air, food and radiation problems are the ones that will decide the escape.

So the prison is real, but only in the physics sense. The bars are gravity, air, food and distance, and each one has to be solved before the door opens. The door is still open, and what is missing is the energy and the patience to use it.
