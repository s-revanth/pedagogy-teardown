## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Quick word from our sponsor before the war. It's fifteen seconds, it's a good one, and I promise it's the only thing in this video that goes according to plan.

It's the early 1930s, and Australia is in a depression. Wheat prices have collapsed, banks are nervous, and people are queueing for work. The government has an uncomfortable problem, and it has a plan that sounds nice on paper. Give land to the soldiers who came home from the last war, and let them become farmers. What could go wrong?

Here's some context. After the First World War, the government offered returned soldiers blocks of land, which sounded like a lovely reward. Many of those blocks were in marginal country, where it's very hard to grow anything without water, roads and money. You can probably see the trap already. The men got land, but the land was barely worth farming, and the price for what they could grow had just fallen off a cliff.

A lot of those blocks ended up in the Campion district of Western Australia. Picture the soldier arriving with a sack, a debt and a farm that's half scrub. Then the Depression takes his crops and his prices, and suddenly the place looks very different. I'd argue the soldiers were promised a future, and the promise was far too cheap to keep. I think that's the detail people miss when they hear the phrase Emu War. It isn't a joke about birds. It's a story about a promise made on paper and then not backed up with water, roads or money. Would you have taken that deal in 1920?

And then the birds show up. The emu. A large, flightless bird, about as tall as a person, and in the early 1930s there were reportedly around twenty thousand of them in the area. Emus are not stupid. They are fast, they are tough, and they travel in flocks that can wander through fences like the fences are a rumour. Reports put their running speed at around fifty kilometres an hour, which is not a number you want to hear when you're trying to keep a fence up.

Now, the damage. In the breeding season the birds come in, eat the crops, trample the paddocks and wreck the fences that keep the farmers' stock where it belongs. So what's a farmer supposed to do? You ask for help, obviously. The farmers went to the government. The government went to the army. And a major named Meredith turned up with a pair of Lewis machine guns, a few soldiers and a plan.

Think about that for a second. A machine gun against a bird that runs at fifty kilometres an hour. Would you bet on the gun? A lot of people in government were pretty sure they would. I'd have bet on the birds, honestly, but I'm not the one who wrote the budget.

Okay, quick guess before we see what happens. How many birds do you think two machine guns could kill in a campaign? Pause and commit to a number. Got it? Good, because nobody agrees on the answer, and that's the first lesson of this whole war. You could have guessed a few hundred. You could have guessed three. Let's not pretend anyone got this one right.

The first attack, late in 1932. The guns open up. The flock breaks into smaller groups and spreads across the paddock. Then the groups regroup and walk away, and then they keep going. Soldiers chase them, guns jam, and the ammunition goes faster than anyone planned. The birds had not received the memo, and they weren't going to.

Here's the part that matters. The birds were tough, they were hard to pin down, and they kept coming back. The army's own accounts describe a lot of rounds fired and a far smaller number of birds brought down. How many? Sources disagree. Some have quoted dozens, others hundreds, and the exact count is still argued over. Keep that in mind, because every number you hear from this campaign deserves a little suspicion.

Week by week, the campaign looks less like a military operation and more like a long argument with feathers. The birds go around the guns. The guns chase the birds. The farmers still have fences broken, and the newspapers start having a field day. The jokes write themselves. I'll admit I laughed at the headlines too, and then I thought about the farmers and stopped laughing. Let's be clear about the scale: a soldier with a rifle against a bird is a fair fight only in the cartoon version. The birds had not received the memo, and they weren't going to.

Why did it fail? Partly because machine guns are great against lines of people and pretty bad against a moving flock across open country. Partly because the whole idea was a publicity move dressed up as a plan. A government that wants to look busy can send soldiers to almost anything, even when the soldiers can't win. Nobody in Canberra was expecting a bird to read the operation orders.

I want to be clear about the myth, because it's a very common one. The comfortable belief goes like this: a few trained soldiers with proper weapons will clear a pest problem in an afternoon. Then the emus show up, and that belief gets quietly retired. Machine guns don't solve an ecological problem, and a problem that moves around does not stay solved.

Withdrawal is the honest end of the story. The army pulls out within weeks. The birds are still there. The farmers are still there, with broken fences and bad prices. The emus, to put it bluntly, won.

A quick caveat, because the details are messy. Numbers vary by source. Ammunition figures, bird counts and even some dates are reported differently in different accounts. So treat the tallies as estimates, and treat the broad story as the stable part. Nobody has a clean receipt for this war. We have seen plenty of plans like this since, and the pattern is always the same: a tidy idea, a messy field, and a bill that goes to somebody else.

That's the arc: promises to soldiers, a depression, a flock that doesn't read the plan, and a government that sent guns to do a fence's job. Which brings us back to the hook. What could go wrong? The answer, it turns out, is nearly everything, plus a few thousand birds walking away in formation. The birds had not received the memo, and they weren't going to. Nobody has sent them a memo since, either.
NARRATION>>>
