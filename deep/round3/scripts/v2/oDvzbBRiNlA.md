<<<NARRATION
Why do things exist? It sounds like a question for philosophers, and in one sense it is. But there's a narrower version we can actually work on, and it's the one that matters for everything in this series. Why does anything stick around long enough to be noticed at all? Here's the answer up front, and then I'll be honest about where it runs out. Patterns that copy themselves tend to stick around. That's the first step toward why anything like life exists.

Let's set the stage with something simple. Picture a crystal growing in a salt solution. A small seed crystal sits in the liquid, and molecules attach to it in the right arrangement. The new layer has the same shape as the seed. The crystal has, in a loose sense, copied itself. Keep that picture in mind, because it's the first concrete example of what we mean, and we'll come back to it several times.

Now the first definition, and it's a careful one. Persistence means something stays around. A rock persists without copying anything. Copying means a pattern makes another pattern like itself. A crystal does both. It persists, and it makes new crystals that match it. Those two things are different, and the difference is the entire point of this video. Most of what follows is about why the second one matters so much.

Here's the first instinct people often have, and I had it too. Things exist because they're sturdy. A tough rock lasts a long time, so toughness must explain why it's here. When I first thought about this question, I assumed the same thing. Sturdiness does explain why a rock lasts. But it doesn't explain why a rock spreads, because nothing about a rock makes more rocks. That's the piece the instinct misses.

So the sturdy-things idea gets the rock right and gets the copies wrong. A pattern that copies itself doesn't have to be tough to stick around. It has to make more of itself than it loses. That's a different kind of survival, and it's the one that builds lineages over time. Dropping the sturdiness idea in favour of copying is the first real move in this argument, and it's a move worth making on purpose.

Now a common worry, and it's worth addressing directly. Doesn't copying need a designer? Doesn't something have to set up the pattern every time? Not in the crystal case. The seed is the template, and the molecules follow the rules of their own shape. No planner is needed for that. Persistence is not the same thing as design, and a pattern that copies itself can get going with no one in charge at all.

Let's look at a stamp for a moment. Imagine a carved stamp. You press it into clay, and the clay takes the shape. Press again, and you get another copy. The stamp doesn't need a new designer for each press. It needs ink and a surface, and that's all. This is a useful picture, because it shows that copying can run on simple rules. But the picture has a breakpoint. A stamp never makes a mistake, and real copying does. That's the next idea, and it's an important one.

Let's name the thing we've been describing. A replicator is a pattern that copies itself, and the copies are the important part. We could have named it at the start, but the word would have meant nothing until we saw the crystal making crystals. So we named it after the copying was on screen. A replicator is what persists by making more of itself, and nothing else is needed in the definition.

Here's a question to pause on. Which lasts longer: a single crystal, or the line of copies that comes out of it? Pick one, and hold on to it. Think about what happens when the first crystal dissolves. Make your call before we answer, because the answer depends on whether the persistence lives in the individual or in the chain.

The answer is the line of copies. Any single crystal can dissolve, and eventually it will. But the chain can keep going, as long as there's material and a surface to copy onto. The individual is temporary, and the pattern can outlast every one of its members. That's a surprising point, and it's the first real hint of why things exist at all. What persists is the pattern, not any particular piece of it.

Now the caveat, and it's an honest one. We don't know whether copying started in one place or in many. We don't know which setting produced the first pattern that could copy itself. Several candidates have been proposed, and each one has a different level of support. We can say which ones fit the evidence better. We can't say that any one of them is what actually happened. So each candidate carries its own label, and the question stays open.

Here's a limit that matters, and it's the reason the story doesn't go on forever. Copies need material. A crystal can only grow where there's dissolved salt, and a chain of copies stops when the material runs out. So copying alone isn't enough to sustain a pattern. The pattern also has to find what it needs in its surroundings. That's the first place where the story gets interesting, because it means persistence depends on the world as well as the pattern.

Let's generalize. The same shape shows up well beyond crystals. Genes get copied from one body to the next. Songs and ideas get copied from person to person. Each copy is a pattern making more of itself, and the lines of copies outlast the individuals that carry them. The crystal is simpler, but the logic is the same, and that's why it's such a useful place to start.

Here's where copying runs into a real problem, and it's the setup for the next step. Real copies aren't perfect. Each one comes out a little different, sometimes better and sometimes worse. Some of the variants copy faster or last longer, and they spread. That mixture of copying, variation and limits is the seed of evolution. The stamp analogy breaks at exactly this point, because a stamp doesn't make mistakes, and copies do.

So here's the payoff, and it's the answer we set out to find, at least in its narrow form. Things persist when they copy themselves, and the copies keep the pattern going even as the individuals die. The reason anything like a pattern exists in the first place is that some patterns copy themselves, and the lines of copies stick around. That's the stage. The rest of the story is about what happens when the copies are imperfect, and that's where we go next.

So where does that leave the big question? Why do things exist? Here's the honest version. Patterns that copy themselves persist, and the ones that persist are the ones we see. What we don't know is how the first copying pattern came about, and that's still an open question. The stage is set. The next step is to ask what happens when the copying gets messy.
