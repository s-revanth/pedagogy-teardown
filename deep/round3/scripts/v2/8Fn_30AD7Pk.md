## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Highway numbers aren't names. They're coordinates. Once the system is clear, the number tells you which way a road runs, and roughly where it sits. A street grid works the same way. You can find a block without knowing what anyone calls the corner.

Think of a city laid out on a grid. Fifth Avenue and Twelfth Street point at one spot. You don't need a name for the corner. The numbers do the work. The interstate system does the same job, but for a whole country instead of a city.

Here's a shield on a sign. It says I-90. That number isn't a label somebody picked because it sounded nice. It encodes where the road goes. And the code is simple enough that you can read it at highway speed.

Start with the simplest part. Two-digit numbers are the main routes, the long ones that cross big distances. Interstate 90 runs across the northern part of the country, east to west. Interstate 10 runs across the south. Those two sit at the top and bottom edges of the map.

Now the rule that does most of the work. Odd numbers run north and south. Even numbers run east and west. Seeing I-5 tells you it's a north-south route on the west coast. Seeing I-80 tells you it's east-west.

The pattern goes further. Within each family, the numbers count up in a direction. North-south routes count up from west to east. So I-5 sits on the west coast, and I-95 sits on the east coast. East-west routes count up from south to north. So I-10 is down in the south, and I-90 is up in the north.

Notice what that does. Knowing nothing about a road except its number still lets you place it roughly on the map. A low odd number is a western north-south route. A high even number is a northern east-west one. You've just used the number as a coordinate.

Why bother with numbers at all? Before this system existed, roads mostly had names. Some were named after the people who pushed for them, some after the places they connected, and some after a color or a feature of the land. Names are fine for a local driver who already knows the road. They fail for a stranger, because a name tells you nothing about where the road goes. A number can be read by anyone the first time they see one.

Now the three-digit numbers. These are not main routes. They're attached to a main route, and the last two digits tell you which main route it belongs to. So I-405 is tied to I-5, because 05 is the parent. An I-290 would be tied to I-90, and so on.

The first digit tells you what kind of attachment it is. An odd first digit means a loop. That's a road that leaves the parent route and comes back to it, going around a city. An even first digit means a spur. That's a road that leaves the parent and heads into a city, and stops there.

I-405 is a good example. In the Seattle area, it runs as a loop around the east side of the city and reconnects with I-5 at both ends. The 4 says you're dealing with a loop, and the 05 says it belongs to the north-south route on the west coast. You can read the whole relationship from the digits.

The same pattern shows up elsewhere. The beltway around Washington, D.C. is I-495, and the 95 at the end tells you it's a loop of I-95. Atlanta's perimeter road is I-285, a loop of I-85. Each loop sits around a city, and the digits give the parent route away before anyone reads a town name. Ninety-five and eighty-five are both two-digit main routes, so the loops hang off the top of the grid, not the middle.

Let's try one. Here is a number you might see on a sign: I-64. Before I tell you, pause and decide. Is I-64 a north-south route, or an east-west one? Make your call.

Okay. It's even, so it's east-west. It's two digits, so it's one of the main routes. Everything I just said says so. If you got it right, it wasn't luck. You used the rule.

Try another. I-35 is odd, so it runs north and south, and it sits near the middle of the country as a long corridor that carries heavy truck traffic. Some routes also carry nicknames. Those are the names people remember, but the number is the one on the sign.

Now, I want to be honest about how far the rule goes. It's a very good rule, but it isn't a law of physics. A few routes break the pattern. Usually that happens because a route was renumbered, or because it grew out of an older road that had its own name and its own history. The system was built on purpose, but it was built over decades, by people with other problems to solve. Some of the seams show.

That history is worth a minute, because it explains why the system looks so tidy. The interstates were planned in the 1950s, when the federal government funded a large network of new highways across the country. The numbering was designed to be readable from a moving car, so drivers could navigate without stopping to read a map. That's the point. The sign is the interface. A number that tells you direction at highway speed is doing a job a name can't do.

And this is where the coordinate idea gets interesting. A name is a label you have to learn. A coordinate is information you can use the first time you see it. The system doesn't require you to know anything about the road in advance. A stranger from another state can read I-35 and make a good guess before they've ever driven it.

Now the anchor. Why does one road matter so much? Think about I-90 again, the northern cross-country route. Because it sits at the top of the map, it tells you something useful about every east-west road below it. Every even number between it and I-10 is one of the cross-country routes, placed somewhere between the top edge and the bottom edge. The one northern road gives you the whole ruler.

I want to be careful about claiming too much here. Not every numbered system works like this. Other countries use different schemes, and some use names outright. The American interstate system is a specific design, and it's a good example of numbers doing the work that names usually do.

The numbers have an older cousin, too. The first national route numbers came in 1926, and before that, most roads went by names. The Interstates came later, in 1956, when Congress passed the Federal-Aid Highway Act and funded a network planned at around 41,000 miles. Today it's close to 50,000 miles, and nearly all of it carries a number that follows the same pattern. That's a lot of coordinates to get right on the first attempt, and the planners mostly did.

It's also worth knowing that a number can mislead if you read it too literally. A route number tells you the family and the rough position. It doesn't tell you where the road is at every point along its length. Roads wander, the country is not a perfect grid, and a route can change character as it crosses a state line. Use the number as a first guess, then check the map. The rule probably holds for most routes you'll ever drive, and that's the useful part.

Why design for a glance? A driver at highway speed gets a glance, not a study session, so the design has to pay off fast. The design was a bet that drivers would learn the pattern quickly, and most of them did. The numbers keep working on a stranger's first drive. Now let's go back to the sign. It's not just a shield with a number. It's a small map you can read at seventy miles an hour.

So here's the game to play next time you're on a highway. Look at the number. Decide whether it's odd or even. If it's odd, it runs north and south, and a low number means farther west. If it's even, it runs east and west, and a low number means farther south. If it's three digits, look at the last two first. Then see whether the first digit is odd or even, and decide if the road is a loop or a spur.

Try it on every shield you pass. Most people are surprised how quickly they stop reading names and start reading coordinates. It takes a few tries, and after that it's automatic.

That's the whole idea. A highway number doesn't tell you what a place is called. It tells you where the road is headed, and where it fits in the grid. Highway numbers aren't names. They're coordinates, and the map is printed right there on the sign.
NARRATION>>>
