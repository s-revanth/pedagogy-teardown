## Narration

<<<NARRATION
You have probably heard that you can't take the square root of a negative number. Your calculator agrees with you. Yet mathematicians have been using the square root of minus one for nearly five centuries, and it now sits at the heart of how your phone talks to the internet. The catch is that the people who first used these numbers did not believe in them.

Here is the answer up front: imaginary numbers were not a hobby for mathematicians. They came out of solving cubic equations, the ones with x cubed in them, and the formula for those equations sometimes passed through square roots of negative numbers, even when the final answer was an ordinary number. I want to show you how that works, one step at a time, and why it took nearly three centuries to be accepted.

Let's start with the number line. Every number you can square gives a positive result, or zero. Square any number, positive or negative, and you get something positive or zero. So nothing on the line squares to minus one, and the square root of minus one seems to live nowhere at all. That is where most of us first hit a wall in school. Why would anyone keep going? Mathematicians didn't stop.

So let's do what a mathematician does. Give the thing a name and see what breaks. Call the square root of minus one i, and that is the whole definition. You don't need to believe in it yet; you only need to follow its rules and see where they lead.

Let's take a simple example. Suppose x squared equals minus four. If i is a number whose square is minus one, then two i squared is minus four, so x is two i. Nothing about that is mysterious; it is just algebra with a new letter, and you can check it yourself by squaring two i on a piece of paper.

Here are the rules we need. i times i is minus one. You can add and multiply it the way you would any letter, collecting the real terms and the imaginary terms separately. Everything else follows from those few lines, which is part of why the idea survived as long as it did.

The first serious encounter came around 1545, with the Italian mathematician Gerolamo Cardano. He had a method for cubic equations, but when he followed it through, he sometimes met square roots of negative numbers. He labelled them as useless, and then he kept computing with them anyway, which is a very mathematician thing to do; he did not know what to make of them, and he said so.

Here is the problem that forced the issue. Take the cubic x cubed equals fifteen x plus four. We know the answer is four, because four cubed is sixty-four, and fifteen times four plus four is also sixty-four. But the formula for this equation tells you to take the cube roots of two plus eleven i and of two minus eleven i. If you refuse to touch the i's, the formula looks like it produces nonsense for a problem that has a perfectly real solution.

This is where Rafael Bombelli comes in, in 1572. Bombelli treated those strange numbers as tools rather than mysteries, and he worked out the arithmetic. He noticed that two plus i, cubed, gives two plus eleven i. So the cube root of two plus eleven i is two plus i, and the cube root of two minus eleven i is two minus i. Now what happens when you add the two roots together? The imaginary parts cancel out, and what is left is four, the real answer.

Add those two roots and the imaginary parts cancel, leaving four. Plug four back into the original equation and it checks out. Nothing about the answer is imaginary, and the detour through impossible numbers was the only route the formula could take, and you have now seen the whole thing from start to finish.

Bombelli published rules for this kind of arithmetic, and he is often credited with taking the first honest step toward using these numbers, though he still did not like the word that came with them.

In 1637, René Descartes gave them a name that stuck, but it was not a compliment. He called them imaginary, as if they were only figments of the mind and not real things. That word has done a lot of damage. A student hearing imaginary tends to think fake, and that is not what the concept means at all. A better word would have described where they sit, which is off the line, not outside mathematics.

So here is a picture that helps us see it. Multiply any number by i, and something interesting happens. Take one, and multiply by i to get i. Multiply again to get minus one, multiply once more to get minus i, and once more to get back to one. Each multiplication turns the point ninety degrees around the origin. Multiplying by i is a rotation, and that is the first clue that these numbers are about direction, not about nothing.

That is the bridge from the line to a plane. Keep the ordinary numbers running across, and put the imaginary numbers running up. Now every number is a point, with a horizontal part and a vertical part. Caspar Wessel, a Norwegian surveyor, wrote this down in 1797, and Jean-Robert Argand published his own version a few years later. Carl Friedrich Gauss made the plane picture standard in the early nineteenth century.

Around 1748, Leonhard Euler found a link that made the rotation idea precise. He showed that the exponential function, raised to i times an angle, gives the cosine of that angle plus i times its sine. In plain terms, i is the key that turns a wiggle into a circle. What do you get for a right angle? The output is i itself. That is a worked example, and you can check it against a calculator if you have ninety seconds.

Push that one step further, and you get an equation that many people call the most beautiful in mathematics. e to the i pi, plus one, equals zero. A handful of famous numbers in one line, and the imaginary unit is holding them together. You don't have to love it to see what it does.

Let's run the rotation on the board one more time: start at one, multiply by i, and watch it swing up to i. Again, and it lands on minus one. Again, minus i. Each step is the same operation, and that repetition is the whole story of the picture; nothing new has been added, only the same move applied once more.

The next rung belongs to Carl Friedrich Gauss. In 1799, in his doctoral thesis, he proved that every polynomial has a root once you let the numbers be complex. A polynomial of degree n has n roots, counting repeats. That result makes the complex numbers a closed system for algebra. Why does that matter? Nothing has to be added later to finish the job.

One rung is left, and it is the one that breaks the pattern. Before we get there, count what the imaginary numbers have already done. They solved cubics. They turned points into rotations. They turned waves into circles. And they closed the algebra.

Here is the payoff: the same i that once looked like a dead end is what let every polynomial finish with a full set of answers. The impossible detour from 1545 turned out to be the shortest road home.

Now we need a caveat, because the name still misleads. A complex number does not read off a ruler, and nothing in your kitchen weighs an imaginary amount. In many physics problems, the number we actually measure is only the real part, and the i is a bookkeeping device that keeps two quantities tied together. The imaginary part is real mathematics. It is just not a real length.

Where does it show up? Alternating current is the classic case. A voltage that swings up and down can be drawn as an arrow spinning in a circle, and the i is the tool that keeps track of the shift between voltage and current.

Then there is signal processing, where the arithmetic that turns radio waves into the video on your phone, and the coding that keeps a Wi-Fi link stable, leans on the same complex numbers. If you ever wondered why the mathematics of the 1500s still matters, the answer is in your pocket.

How far does this go? Quantum mechanics goes further. Its wave functions are complex valued, and the rules that generate probabilities depend on the imaginary parts working correctly. The outcomes you measure are real numbers, but the story underneath needs the i to hold together.

Once mathematicians had complex numbers, the natural question was whether you could add another imaginary unit and keep the rules. In 1843, William Rowan Hamilton found a four part system, the quaternions, but only by giving up a rule you had always taken for granted. In quaternions, the order of multiplication matters. That is the break in the ladder. Each new number system gives up something comfortable.

Quaternions still do real work: they rotate objects in computer graphics and guide spacecraft. The price is that the familiar rules change.

The name changed as well, and through the nineteenth century mathematicians drifted toward the word complex, partly to remove the insult. By the time Gauss used it in 1831, the new name had a better chance of sticking. But the old word lingered in textbooks, and you will still see imaginary parts in every engineering circuit diagram.

So let's go back to the cubic one last time: four cubed, minus fifteen times four, minus four, equals zero. The detour through impossible numbers was necessary to reach a plain real answer. The formula did not care whether the middle steps felt respectable.

The lesson is not that imaginary numbers are real in the everyday sense. They are not. The lesson is that a number can fail to sit on a line and still be the most useful tool in the room. Mathematicians spent centuries learning to trust something they could not picture. Then they used it to build the modern world.

The number nobody believed in is the one that finished the equation.
