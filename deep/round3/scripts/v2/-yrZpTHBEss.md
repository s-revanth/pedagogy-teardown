<<<NARRATION
Michael here. What colour is a mirror? If you are like most people, you answered before I finished the sentence. Silver, obviously. It is shiny, it is metal, and it is the first thing that comes to mind. I am going to argue that this obvious answer is wrong, but first, let's test it properly.

A mirror has no colour of its own. It hands back the colours that hit it. That is a bold claim, so you should check it yourself. Look at the edge of a mirror, where the thick glass shows. Often it looks faintly green. That is the first hint that the glass, not some silver paint, is part of what you are seeing.

Now let's set the scene. Picture a bathroom mirror, then turn off the light. In a dark room, the mirror looks black. Nothing is coming in, so nothing comes back. If the mirror were silver in its own right, it would still look silver in the dark. It does not. It only looks like a mirror when light falls on it, and the less light there is, the less of a mirror it seems.

So let's shine some light on it. Take a green flashlight and point it at the mirror. The mirror now looks green. Put a red apple in front of it, and the reflection looks red. Swap the apple for a blue cup, and the reflection turns blue. Each time, the mirror is only passing along the colour it was given, in from the light and out to your eye.

Here is the physics in plain words. A mirror is a sheet of glass, often a few millimetres thick, with a thin coating of metal behind it, usually silver or aluminium. That coating is only about a ten-thousandth of a millimetre deep, and it reflects roughly nine tenths of the visible light that hits it. It reflects the different colours of visible light nearly equally. Because the reflection is almost even across colours, nothing about the mirror tints what it returns. It is close to neutral, which is why it looks like the room.

A simple way to picture it is a wall you bounce a ball off. The wall does not change the ball. The ball goes in, and the same ball comes back out, with its colour and most of its speed intact. A mirror does the same thing with light. It returns what arrives, almost unchanged, and it does so in a fraction of a second.

I like to test this at home with two sheets of white paper, a red marker and a window. Lay one sheet against the mirror and colour it red at the edge. Then look at how the reflection of that sheet changes as you turn it. We tend to forget how much the room is part of the picture, and this little test makes that obvious. If a mirror sent back only half the light, we would notice the room getting dimmer right away.

I should be honest about the limits. The coating is not perfectly even. Silver and aluminium differ a little in how they reflect each colour, and a cheap mirror can carry a faint tint of its own. Old mirrors can drift grey or yellow as the coating wears down over decades. So the answer is near-neutral, not perfectly neutral. That tiny gap is exactly what makes the green edge visible.

Now step back. If the colour does not belong to the mirror, where does it come from? It comes from the light and from the room. The mirror is only a surface that sends the light back, and the light decides what is sent. That is the real answer. Colour is a relationship between the light, the object and your eyes, not a paint on the glass.

So what colour is a mirror? A mirror is the colour of whatever is in front of it, and of whatever light is falling on that thing. Put a green cloth in front of it, and the mirror turns green. Turn the lights off, and it turns black. The silver was never the whole story, and you can check that in about ten seconds with any mirror you own.

What colour is a mirror, in the dark? Not silver. Not anything at all.

Thanks for watching.
