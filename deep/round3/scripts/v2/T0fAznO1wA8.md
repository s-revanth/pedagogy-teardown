## Narration
<<<NARRATION
The captain of a pirate ship wasn't really the boss. That sounds backwards, so let's start with the picture most of us carry around, and then turn it over.

The familiar case is a navy ship. On a navy vessel, the captain is the boss in the full sense. His word is the rule. The crew carry out orders, and disobeying them is a serious offense that can end in a court-martial. If you picture a captain, you're probably picturing that.

Navy captains weren't tyrants by default. The point is where the authority came from. In the navy it came from a commission and a chain of command, and the crew had no say in who held the post.

Now here's the reversal. On a pirate ship, the captain was elected by the crew, could be voted out by them, and had to live under a set of written rules that the crew had agreed to. The captain had authority, but it was on loan from the people he led.

Let's be exact about what that meant. A pirate captain ran the important things during a chase or a fight. In those moments, orders had to be obeyed fast, and there was no time for debate. But the rest of the time, the crew held the power, and they used it.

The clearest evidence for this comes from the written articles. Many pirate crews drew up a set of rules and had everyone sign or swear to them. One of the best-known sets belonged to the crew of Bartholomew Roberts, a pirate active in the early 1720s. Many of these rules are known because they were recorded in a book published in 1724, which gives an account of several pirate crews.

Here's the first rung on the ladder. The articles set out how the loot was divided. The captain got a larger share than an ordinary crewman, but not a royal share. A bit more, not a kingdom. The crew could read the numbers, and the arrangement was a bargain, not a favor.

Second rung. The articles set out who handled disputes. Pirate crews often had a quartermaster, chosen by the crew, who looked after the loot and settled quarrels between the men. The quartermaster was the crew's representative. When the captain and the crew disagreed, the quartermaster was often the person who spoke for the crew, and sometimes that meant the captain had to back down.

Third rung. The articles set out what happened if a crewman got hurt. They listed fixed payments for losing a limb or an eye. That's a striking thing to write down, because it means injury had a price the crew had agreed on in advance. It also means the crew expected to get hurt, and wanted to be sure they'd be looked after.

Pause on that for a second, because it's a clue about the whole system. A captain who ruled by fear doesn't need written payments for injury. A crew that holds the power wants them in writing.

Now a caveat, because this is where care is needed with the sources. Our main account comes from that 1724 book, and its author wrote under the name Captain Charles Johnson. Nobody is entirely sure who that was. The book was written for readers who loved a good pirate story, and it has its own agenda. Some of the details are probably embellished, and some may be invented. So when I say the articles say this, I mean the account says the articles said this. That's the best evidence we have, but it isn't a court record of every ship.

Now, the second part of the title. The captain could be removed. And crews did it. A captain who lost the crew's confidence could be voted out in the same way he'd been voted in. Sometimes that meant a new captain. Sometimes it meant the old one was put ashore, marooned on a beach or an island with a few supplies and not much else. Either way, the power was the crew's to take back.

That's a big difference from a navy ship. On a navy vessel, a captain who loses the crew's confidence is still the captain. The crew can complain, petition, and hope. They can't vote him off the ship. The pirate captain was in a different position altogether. His authority depended on continuing to earn it.

Why would a crew build that? Think about who was on these ships. Many pirates had been sailors before. They had served on merchant ships and navy ships, under captains who could flog them, cheat their pay, or keep them at sea for years. Some had been pressed into service against their will. When they took a ship, they knew exactly what it felt like to have someone else's word be law, and they didn't want to live that way again.

Pirate life also made the risk concrete. Every raid could end in a hanging, so the crew wanted a say in decisions that could get them killed. A captain who made bad calls could end the voyage for everyone. So the crew's control was a kind of insurance.

And there was a practical reason too. A pirate crew wasn't a fixed institution with a rulebook from above. It was made up of people who had chosen to be there, often from different ships, sometimes from different countries. They had to find a way to live together that didn't rely on one person's temper. Written articles were a way to make the arrangement stick, because everyone could point to the same page.

Did this make pirate ships democracies in the modern sense? Not really. A pirate crew wasn't a town meeting. There was no universal franchise, and a crew could be fierce and brutal to the people it captured. The point isn't that pirates were gentle. The point is that the captain's authority wasn't the kind of absolute authority we usually picture.

And that's the reversal. The familiar picture of a captain as an absolute boss is right for a navy ship. It's wrong for a pirate ship. Put the two side by side and the difference is the source of the authority. In the navy it comes down from above. On the pirate ship it comes up from the crew, and it can be taken back.

Put yourself in the crew's place. If you'd spent years under a captain who could flog you and cheat your pay, you'd want that captain's power written down, limited and removable. If you want proof, the pirates just wrote the terms down.

Let's go back to the articles one more time, because they keep coming back in every account. They are the object that holds the whole arrangement together. Without them, a captain's authority would be a matter of mood and muscle. With them, it's a document, and everyone knows what it says.

On the sources, you should know that most of what historians know about the articles, the votes and the punishments comes from accounts that weren't neutral. Some were written to sell books. Some were written to portray pirates as monsters, and some to portray them as rebels. A careful historian treats each detail as a claim to check, not a fact to repeat.

With that in mind, the picture still holds. The crews had written rules, they had a way to remove a captain, and they had a quartermaster who spoke for them. What survives the noise? Those three things recur across accounts often enough that they are the best-supported parts of the story.

So what's the lesson? Authority on a pirate ship was a loan. The captain could use it when the moment needed a single voice. The crew kept the right to ask for it back, in writing, with a vote.

That's why the title works. The pirate captain wasn't really the boss, in the sense we usually mean. He was the leader the crew had picked and could replace, under rules they had written themselves. The boss, in the end, was the people who held the articles.
