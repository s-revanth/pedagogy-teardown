## Narration

<<<NARRATION
I'd like to start with a question that sounds backwards. Why is there antimatter? If you look around, the answer seems obvious, which is that there is almost none. Your chair, your coffee and your own body are made of ordinary matter. So I want you to ask a different question. Not why antimatter exists, but why matter exists at all, because that is the real puzzle.

Here is the reframe, stated plainly. Antimatter is made all the time. High energy collisions in particle accelerators produce antiparticles, and cosmic rays strike the atmosphere and make them too. The strange thing is not that antimatter exists. The strange thing is that the universe seems to contain far more matter than antimatter, and I want to be precise about why that imbalance should not be there at all.

Let's use a simple toy. Picture two particles, an electron and its antiparticle, the positron. They have the same mass and opposite charge, and they sit next to each other like a mirror image. When they meet, they annihilate, and the energy they carried comes out as light. Keep that pair in mind, because you will see it again and again.

The energy that comes out follows Einstein's famous relation. Energy equals mass times the speed of light squared, or E equals m c squared. For our pair, m is the mass of one particle, c is the speed of light, and E is the energy released, which is the rest energy of two particles together. You don't need the derivation to see the scale. A tiny amount of mass becomes an enormous amount of energy.

The first rung of the ladder is a prediction. In 1928, Paul Dirac combined quantum mechanics with special relativity, and the resulting equation had solutions that looked like an electron with positive charge. He was not sure what to make of them at first. I find that a striking example of mathematics running ahead of experiment, and it is worth remembering when you hear a bold new equation. I'd say it is one of the best examples in the whole field.

In 1932, Carl Anderson saw the positron in cosmic ray tracks, a particle with the electron's mass and the opposite charge. Its track bent the wrong way in a magnetic field, and that was the clue. Anderson was not looking for Dirac's prediction, but he found it anyway. Two particles, one mass, and two signs of charge.

Physicists soon made antiprotons as well, in accelerators built for that purpose, starting in 1955. By the 1950s, antimatter was no longer a theoretical curiosity. It was a routine product of high energy collisions, and you could make it on demand, one collision at a time.

Now the imbalance, and I want you to take part. Suppose, if you could, that the early universe started with equal amounts of matter and antimatter. When they met, they would annihilate in pairs and leave only light behind. So how much matter should be left at the end? What would you have said? Make a guess before I tell you, and pick a fraction you can defend.

The answer is about one extra particle of matter for every billion pairs. Everything else annihilated into radiation, and what is left is the matter we see today. That is an absurd magnitude, and it is why the story is so unsettling. The universe we live in is the leftover of a tiny imbalance. If you guessed a large fraction, you were in good company, because most of us expect symmetry to win.

So let's be clear about the puzzle. Symmetry predicts no imbalance at all, and the observed imbalance is real. Something in the early universe must have broken the symmetry. What could do that? The question is what, and that is where the search begins.

The second rung is an experiment. In 1964, James Cronin and Val Fitch measured a tiny difference in how neutral kaons decay compared with their antiparticles. That broke a symmetry physicists had assumed was exact, called CP symmetry, which combines charge reversal with a mirror flip. It was a surprise, and it earned a Nobel Prize in 1980.

Think about what CP symmetry means, and what it would take for you to notice a crack in it. Why should a crack matter so much? If you swap every particle for its antiparticle and then look at the world in a mirror, the laws should look the same. The kaon result said they do not, quite. It was a small crack, but it was in exactly the place where a cosmic imbalance could hide.

The third rung is a set of conditions. In 1967, Andrei Sakharov showed that a universe starting symmetric could only produce an imbalance if three conditions held. It needs a process that changes the number of baryons, it needs C and CP violation, and it needs to depart from thermal equilibrium at some stage. Three conditions, and each one is a hard requirement.

I should be careful here, because those conditions tell us what is needed, not what happened. The known physics supplies some of the ingredients, and the question is whether it supplies enough. That is the honest state of play, and I think it is worth being clear about it.

The break in the ladder is this. The CP violation in the Standard Model is real, but it is far too small to produce the observed imbalance, by many orders of magnitude. So the known physics is not enough, and the leftover asymmetry points beyond it. That is a frustrating result, and I'd argue it is also the most useful one, because it tells us where to look.

Two candidate mechanisms are on the table, and which one is right is an open question. Which one is right? One involves heavy neutrinos that decay in slightly different ways for matter and antimatter. Another works at the electroweak scale, where the early universe passed through a phase change. Each candidate predicts something different that we could, in principle, detect, and that is what makes them testable.

The neutrino candidate has an appealing feature. The same heavy particles that would create the imbalance could also explain why neutrinos are so light. Physicists call the process leptogenesis, and the test is whether neutrinos behave the way the model needs. Experiments that send neutrino beams about 300 kilometres through the Earth are one way to look.

The electroweak candidate is different. If the phase change in the early universe was strong enough, it could have left the imbalance behind. The Standard Model says the transition was too weak, which is one reason many physicists look beyond it. We can test that by looking for extra particles or unusual behaviour in the Higgs field.

Here is a measurement you can trust more. The cosmic microwave background and the early abundance of light elements both fix how much matter the universe contains, and they agree on the same tiny number, to within roughly 10 percent. That is the one part in a billion we started with, measured from two different directions. It is a good sign that the size of the effect is right.

Those measurements are precise, but I would still say they depend on a model of the early universe. If the model is wrong in some detail, the number shifts a little. So think of the figure as a strong estimate, not a final verdict, and watch for updates as the data improve.

Another route, and one I find especially clean, is to look for a tiny electric dipole moment in the neutron. A nonzero value would be a sign of new CP violation. So far, measurements have shown none, and each null result narrows the field. A null result cannot rule out every model, but it can rule out a great many of them.

The same logic appears in dark matter research. We see its gravitational effects and infer its amount, long before we know its nature. Antimatter asks a similar question: we measure a surplus, and we need a mechanism to explain it. In both cases, the measurement comes first and the explanation follows, often years later.

So let's tally what we have. Matter won over antimatter by about one part in a billion. The imbalance needs three conditions, and the known physics supplies only some of them, too weakly. The rest is still open, and the open part is where the next experiments will look.

The positron is not only a theorist's curiosity, and I'd argue it is one of the most useful particles in medicine. Positron emission tomography, a common medical scan, uses positron emitting tracers to map how the body uses sugar. When a positron meets an electron inside you, the annihilation releases light, and the scanner detects it. If you have ever had a PET scan, that is the process your doctor was seeing.

Physicists also test a deeper symmetry called CPT, which you may have met in a textbook, and which must hold in any consistent quantum theory with the usual assumptions. Experiments compare particles and antiparticles to a part in a billion or better, and so far they agree. A difference would be a discovery, which is why these tests are designed so carefully and checked so often.

Here is a caveat that matters, and it applies to any result you read about. Many precision comparisons involve particles that are cheap to make and easy to measure, while the early universe involved conditions we cannot recreate. So a laboratory result is evidence, not the full answer. We know the requirements better than we know the mechanism, and we should be honest about that gap.

Now a longer view. Several of the candidates are being tested with neutrino experiments that will run for years, and some early hints of CP violation in neutrinos are being studied. Those hints are not yet conclusive. If they hold up, they would connect directly to the early universe, and that would be a remarkable link to make.

Consider what would happen without this tiny excess, and I think you will find the result sobering. The universe would be full of light and nothing else. No stars, no planets, no chemistry, and no one to ask the question. You would not be here to ask it. That is the stake of one part in a billion, and it is why this is more than an academic puzzle.

We know that antimatter exists. The real question is why anything is left over at all, and the answer, so far, is a tiny imbalance that nobody has fully explained.
