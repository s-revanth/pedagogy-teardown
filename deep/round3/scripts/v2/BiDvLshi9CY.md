## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Quick word from our sponsor first. It's a short one, and I promise we'll get to the war in less time than it takes to make a cup of tea. Okay, a little longer than that.

In early 1982, Argentina is in a real mess. The economy is wrecked, inflation is brutal, and the people in the streets are angry at a military government that has been making its own citizens disappear for years. So the junta has a brilliant idea. Start a short war, rally everybody behind the flag, and watch the protests evaporate. Have you ever heard of a plan that dumb? Hold that thought.

Let's back up and look at the mess first. I'll be honest, the logic of the plan is almost hypnotic, which is exactly the problem. The junta is running a country where repression is routine, and the economy is in free fall. A military government that cannot deliver bread has very few tools left. So the generals look for an enemy, and they look for one that is far away, weakly defended and easy to say yes to.

The target is a set of windswept islands in the South Atlantic. The islands have more sheep than people, and nobody asked the sheep.

The Falklands, which Argentina calls the Malvinas, have been run by Britain since 1833. Argentina says they were always theirs. The islanders, around eighteen hundred people, mostly want to stay British. So you have a claim, a population that does not agree with the claim, and a government that has been looking for a distraction. You can see where this is going.

So the junta gambles. They assume Britain is too far away, too broke and too busy to care. They assume the Americans will sit on the fence. And they assume nobody in London will send a fleet eight thousand miles to fight over a few thousand farmers. Three assumptions. Remember that number. Three. Why do I keep coming back to those three? Because every one of them was a reasonable bet on paper.

Let's do the invasion twice, once from each side. From the junta's side, the logic almost works. They invade on the second of April. The small British garrison is outnumbered, and the islands fall within a day. The crowd in Buenos Aires goes wild, and the generals start picturing a parade. The junta misread the room, but at that moment they don't know it yet. Would you have seen the trap from the balcony? No, and that's what makes it a trap.

Now look at it from London. Just months earlier, the government had proposed cutting back the navy's presence near these islands. The junta read that as a signal. Britain doesn't care. From Downing Street, it looks completely different. Here is a defence review that looked like abandonment, followed by an invasion. The prime minister has a cabinet, a press and a public that all want the same thing: not to look weak. So what did the government do next? It did what a government does when it can't afford to look weak. It sent ships. I'd argue that decision was the whole war. The junta misread the room.

So would Britain send a task force eight thousand miles to a few thousand islanders? Pause for a second. Make your guess. Yes? Yes. They did. You might have guessed no, and a lot of people in Buenos Aires guessed no too.

Within days, ships leave Portsmouth. The task force steams south across the Atlantic, and plenty of people back home don't really grasp how far eight thousand miles is until they look at the map. Distance is the thing that the junta was counting on, and distance is the thing that stops mattering when you have ships.

In late April, British forces retake South Georgia, a small island group to the east. That's the first win, and it's a big morale boost in London. The islands have more sheep than people, and nobody asked the sheep. Let's be clear about one thing: the sheep did nothing wrong. Honestly, the sheep are not the only ones who weren't asked.

On the second of May, the British submarine Conqueror sinks the Argentine cruiser Belgrano. Hundreds of sailors die. Historians still argue about whether it was the right call, and you should be suspicious of anyone who says the answer is obvious. Then, on the fourth of May, an Exocet missile hits HMS Sheffield. Those are not the numbers the junta expected from a war against a few sheep farmers. Did they have a plan for the second act? I don't think they did.

On the twenty-first of May the British land at San Carlos. From there the fighting moves inland. Goose Green falls after a hard battle at the end of May, and then the push toward Stanley, the capital, begins in earnest. It is slow, cold and miserable, and every hill has a name that somebody will be writing about for decades. I find that part hard to read, honestly.

On the fourteenth of June, Argentina surrenders. It has taken about ten weeks. The death toll is roughly nine hundred people across both sides, plus three islanders. Those figures vary a bit by source, so treat them as careful estimates rather than receipts.

A quick caveat, because it matters. Historians still argue about how much the junta planned this as a distraction from its domestic mess, and how much it simply misjudged London. Both explanations are plausible. Pick your favourite, then look at the timeline and see what it does to your choice. Which one do I believe? I lean toward the misjudgement, but I'd want to see the archives first. We've seen the same pattern in other wars: a government that wants a quick win usually gets a long one.

The junta misread the room. Every version of the story comes back to that.

The islands have more sheep than people, and nobody asked the sheep. Nobody asked the junta either.

So the short war was supposed to fix everything. It fixed nothing, and the sheep are still wondering what happened.
NARRATION>>>
