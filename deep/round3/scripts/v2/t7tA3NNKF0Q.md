## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Some of the biggest numbers on the internet are not what they seem, and some of those views are taken from creators who never agreed to share them. Not every view is theft, because some people really do watch a video to the end, but the way the counting works makes it easy to lose track of which is which.

Picture a creator who spends seven days on one video. If you were that creator, you would care about every frame of it. It goes up on their own channel, and a few days later a page they have never heard of posts the same video with its own caption and its own logo. Within 24 hours, the copy has more views than the original, and the creator is watching their own work collect attention under someone else's name.

The reason this works is simpler than you might expect, because the counter does not know who made the video. It only knows that something was played, and it reports that fact as if it were a person paying attention.

Think of a turnstile at a stadium gate, the kind you walk through on the way to a match. Every time someone walks through, it clicks once. But the counter cannot tell whether a fan stayed for the match or walked straight past to the snack stand. A view counter works the same way, and it clicks when something starts, not when a person decides to care.

On Facebook, a video view is counted after about three seconds of playback. The video often starts on its own, through autoplay, as the feed scrolls past. Nobody has to decide anything for the counter to move, and you do not have to press play.

Let's start with rung one, the autoplay counter. A video begins to play as the feed slides past it, and three seconds later the number goes up. The person may have glanced for half a second and kept scrolling, and that view is real in the platform's ledger, but it is almost nothing in terms of human attention.

Rung two is the repost, and it is where we see the most damage. A page downloads a creator's video, adds its own caption, and posts it again. Each copy starts its own counter from zero, and each copy can collect its own views. The original creator loses traffic that would have gone to their own page, and nobody has to fake a single number for that to happen.

Rung three is the sum: say there are 50 copies spread across 12 pages. Added together, the total on the screen grows far beyond the number of people who actually cared. Rung four is the bill, and the original creator pays it in lost traffic and lost reach.

Let's pause here and make a guess. Does a view mean that someone actually watched the video? Say yes or no, and then keep going.

The answer is no, not in the sense most people expect. A view means the counter fired. It often means a few seconds of playback, sometimes started without a click, and sometimes on a copy of someone else's work. It is a signal worth tracking, but not a measure of attention.

There is also a limit to what we can say from outside. The platform's numbers are self-reported, and nobody outside can audit every copy, every autoplay and every counter. So the mechanism is strong and easy to demonstrate, while the exact size of the loss is much harder to pin down. Any single total is the platform's own count, not a neutral audit.

The same problem appears wherever we let a counter stand in for a person. Downloads, follows, likes and clicks can all be counted in ways that feel more like a turnstile than a conversation.

So what should a creator, or any of us, watch instead? We should look at watch time, meaning how long people stay. Look at how many people stay past the first 30 seconds, and where the rest leave. A huge total with short watch times is a warning sign, not a victory.

Now let's replay the first three rungs, because together they make a test. Ask whether a video shows autoplay counts, reposts and a shared total. If all three are present, the headline number is probably a mix of attention and copies. If only the first is present, the number is much closer to attention.

So the next time we see a number that looks huge, ask who it counted, and who lost views to make it. A view is a click on a counter, not a person who stayed.
NARRATION>>>
