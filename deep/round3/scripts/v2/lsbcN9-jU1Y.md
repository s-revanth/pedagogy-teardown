## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Quick word from our sponsor first. It's fifteen seconds, it's fine, and I promise the elephants won't come into the studio.

So here's the question. How do you march an army across the Alps, elephants and all, into a country that thinks it owns the Mediterranean? Hold on to that, because the answer is not what you'd expect, and the reason Rome survives it is even less expected.

Let's start in Spain. You may not know much about Spain in this period, and that's fine. Saguntum is a town on the eastern coast of the peninsula, and it's an ally of Rome. Hannibal, the young Carthaginian commander, decides to besiege it. Why would a commander pick a fight with a town so far from home? Partly for the money, partly for the glory, and partly because Saguntum is the kind of target that makes a point. Would you pick that fight? I'd guess most commanders would say yes, and then regret the paperwork. The siege lasts about eight months. And the town falls.

When Saguntum falls, Rome demands that Hannibal be handed over. Carthage refuses. And that's the moment the Second Punic War begins, in 218 BCE. I'd say that's a pretty clean chain of cause and effect, and it's why this war gets called the war of one man's decision.

Now the cast, because it's about to get busy. Hannibal, the commander who will not stop. His brother Hasdrubal, who is supposed to be holding Spain. The Carthaginian senate, which is slow to send help. Scipio, a Roman consul with a very long career ahead of him. Sempronius and Flaminius, consuls who want a battle. Fabius, who will become famous for refusing one. And Varro, who will be the one who gives Hannibal the battle he wanted. That's more than six names, so keep one thread: Hannibal, and the men he has to feed.

The route is the next part of the story. I think it's the most under-appreciated part. Spain, then the Pyrenees, then southern Gaul, then the Alps. A column of soldiers, a few dozen elephants, and a lot of animals that did not sign up for mountains. Hannibal's men come out of the Alps thinner, colder and very angry, but they come out. You would be angry too, and you'd be cold too.

The elephants did not enjoy the Alps, and nobody asked them.

At Ticinus, the Roman cavalry gets beaten, and the consul Scipio is wounded in the fighting. His son pulls him out of the mess, and the story is told for generations. You can picture it: a young man dragging his injured father from the battlefield while the cavalry falls apart around them. Hannibal wins the first real clash in Italy, and the Romans have to admit that the new enemy is serious.

Then winter arrives in northern Italy. The Gauls, who have every reason to hate Rome, join Hannibal's side. They bring horses, men and local knowledge. I'd argue that's the moment Hannibal stops being an invader and starts being a power in the north, with allies, supplies and a plan.

At the Trebia, in December 218, Hannibal baits the Romans across a freezing, flooded river. The Romans attack in the cold and the mud, and the Carthaginians are waiting for them with fresh troops. Sempronius wanted a battle, and he got one, though not the one he expected. Would you attack across a river in winter, with the enemy sitting on the far bank? The Romans did, and it cost them dearly.

The elephants did not enjoy the Alps, and nobody asked them. They were, at least, right about the river.

In 217 BCE, at Lake Trasimene, the Romans walk into an ambush. The morning mist hangs over the water, and the Carthaginian troops are already in position on the slopes. The consul Flaminius is killed, and thousands of Romans die in a few hours. The lake fills with the wounded, and the survivors are cut down as they try to escape. Let's pause for a second on the Roman side of this. Think about that from the Roman side. You march in the morning, and by the afternoon your army is gone.

After Trasimene, the Senate appoints Fabius, a cautious commander with a famous habit of refusing to fight in the open. Would you wait for a man like that while the enemy is burning your farms? I'd find it hard. People mocked him for it. The comfortable belief is that delay is cowardice, and it's easy to repeat in a bar. The flip is that delay is how Rome keeps its army alive, and it's a strategy that works only if the Romans are patient enough to keep it.

But not everybody is patient. Rome's allies start to wobble, grain prices rise, and the Senate wants a battle. The new consuls want glory. The people want the enemy gone. Could you blame them? I'd want the enemy gone too. So the pressure on Fabius's plan grows week by week, and it's not hard to see why.

Okay, a quick guess. Wait with Fabius, delaying and watching, or attack now with the new consuls, with the army they've been given? Pause and decide. Got it? Good. They attack. And that's the mistake, and you'll see why in a minute.

A quick caveat before the big battle, because the numbers matter. Army sizes and the elephant count vary between sources, and the ancient writers each had their own reasons to shape the story. Treat the scale as real and the exact tallies as careful estimates.

At Cannae, in 216 BCE, the consuls Varro and Paulus lead a huge Roman army toward Hannibal's line. Hannibal is ready. He pulls his centre back, lets the Romans push into a box, and then closes the trap. It's a double envelopment, and it's one of the cleanest tactical moves in ancient history. The Romans are surrounded from both sides, and there's nowhere to go.

The result is a slaughter. Tens of thousands of Romans die in a single day. Rome has lost an army in a way it has rarely lost one before. The elephants did not enjoy the Alps, and nobody asked the Romans either.

Here's the wall. Cannae is one of the worst defeats in Roman history, and the fates of the consuls are argued over in the sources. Some of the stories were possibly written to make a point, so treat them as competing accounts rather than a clean record. Whatever happened, the Roman army in the field is gone.

Then comes the break. Capua, a wealthy city in the south, switches sides and opens its gates to Hannibal. For a commander who has been fighting far from home, that's a huge prize. Hannibal now has a base, a city, and a reason for the south to think twice about Rome.

Here's the important part. Losing a battle is not losing a war. Rome has manpower, the Senate is not finished, and the next levy is already being raised. So when people say Rome must be finished after Cannae, I'd say that's the comfortable belief, and the flip is that Rome is still standing and still raising armies. Rome lost an army and is still standing, which is rude of Rome, honestly.

Hannibal, meanwhile, is stuck in Italy. If you were him, would you stay? I think I'd go home, but I'm not the one with the elephants. Year after year he wins battles and cannot take the one thing that would end the war. He can march around Italy, collect allies and win in the field, but he can't force Rome to surrender. And the longer he stays, the more the question becomes what he can actually do with all that success.

So why does this matter? Because the Roman problem is a rival that wins battles but cannot take cities, cannot force a peace and cannot get the home front to give up. That's a very modern kind of problem, and I'd argue it's the real reason the war lasted as long as it did.

The sources for all of this are Polybius and Livy, written well after the events, with their own agendas, which is probably the most important caveat of all. Look at the pattern together: we have a commander who keeps winning, and a Senate that keeps sending the same kind of army. A quick reminder: treat their details as a starting point, not a final answer. You'll see that same caution in the next part.

Let's do a little skit, because the Senate deserves one. A senator stands up in the chamber and asks why the army keeps losing. The general says the enemy is very good. The senator asks whether that's a plan. The general says it's a diagnosis. The senator says it's expensive. And nobody has a good answer for the bill.

So how does Rome survive a man who keeps winning in its own backyard? That's the question we started with, and it's still open. Hannibal has won the battles. He has the allies, the elephants and the reputation. What he doesn't have yet is a way to make Rome give up, and that's exactly the question that carries us into the next part of this story.
