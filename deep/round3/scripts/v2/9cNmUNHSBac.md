## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Ask ten people how a bicycle stays upright while it is moving, and I bet you will hear some version of spinning wheels. Gyroscopes, the spin keeps it up, that kind of thing, and I had the same story in my head for years. Why does a bike need so little help to stay up in the first place? It sounds right, and that is exactly why it is worth checking, because a plausible story is the easiest one to stop questioning. So here is the short answer, up front. A bike stays up mainly because of its steering geometry, especially where the front wheel touches the ground, not because of spinning wheels alone. The spinning does matter a little, and I'll come back to that.

Let's start with a bike on a stand. Spin the front wheel, then turn the handlebars. Notice that the wheel doesn't swing sideways like a door. The fork tilts, and the wheel sets itself into a new line. That is the first clue, and it's the thing most of us never notice when we ride. Did you ever wonder why the bars feel so light at speed, and why they seem to want to go straight on their own? I did, for a long time.

Look at the front fork. The steering axis, the line the fork rotates around, does not hit the ground at the same spot as the wheel's contact patch. The contact point sits a little behind where that axis meets the ground. Engineers call that offset the trail. Typical road bikes have a trail of around 50 millimetres, and mountain bikes often have more. You can see the offset clearly if you drop a plumb line from the steering axis to the road, and I'd recommend doing it with your own bike before you believe a word of this.

A simple way to picture trail is the wheels on a supermarket shopping cart. The little caster wheels trail behind their pivot, and there are usually four of them. That is why a cart pushed sideways snaps back into line the moment you let go, which is something most of us have felt without ever naming it. The front of a bike works in a more interesting way. When the wheel is trailing properly, steering and leaning are linked, and that link is what we need to understand, so let's be careful about each step.

Now let's roll the bike and lean it. When the bike tilts to one side, it wants to fall that way, and we can see why in a moment. The steering responds, and the front wheel swings toward the lean, putting the contact point back under the centre of mass. You can feel this for yourself if you ride with a loose grip on a quiet road, and it is one of the first things a rider learns. Lean left, and the bars turn left. Lean right, and they turn right. Why would a bike do that? Do it two or three times, and you'll notice your body doing it without being asked.

That leads to a counterintuitive move called countersteering. To lean a bike to the left, you push the bar briefly to the right, for one or two seconds. The front wheel swings right, the bike rolls out from under you and tips left, and then the trail and the lean bring you round into a turn. I know it sounds backwards. Most riders learn it long before they know the name, and I did too.

Let's test that on the board. We can slow it down, and it helps to see it twice. Here is a rider demonstrating: bars right, and the bike leans left. Count to three, and you'll see the bike is already on its way before the bars have finished turning. That is the whole mechanism in one clip. The steering is the control input, and the lean is the response.

I'd like to understand that before we dismiss it, so where does the gyroscope story come from? The idea is that spinning wheels act like gyroscopes, and a gyroscope resists being tilted, so the bike stays up. It sounds neat. The problem is that it is only part of the picture, and in most bikes a small part. A gyroscope stabilises a spinning top, but the front wheel of a bike is also steering freely, and that changes what the spin does. Is the gyroscope wrong, then? Not exactly. It is just not the main story.

The bicycle's ancestor is the draisine, built by Karl Drais in 1817. It had no pedals and no chain. A rider pushed along with their feet and balanced by steering. Drais was already relying on the steer to keep the machine up, long before anyone wrote down a formula for it, and I find that a humbling lesson.

Then, in 1899, the British mathematician Francis Whipple wrote out the equations of motion for a bicycle. His analysis showed something important. At a sensible speed, around twenty kilometres an hour, a bike is self-stabilising, which is a comfortable cruise for most riders, but at very low speed it wobbles and falls. Speed matters, and that is a clue that the stability is not just a property of spinning wheels. We will return to speed in a moment, because it turns out to be the key to the whole demonstration.

Jump forward to 1970, when the engineer David Jones built a bicycle with two wheels counter-rotated, so their spin angular momentum cancelled out. The gyroscope story predicts the bike should be hard to balance. Jones reported that it could still be ridden, and I find that result startling after more than fifty years of folklore. That is a clean test, and it knocked a big hole in the popular explanation.

In 2007, Meijaard and colleagues published a benchmark that compared the full set of equations with experiments and earlier models. Their conclusion was that gyroscopic effects are real but small, and that trail and steering geometry do most of the work. That is the consensus you will find in many engineering texts, although the details still depend on the rider. We still rely on that work today, and it is worth reading slowly.

And here is where the story breaks, and I want to be honest about it. The linear models that describe a bike going straight are approximations. They capture the gist, and they tell you what happens for small disturbances. They do not tell you how a real person balances a bike with a bag on one side, a loose grip, and a crosswind coming through. Those cases need more work, and I'd be wary of anyone who claims the question is closed.

So the honest version is this. The geometry explanation is the best short answer, but it has limits. It is about bikes on flat ground, at speeds from about five to thirty kilometres an hour, with a rider holding roughly steady. If you add cargo, a passenger, or a rider who lets go of the bars, the picture changes, and the models become less reliable. I would not trust a single number from a model that has never met a real commute. What happens when the load shifts?

Let's check the speed effect on the simulation. At walking pace, about five kilometres an hour, the simulated bike wobbles and then falls. At twenty kilometres an hour, the same model rolls on its own and recovers from a nudge. The change is not the spinning wheel. It is the speed at which steering and trail can do their job.

A useful analogy, and one I find helpful, is balancing a broom on your palm. You don't keep it up with a gyroscope in your hand. You watch the top move, and you move your hand under it. Do that for ten seconds, and you'll see the loop. Then try it with your eyes shut, and you will feel how much the body is correcting without you. Your eyes and hands run a correction loop, and a bike rider runs one too, through the bars and their own body. The bike is a broom with wheels and a trail.

Here is a no-hands ride down a quiet road, shot from two cameras, and I want you to watch the bars, not the rider. Watch the bars. They are not doing nothing. They are slowly recentring as the bike rolls, and they settle as the speed holds up. That recentring is the trail at work, and it is why many riders say a bike seems to steer itself.

The same geometry shows up everywhere, and we barely notice it because it works so well. Motorbikes have a trail, and so do many shopping carts and some office chairs. The caster wheels on those chairs follow the same design, a wheel set behind its pivot that straightens itself when it is pushed. Once you see the pattern, it is hard to unsee it in everyday objects.

Engineers tune three things on purpose: trail, wheelbase and head angle, and that is one of the most satisfying parts of the story. A trailing wheel gives a machine a self-centring tendency, and too much or too little trail changes how it handles. Push trail up and the machine calms down, and pull it down and it becomes quick but twitchy, which is a trade most designers have argued about for a century. Designers balance that trade off for every bike, depending on whether they want a stable touring frame or a quick racing one.

Some numbers help here, and we should keep them in proportion. A typical bike wheel weighs about 1.5 kilograms, and a rider might weigh 70 kilograms. The part of the tyre touching the road is about the size of a postage stamp. So the whole machine and the rider rest on a few square centimetres of rubber, and the geometry does the quiet correction in every moment. That is a small patch for a machine that can carry a rider at speed, and it helps explain why the details matter.

So here is the honest answer you were looking for. A bike stays up mainly through its geometry and your steering, with the front wheel's trail doing most of the quiet work. The spinning wheels contribute a small amount, but they are not the main story. Most people don't know that, because the bike does most of the work without asking. Does that match what you expected?

Next time you're on a bike, notice the front wheel and how the bars move as you lean. The bike is doing more thinking than you realise.
NARRATION>>>
