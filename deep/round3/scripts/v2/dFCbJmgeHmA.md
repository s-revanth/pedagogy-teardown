## Narration
<<<NARRATION
What did an ordinary afternoon look like about 66 million years ago? Picture a warm river valley in the late Cretaceous, with broad leaves moving in the wind and water running over smooth stones. Let's walk through that afternoon slowly, because the world of that time was far stranger, and far more crowded, than most of us expect. You would have seen giants everywhere you looked.

The valleys were full of plants that would look unfamiliar today. Ferns grew thick along the banks, conifers rose above them, and flowering plants were spreading into new kinds of habitat. Insects moved through the leaves, and the air was alive with the sound of things we rarely hear now. Much of the land was green, warm and wet, with few cold seasons to slow things down. If you stood on that riverbank, you would hear insects and birds without pause.

Among the plants moved the giants. Herds of duck-billed dinosaurs grazed along the river edges, moving in groups for safety. Armoured plant-eaters, some covered in bony plates, pushed slowly through the undergrowth. Their size was a defence, because few predators could take on a healthy adult. We know this from the bones and tracks that survive in the rock. Along the trails, smaller dinosaurs scurried, and the largest predators on land hunted in the same lowlands.

Most people picture dinosaurs as one great group that simply vanished. The full story is more complicated than that, and we will see why as the afternoon unfolds. Along with the big creatures, there were small birds, many lizards, and early mammals that were mostly no larger than a shrew. These small animals kept out of the way of the giants, and they waited for their own chance.

The oceans were just as busy. Marine reptiles swam in the open water, some with long necks and others with powerful jaws. Ammonites, shelled relatives of today's squid, drifted through the shallow seas, and the bottom of the food web was made of plankton that fed almost every creature in the water. How many of those sea creatures were there? We cannot count them, but the fossils suggest enormous numbers.

Then there is the sky. Above that valley, the sky was ordinary blue, with clouds drifting over the river and a faint breeze moving through the trees. Nothing in the air hinted at what was coming, and that is the point. If you had been standing there, you would have seen nothing unusual at all. The sky is the first thing we watch, because it is about to change more than once, and each change tells a different part of the story.

Then came the trigger. Somewhere far out in space, a rock about 10 kilometres across was on a path toward Earth. It struck the planet at roughly 20 kilometres per second, into the shallow sea over what is now the Yucatan Peninsula, in Mexico. That rock, the asteroid, is the cause of the day. It struck about 66 million years ago. The crater it left, the Chicxulub crater, is still buried under the land and sea floor today, and it is about 180 kilometres across.

Minute zero. The rock hits, and the flash is brighter than the sun for a fraction of a second. The ground shakes, and a shock wave races outward faster than any wind. Trees near the impact are flattened in less than a minute, and the sea surface is thrown into the air. The place where the valley once stood is now a bowl of rock and water. The energy released was roughly 100 million megatons of TNT, and the shock wave reached over 1,000 kilometres away.

In the first few minutes, a fireball expands across the sky. Rock and seawater are lifted into a huge plume, and debris climbs higher than the atmosphere reaches. Within about ten minutes, the plume begins to fall, raining glowing fragments back down across the planet. The first ten minutes decide the fate of everything close to the impact, and almost nothing near it survives. If you were near the coast, you would have had very little time to react.

Within the first hour, the falling debris reaches the surface across the world, and each fragment heats the air as it comes down. Forests catch fire in many regions, and the sky turns a deep orange. The heat pulse arrives in places thousands of kilometres away, and it may have started wildfires far beyond the reach of the blast itself. The sky that was blue at the start is now the colour of a furnace.

Within hours, waves may have reached hundreds of metres high along the coasts of what is now the Gulf of Mexico. The ground keeps shaking with earthquakes far stronger than anything humans have ever recorded. The coastline is reshaped, and the animals near the shore have little chance of escaping. The damage is already enormous, and the worst is still to come. We can see how this unfolded by reading the layers that formed in those days.

Over the following days, smoke and fine dust spread around the planet. The sunlight that once warmed the valley is blocked, and the sky becomes dark, grey and cold. The sun still rises each morning, but its light no longer reaches the ground with much strength. Night begins to feel longer, and the cold begins to settle in.

Pause here and make a guess. How long do you think the sky stayed dark enough to stop plants from growing? Pick one answer: days, months or years. Keep your guess in mind while we continue, because the answer changes how we read the rest of the story.

The answer is months, and possibly several years. Plants depend on sunlight, and without it photosynthesis slows and then stops. The bottom of the food chain, the tiny plankton in the ocean and the plants on land, collapses first, as we will see. Everything above them starts to starve, because each level depends on the one beneath it. That is why the damage spreads upward through the whole web.

The timings we use in this story are modelled, not measured minute by minute. Scientists reconstruct them from computer simulations, from rock layers, and from the spread of ash and debris across the planet. The broad sequence is well supported, but the exact minutes and hours are estimates, and they should be treated that way.

Here is the escalating ladder we will follow, and each rung adds a new layer of damage. Rung one is fire. Heat from falling debris ignites forests across the world in the first hours, and smoke adds to the darkness that follows. Rung two is dust. Soot and sulfur particles hang in the air for months and block most of the sunlight that plants need to grow. Rung two lasted months, and rung three lasted years.

Rung three is cold. Temperatures drop, and in many places they stay low for years. Rung four is the break. Roughly three quarters of all species disappear, and that includes every non-bird dinosaur. The big animals were hit hardest, because they needed the most food and could not easily hide from the cold.

Let's return to the first three rungs, because they explain who survived. Fire hit everything above ground, so the animals that could burrow or hide had a better chance. Dust cut the food supply, so small creatures that needed little food could last longer. Cold lasted for years, so animals that could store seeds or eat almost anything had another edge.

The surprising part is that birds survived. Why did they manage it? Birds are dinosaurs, and some lineages of small feathered dinosaurs kept going after the impact. They could fly away from the fire, eat seeds that lasted through the winter, and live on a wide range of foods. The survivors were not the biggest dinosaurs. They were the smallest and most adaptable ones.

It is a common mistake to picture the extinction as the end of every dinosaur. The better picture is that most dinosaur lineages ended, while the bird lineage continued. Every sparrow or pigeon you see is a living dinosaur, and that is one of the most surprising facts in this whole story. If you have ever fed pigeons in a city square, you have shared a meal with a dinosaur.

Mammals survived as well, mostly small burrowing species that could live off insects, roots and worms, and crocodiles, turtles and many kinds of fish also made it through. Plants, as we know from the fossil record, recovered in a fairly clear order, with ferns often among the first to return to the damaged land. The recovery took a long time, but it was not endless.

The rock record, which we can read, tells the same story, because a thin layer of clay, found across the world, contains a high level of iridium, a metal that is rare in Earth's crust but common in asteroids. That layer marks the boundary between the age of the dinosaurs and what came after. It is one of the clearest records of a single event in the whole geological column.

The oceans followed their own path back. Plankton returned first, because the microscopic base of the food web can multiply quickly when conditions improve, and larger animals followed in the years and centuries after, as the food chain rebuilt itself one level at a time. The sea came back to life, but it was never the same sea.

Now let's go back to the sky. Over the following years, the dust slowly fell out of the air, and the sunlight came back little by little. Plants grew again in the ash-covered land, while the oceans refilled with new kinds of life. The sky was blue again, but it belonged to a very different world.

Let's return to the question we started with. What did an ordinary afternoon look like 66 million years ago? The same valley, today, would hold birds, small mammals and flowering plants, and it would hold no giant dinosaurs at all. The river would still run, and the sky would still be blue, but the afternoon would belong to very different animals.

The most striking thing about this story, as we have seen, is the timescale. The blow itself took minutes, the fire took hours, the dust took months, and the cold took years. The recovery took millions of years, and the afternoon that began as ordinary ended in a single day. The day the dinosaurs died is still written in the rock, and it is still there to be read.
