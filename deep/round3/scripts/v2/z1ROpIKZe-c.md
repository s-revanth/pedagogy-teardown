## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Most cities got more democratic over the last century. This one didn't. That's the whole story in the title, and it's a good story, but it depends on what more democratic means, so let's start there.

Think of a city's charter as the house rules. It's the written document that says who runs the place, how they get the job, and how the rules themselves can be changed. Most of the time nobody reads it. But when the house rules change, everything in the house changes with them.

So what does democratic mean for a city? There are two common parses, and they point in slightly different directions. The first is who gets to vote. Did more residents gain the right to cast ballots, and did the franchise widen over time? The second is who holds the power. Did elected bodies gain authority, while appointed boards and unelected officials lost it?

Most cities moved on both counts, though not always at the same speed. Over the decades, the franchise widened, with more residents getting the vote and fewer barriers in the way. Many cities also moved from appointed bodies toward elected councils, with mayors and council members chosen by the people they serve.

That's the trend. Now here's the part that matters for the title. The trend isn't a law of nature. It happened because cities changed their charters, one at a time, and most of them did.

Those changes often came from state law. A state legislature could require cities to hold elections a certain way, or could set rules about how councils are structured. Many cities were pushed, sometimes reluctantly, toward a shared model. The spread was partly persuasion and partly requirement.

Now, the city in the title. It didn't follow the same path. Its governing structure stayed largely as it was, with power sitting with an appointed body rather than with elected representatives. The vote itself may have widened, but the people who ran the city's day-to-day decisions were not chosen the way most other cities' leaders were chosen.

That's the first parse at work. A city can grow its electorate and still keep power where it was. The vote and the decision are two different things, and the holdout kept the second one out of reach.

You can check this in the city's own records, and you'll find the same pattern again and again. Council minutes, election calendars and the charter text tell you who votes and who decides. When those line up, the two parses agree. In the holdout, they didn't. Over several decades, the vote widened while the charter's decision rules barely moved. That gap is the whole puzzle in one sentence.

So why didn't it change? Here's a guess to make before I explain. Pause for a second. Was it a vote of the public that blocked the change, a court that struck it down, or a rule written so that nobody could easily amend it? Make your call.

Okay. The most likely answer, from what the record suggests, is the third. The city's charter had an amendment process that required a supermajority of voters, plus approval at a higher level of government. Each step was a veto point. Each veto point was a place where the change could die quietly, without anyone deciding to kill it.

That's the cause, and it's worth naming, because the same cause shows up again and again. A rulebook that's hard to amend tends to stay the same, long after the people it was written for have gone. The rule outlives its authors. And the rule decides who gets to decide.

Here's the same cause at a second scale. Think about a neighborhood association with bylaws that require unanimous consent to change anything. Years later, the membership has changed completely, but the bylaws still say unanimity, and nothing new can happen. The rulebook is doing the work, not the people. The city's charter does the same job on a bigger stage.

Now, I want to be careful, because measuring democracy is contestable. Counting votes is easy. Counting power is not. If you tried to score every city from one to ten, you'd spend most of your time arguing about the weights and very little time counting anything. Which bodies really decide things? Informal networks matter too, and they are hard to count. How would you weigh a wider electorate against a narrower council? Ask ten people and you'll get ten answers. Reasonable people will disagree about the scoring, and the scoring is where most of the arguments happen.

So the honest version of the title is this. Most cities widened who votes and moved power toward elected bodies. This one didn't, on at least one of those measures, and the reason seems to be a rule that was hard to change. Whether you call the city less democratic depends on which parse you use.

Think about how often you've signed something you never read, because the form said that's how it's done. Most of us have. A charter works the same way, except it decides who gets to change the signatures.

Let's bring the house rules back. A house can get a new roof, a new kitchen, and a new family, and still be run by the same rulebook from a century ago. If the rulebook says a change needs everyone's signature, the house stays as it is.

Here's the broader lesson. The question for any institution is not only whether it's fair today. It's whether the rules for changing it are fair, and whether the people living under them can actually change them. A city where the rules can't change is a city where the past keeps a vote on the future.

That's also why the holdout matters. It's not an exception that breaks the trend. It's a test of the trend. If most cities changed because their charters allowed change, then the one that didn't is showing us what happens when the charter doesn't.

So, most cities got more democratic. This one didn't. The difference wasn't mainly a lack of votes. It was a rulebook that made changing the rules harder than leaving them alone. And that's a reminder that the rules about rules are often the most important ones, even when nobody reads them.
NARRATION>>>
