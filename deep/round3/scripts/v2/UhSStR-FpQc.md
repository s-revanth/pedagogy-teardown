<<<NARRATION
Where did the first copy come from? That's the question this video is built around, and it's an old one. Here's the answer up front, and then we'll be honest about what it leaves open. Copying mistakes are the engine of change. Every bit of variety we see in the living world starts with a copy that came out a little different. What we don't know yet is where the very first copy came from, and we'll say so every time we get close to it.

Let's set up the plane. Our blob replicators live here, and each one splits at a fixed rate. When a replicator splits, it makes a copy of its own pattern, and the copy goes on living. That's the whole setup, and it's the same blob world we've been using. Keep an eye on the typo copy, because it's going to be the anchor for the rest of the video.

First, a minimal definition, because the word replicator gets used loosely. A replicator is a pattern that copies itself. It doesn't have to be alive. It just has to make another pattern like itself, and that new pattern has to be able to make another one. A rock doesn't copy itself, so a rock isn't a replicator, even if it lasts a very long time. Copying is what matters here, not survival alone.

Let's start with the dumbest version. Every copy comes out perfect, identical to the one that made it. Run that for a while. The population grows, the plane fills, and the blobs all look exactly alike. Nothing changes. Nothing new appears. That's a clean baseline, and it's useful exactly because it does nothing interesting. Without mistakes, there's no story at all.

Watch the population climb, the plane crowd, and every blob look exactly like every other. The run tells us what copying alone can do, and the answer is: fill the plane with copies of one thing.

Now let's allow mistakes. Suppose one copy in every hundred comes out with a typo. The typo changes the pattern a little, so the copy isn't a perfect duplicate anymore. Most typos are harmless, or slightly bad, but occasionally one comes out copying faster than its parent. Watch what happens when that faster copier appears. It starts to take over the plane.

I'll admit something about this part. When I first thought about copying mistakes, I assumed a typo could only damage the line. Each error would make the copy a little worse, and the errors would pile up until the line died out. That's a reasonable first guess, and it's wrong. The run shows that the errors also produce the occasional copy that works better, and that copy spreads. The error becomes a feature only because the copy works.

So let's be careful about a misconception we almost slipped into. A mutation is not just damage. It's a difference in the copy, and most differences are neutral or bad. But some differences make the copy better at copying, and in that case the difference spreads. The faster copier isn't a mistake that got rescued. It's a mistake that happened to work, and it works because of the rule, not because anyone intended it.

Now, the open part. Where did the first replicator come from? Here's how we can talk about it honestly. There are several candidate settings, including warm chemistry in shallow pools, and mineral surfaces that might have held molecules together long enough to copy them. Those are guesses with different levels of support. We can say which is more plausible, but we can't say which one actually happened. So each candidate carries its own label. Some are reasonable, some are speculative, and none of them is settled.

Here's a prediction, before we run the next step. Two copies are in front of us. One is the typo copy, which copies a little faster. The other is the original, which copies at the old rate. Keep the typo, or keep the original? Pick one and hold on to it. Think about what the plane will do over many rounds, and which one wins the room. Make your choice now, before the answer arrives.

Okay, let's run it. The answer is keep the typo. The faster copier spreads through the plane, and the original slowly disappears. Within a few dozen rounds, almost every blob is a descendant of the typo. If you picked the original, you were thinking about the copy that worked the first time, which is a reasonable instinct. But the rule doesn't care which copy you like. It cares which copy copies more.

Let's look at what the plane is doing now. Copies compete for food, and the plane has a limit, so the population can't grow forever. The faster copier still wins, but as the plane fills, the margin shrinks, and the blobs start to crowd each other at every pair of food. The competition is what keeps the number in check, and it's also what makes the faster copier's advantage matter. Without a limit, the advantage would only show up as a bigger count.

Here's the rule written out in words. A copy that copies faster leaves more copies behind. Those copies leave more copies of their own. Over enough rounds, the faster pattern dominates the population, and nothing else needs to be added. No plan, no goal, and no one in charge. The rule is just copy, vary a little, and let the population sort the results.

Before we add anything else, here's a checkpoint. Check that the copy rule still holds in what you just watched. The faster copier should leave more copies than the original, round after round. If it doesn't, the crowding is doing something we didn't account for, and we should go back to the setup before we go on. A rule that works here will carry into the next step, so make sure it's right.

Now let's generalize. The same logic shows up well beyond blobs. Genes are copied in each generation, and the copies carry small changes. Words get copied, misspelled, and reshaped as they pass between people. Scripts get copied by hand, and the errors pile up in ways that sometimes improve them. In each case, a little variety plus a selection rule gives change over time. That's a pattern to watch for, and not a proof of anything about the first copy.

So here's the payoff, and it's a modest one. Variation and selection give change. That part is observed in the run, and it's also observed in real populations, although real ones are much messier. What the run can't tell us is where the first pattern came from. The plane begins with a copy already in it. The question of how that first copy appeared is outside the model.

Which copy came first? We don't know yet. We have candidate settings and reasons to prefer some of them, and each one still has to earn its place. The honest answer is that the first copy is an open question, and it may stay open for a long time. What we can say is that once a copy exists, the rule of copying with small mistakes is enough to start the story.
