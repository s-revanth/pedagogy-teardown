<<<NARRATION
Michael here. What was the first photo ever put on the internet? Take a guess, and then keep watching, because the answer is a little messier than the question. You may already be picturing something famous, so let's check.

Let's start with the web itself. In 1991, Tim Berners-Lee put the first website online at CERN, the physics lab near Geneva. It was text. There were no pictures and no colours, just words and links that you could click to jump to other pages. If you had visited it, you would have seen plain text on a plain screen. We would call that a very quiet internet today. There was no search box, no feed and no images to scroll past. Yet the basic idea, a page that links to another page, is exactly what we still use every day.

So where do pictures come in? The next year, in 1992, a group photo of women working at CERN was put online. It is often cited as the first photo posted to the web. By today's standards it is small and grainy, and it is a little strange to look at. Still, it is the kind of picture that made the web feel like a place for people, not only for documents. I find that shift more interesting than the photo itself.

Here is where the word first gets slippery. It depends on which network you mean, which format and which definition you choose. Other groups shared pictures on earlier systems, and a different image may have appeared on a bulletin board before the web existed. So the claim is an honest guess based on the best records, not a settled fact. Historians do not fully agree, and I think it is worth saying that out loud.

Let's look at some other firsts. The first email was sent in 1971 by Ray Tomlinson, an engineer who was working on networked computers. He needed a way to separate a person's name from the machine they used. He picked the at sign, which is why every email address has one. That small choice is still everywhere you look.

Then there is the first tweet. In March 2006, Jack Dorsey posted a plain test message about setting up his account. Nothing about it looked historic when it was sent. Its importance came later, once millions of people had already decided the platform mattered. Very few people noticed it at the time, and we can understand why. A plain message about a new account does not look like history until history arrives.

Now here is a question for you. If you could pick the first thing that should have been online, what would it be? Think about what you would want to see as the very first piece of the web, and why you would choose it.

I think the most interesting thing about these firsts is how little they announce themselves. Nobody puts a sign on the first useful thing. The sign goes up later, when people look back and find the moment that started everything. The first website had no pictures. The first photo was a group snapshot. The first email was a practical test. The first tweet was a joke about setting up an account.

So what was the first photo on the internet? The usual answer is a group snapshot from CERN, depending on your definition, and we can read it as a workplace photo rather than a picture made for the web. And the first website was text, with links. Those small, practical acts are how the internet was built, one ordinary moment at a time.

Thanks for watching.
