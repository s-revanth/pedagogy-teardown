## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Quick word from our sponsor first. It's fifteen seconds, it's totally fine, and I promise we'll be back in Vienna before you get bored, because Vienna is where this whole story gets weird and stays weird for a very long time.

Vienna, 1907. A young man walks into the Academy of Fine Arts with a folder of his drawings and an enormous amount of confidence. He's sure they'll be thrilled. They are not thrilled. The art school said no, and he took it personally.

So what does a rejected painter do next? Try again, obviously. He applies the next year, and the academy says no again. Two rejections, zero buyers, and a lot of very long afternoons. He lives in a men's hostel and sells postcards and little watercolours to tourists, telling anyone who'll listen that he's an artist who just hasn't been discovered yet. Not exactly a career. More like a waiting room with a sketchbook.

Have you ever been that guy? The one who's completely sure the world is just slow to notice him? I have, a little, and I'd argue most of us have, and I can tell you that the world does not care about your sketchbook. But he didn't take the hint. He took the grievance, folded it up carefully, and kept it in his coat pocket for the next twenty years.

By 1913 he's packed up and moved to Munich, where he can wait in a different waiting room. Munich suits him. There are beer halls, there are cafes, and there are plenty of people who will listen to a loud young man talk about art. He's still painting, still selling, still telling people about his genius. You get the picture: a man in his twenties who is extremely confident and extremely broke. Then 1914 happens, and suddenly the waiting room has a door.

He enlists in a Bavarian regiment, and the army does something the art school never did. It says yes. He ends up as a dispatch runner, carrying messages between the front line and headquarters, often under fire, which is a dangerous job and a brave one. He picks up medals, including an Iron Cross. He writes letters about how much he loves the front. For a guy who's been told no his whole life, this is the first group that seems to like him. Would you want to be liked by the army? Think about what that does to a man like this.

Four years of war. Then 1918. Germany is losing, the front is collapsing, and in the autumn he's caught in a gas attack that leaves him temporarily blind. When the armistice is signed in November, he's in a hospital bed, and he is furious. The art school said no, and he took it personally. Now a whole country had said no, and he was taking that personally too.

Here's the key bit, and we need to slow down for it. The generals who ran the war knew it was lost, and they did not want the blame. So a story gets passed around. The army was never really beaten, the story goes. It was stabbed in the back by traitors at home. That's the stab-in-the-back myth. Sound familiar? You've probably heard its cousins in every argument about who really lost something.

Now, stab-in-the-back is not the same thing as losing a war. Losing a war is a military fact. Stab-in-the-back is a story that moves the loss onto someone else. I want you to notice that, because it's the whole trick. And it works, because it has villains in it, and a grievance loves a villain. Everybody wants someone to blame when things go wrong, and this story hands you a list of names.

In 1919 he joins a tiny party in Munich, the German Workers' Party. Picture a few men in a back room, arguing over beer. Would you have bought that pitch? He turns out to be really good at speeches, which nobody expected, and he figures out the party needs a bigger stage. His membership number is in the mid five hundreds. By 1920 the party has a new name, and he's its most effective speaker. Who knew? Not the art school.

By 1923 he's decided Munich is where the revolution starts. So he and his allies try to seize power in Bavaria and march on Berlin. It's called the Beer Hall Putsch, and it falls apart within hours. The police open fire. People die. And Hitler is arrested. Why did it fail so fast? Because a coup needs the army to come along, and the army did not. I'll be honest, that's a very familiar kind of failure. Plans that depend on everyone else cooperating tend to end this way.

Let's look at the trial, because it's a strange one. The courtroom turns into a stage, and he uses it like one. He gets five years in prison. He serves under one. And in that cell he starts a book.

Okay. I'll say it plainly. This is the part where the jokes stop. The man who failed the art school exam now has an audience, a party and a book. The book is full of ideas about race and enemies, and the antisemitism in it isn't a side note. It's the plan. Almost everything that comes next grows out of it, and I'm not going to make that funny.

Now, a caveat, because it matters. A lot of what we know about these early years comes from his own later accounts, and historians check those very carefully. So when the colourful details show up, you should treat them as colourful until they're confirmed. Nobody should pretend the paperwork is complete, and you deserve better than a tidy story.

But the basic arc is solid. Rejected. Resentful. Convinced the world owes him a stage. Then given one, with a lot of help from people who wanted someone to blame.

The art school said no, and he took it personally. This time the joke doesn't land, because of what came next. And for a long time after that, nobody told him no.
NARRATION>>>
