## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Here is a picture most of us carry around. Astronauts float on the space station because gravity is gone up there, and the higher you go, the weaker it gets until it switches off. It is a neat story, and it is wrong in an interesting way. So let me give you the answer right away, so you know where we are heading. I'd bet the answer surprises most people, and I'd like you to see why.

Gravity at the station is about nine tenths as strong as it is on the ground. The astronauts float because they are in free fall, falling around the Earth so fast that the floor keeps missing them. Newton's rule is still good enough for most jobs, and that is the catch. The rest of this video is about why the everyday picture works so well, and where it finally breaks.

Let's see it with something you can hold. Picture a bowling ball on a stretched sheet. The ball dents the sheet, and a marble rolling nearby curves toward it. That is a cartoon of gravity, and I'll be honest about its limits, but it gives you a picture to work with. I'd like you to keep that in mind. The ball is the object we will keep coming back to, so keep it in view.

Now picture the space station. It sits about 400 kilometres up, and the pull there is still close to nine tenths of what you feel on the ground. The station is also moving sideways at about 7.7 kilometres every second. Fall toward the Earth, and that sideways speed keeps you missing the ground, so you keep falling around it, forever. We will return to that sideways speed in a moment, and we will need it.

How strong is that pull, exactly? Newton wrote it down in 1687 as a simple rule. We should give him credit for the idea, and we should be honest that it was a remarkable piece of work. The force between two masses is proportional to their product, and it falls off with the square of the distance between them. In symbols, F equals G times m one times m two, over r squared. For the station, m one is the Earth, m two is the station, and r is the distance between their centres.

Let me say what each piece does: F is the force you feel pulling the two bodies together. The masses m one and m two are the things pulling on each other, and r is how far apart their centres are. G is a constant that sets the strength of the whole interaction. You don't need calculus to use the rule. You only need to know that doubling the distance cuts the pull to a quarter. I find that rule almost unreasonably simple for how much it explains.

So why does the station feel weightless? The answer is that the astronauts and the station fall together. Nothing pushes them against the floor, so there is no sensation of weight. Why would falling feel like floating? Because your inner ear and your skin only register forces that push on you, and in free fall there is nothing pushing.

This is the everyday mistake, and it is worth stating plainly: weightlessness means no support force, not no gravity. Gravity is still there, pulling on the astronauts with most of the force they would feel at home. What is missing is the floor pushing back. That single difference is why the station seems empty of gravity when it is full of it. I'd guess most of us have felt that difference in a fast lift, even if we never named it.

Back to the ball on the sheet. If you rolled a second ball beside the first, the dent would guide both of them, and neither would pull against the other in a way you could feel. That is the picture of falling along a shape, not against a force. It is a helpful cartoon, and it is the reason Einstein's idea became so compelling. We can build on that intuition, but we should not confuse it with the full theory.

Newton's rule worked astonishingly well for two centuries. Then, in 1859, the astronomer Urbain Le Verrier noticed that Mercury's orbit drifts forward by a small extra amount every century, about 43 arcseconds. Newton's planets could not explain that last piece. Le Verrier even proposed an unseen planet to account for it, and that planet was never found. The anomaly stayed.

Then came Einstein. In 1915, he proposed that gravity is the geometry of spacetime, and that matter tells space how to curve. The idea made a bold prediction: starlight passing near the Sun should bend more than Newton's rule allowed. Four years later, in 1919, Arthur Eddington led an eclipse expedition to test it, and the measured bending matched Einstein's value. I'd call that one of the great moments in science.

I should be careful here, because the bending was small. It was about one and three-quarter arcseconds, which is about the angle of a coin seen from several kilometres away. That is tiny, but it was enough. Eddington's result did not prove Einstein's whole theory, though. It showed that the newer picture made a prediction that the older one did not.

Einstein's theory is not a cartoon with a heavier ball in it. It describes spacetime itself, and the curvature happens in four dimensions, not on a rubber sheet. The sheet is a useful picture, and I'd say it is the right first picture to hold. But it is an approximation, and I'd be wary of pushing it too far.

Before we go further, here is a number to guess. How many times weaker is gravity than the electric force between two protons? Pick an order of magnitude. Ten? A thousand? A billion? Be honest with yourself about your guess, because the answer is much larger than most people expect.

The answer is roughly ten to the thirty-six. That is a trillion trillion trillion times weaker. It is an absurd magnitude, and it explains why gravity only feels strong at the scale of a planet. Inside your body, the electric forces between atoms are enormous, and they cancel out. What is left for you to feel is the gravity of the whole Earth, which is a very big pull spread across a very big mass.

Look at a single atom and gravity is almost invisible. I find that shift in scale fascinating. Look at a mountain and it becomes obvious. That shift in scale is why our everyday intuition gets it wrong. We never meet gravity alone, only in combination with forces that are far stronger and point in every direction.

Here is the most practical rung of the ladder. GPS satellites carry clocks that run faster than clocks on the ground, because they are higher up and moving. Relativity says both effects matter, and the net shift is about 38 microseconds a day. Without a correction, your position would drift by kilometres within a single day. The navigation in your phone depends on a theory that replaced Newton's.

So the picture is not just a historical curiosity. It is the difference between a map that works and a map that wanders. Engineers who build satellites know exactly which correction applies where, and I'd want the same care in any field, and so should you. They check it against measurement, because a clock error of a few microseconds is a position error of hundreds of metres.

Newton's rule still works for most of the things you will ever calculate. It breaks down near very strong gravity, such as the edge of a black hole, and in the extreme conditions of the early universe. Einstein's theory covers those cases, but it adds a level of complexity that engineers rarely need for everyday work. Use the simplest model that works, and check it against the cases where it might fail.

The same reasoning applies across science. Models are tools, and each one has a range where it works. A tide table is accurate for a harbour and useless for a black hole. What matters is knowing which regime you are in, and that is a skill you can practise with any model, not just the ones about gravity.

Gravity's effects matter well beyond physics classrooms. Satellites, time networks, deep-space navigation and the timing that keeps computers synchronised around the world all depend on getting those effects right. Each of them uses a model with a stated range, and each of them is checked against measurement, which is the only honest way to trust any of it.

So the thesis still stands. Gravity is not gone at the station. The astronauts are not outside its reach. The station is falling along with the people inside it, and that is why nothing presses on them. The everyday picture is a good approximation, and the exceptions teach you what the approximation is made of.

Let's return to the ball on the sheet one more time, and here is a second ball falling beside the first, and both follow the same dent. Neither is pushed against the other, and neither is held up by anything. They are simply following the shape of the space around them, and that is the picture that holds from the classroom to the satellite.

Choose the simplest model that works, and check it where it might fail. That is the whole method, and it applies to gravity as much as to anything else you will try to understand.

The astronauts were never out of reach of gravity. They were falling along with it, and the station was falling too, just sideways enough to keep missing the ground.
