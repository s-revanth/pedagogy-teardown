## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Some of the hundreds of billions of stars in our galaxy will one day end as the strangest objects we know. Every one of them begins as something completely ordinary, a bright point fusing hydrogen in its core. A star like that can burn steadily for millions or even billions of years, and that long calm is only the first stage of its story. Let's follow one star from its first spark to its very last flicker.

A star is a tug of war. Heat from nuclear fusion pushes outward, while gravity pulls everything inward, and the two forces balance each other almost perfectly. As long as the fuel lasts, neither side wins. The star stays stable. Its brightness barely changes for most of its life, and that is why we can see so many of them when we look up. You can picture the balance as a balloon pressed from every side, with the air inside pushing back.

Eventually the fuel runs low. The core stops making enough heat to push back, and gravity starts to win. For a star much heavier than the Sun, the collapse is violent. The core is crushed into a space so small and dense that nothing we know of can resist it. A star roughly 20 times heavier than the Sun may end this way. Lighter stars like the Sun shrink quietly into much smaller remnants called white dwarfs. If you watched from far away, the two endings could look surprisingly alike. Let's pause on that leftover core, because it is about to become something very strange.

That leftover core is a black hole. Its gravity is so strong that anything closer than a certain boundary cannot escape, not even light. That boundary is called the event horizon, and it is a true point of no return. If you could ride a probe across that line, nothing special would happen at the boundary itself, which is one of the odd facts about black holes. Nothing crosses it and comes back, so from the outside we only ever see the black hole through its effect on the matter around it.

Once it forms, a black hole does not simply go quiet. Its surrounding gas and dust can swirl into a flat disk, heat up, and glow brightly before the material falls across the boundary. In this stage the black hole is often the brightest thing in its neighborhood, even though its interior stays completely hidden from view. We can often detect a black hole by its glow, even though the hole itself stays dark, so you can find it without ever seeing it.

What happens inside the event horizon is one of the places where our knowledge runs out. Our best theories describe the outside well. The inside would need a theory that combines gravity with quantum physics, and no such theory is accepted yet. So any picture of the interior is a model, and it should be labelled that way every time.

Here is the death part of the story. Before you read on, pause and make a guess. Can a black hole lose mass at all, when nothing can escape from it? Take a moment and choose yes or no. In 1974, Stephen Hawking showed that black holes should slowly leak energy, a process now called Hawking radiation. The answer is yes, because quantum effects at the edge let energy escape. The effect is extremely faint, so faint that it has no measurable impact on the black holes we can study today.

For a black hole with the mass of the Sun, the process would take roughly 10 to the power of 67 years. That is a one followed by sixty-seven zeros. The universe is about 13.8 billion years old. That means it has barely started compared with the time a black hole needs to die. Such a black hole would be only about six kilometres across. Even a black hole a thousand times heavier than the Sun would need far longer than that to evaporate. So you can see how slow the death really is.

As the black hole shrinks, it gets hotter, and it radiates faster. Near the very end, it would give off a final burst that we can barely imagine. So does that burst flash, or does it fade quietly? Theory is not settled on the final details, and that is where the question stays open. Maybe the answer depends on details that we have never been able to test.

It is worth saying clearly that nobody has seen Hawking radiation from a black hole. We have seen black holes through their effects on nearby stars and gas. We have also seen gravitational waves from black holes colliding, which is strong evidence that they exist. But the evaporation itself is still a prediction, and we should treat it as one. The first detection of gravitational waves from colliding black holes came in 2015, which is why their existence is no longer in serious doubt.

So the birth of a black hole is well understood, the middle stage is observed, and the death is still a prediction waiting for better evidence. From birth to death, a black hole's life is measured in stages that stretch far beyond anything else we can name. There are three stages in this story, birth, a long middle and death, and the last one is the longest by far. If you remember only one thing, remember that the death stage is a prediction, and a serious one.
