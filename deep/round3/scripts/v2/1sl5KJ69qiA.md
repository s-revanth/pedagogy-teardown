<<<NARRATION
Michael here. Imagine you are a doctor in the middle of a crisis. There is one ventilator left in the hospital, and two patients need it right now. One is older and has a somewhat better chance of recovering. The other is younger but in worse shape. You have minutes to decide. What do you do, and who should be making that call?

If you were that doctor, what would you want the rule to be? Would you want it to depend on age, on the chance of survival, or on who arrived first? Each answer carries a different picture of what matters, and I think it is worth noticing which one you reach for first.

I want to start there, because this is not a puzzle from a textbook. Decisions like this happen in real hospitals, in real emergencies, and they are made by people under enormous pressure. Before we get to the famous thought experiment behind it, I want to ask a simpler question. When is it acceptable to cause harm in order to prevent a bigger harm? Let's look at it from several angles, starting with the idea that makes it so hard to answer.

Most people have an instinct about the difference between doing harm and allowing harm. If an action directly hurts a person, it feels worse than something that lets harm happen through events nobody caused. Philosophers call this the distinction between acts and omissions. I find it useful to think of the doctor's choice as an act, and letting a condition run its course as an omission. But notice that not acting is not automatically safe. Letting a patient go is also a choice, and it has consequences you are still responsible for.

Here is where things got sharper. In 1967, the philosopher Philippa Foot wrote a paper about abortion and a principle called the doctrine of double effect. The doctrine says that a harm may be permitted if it is an unintended side effect of doing good, but not if it is the means to that good. Foot wanted a clean test case, so she imagined a runaway tram, a driver who cannot stop, and a choice about where to steer it. That tram became a seed for a debate that is still going. I find Foot's move clever, because it treats the moral question as a question about structure, not only about outcomes. You can see the same structure in medicine. Giving a drug that has a dangerous side effect is different from giving a drug in order to cause harm.

Let's name the object in the title now, because we have arrived at it. The trolley problem is a scenario. A runaway trolley is heading down a track toward five workers who cannot get out of the way. You are standing beside a lever. If you pull it, the trolley switches to a side track, where it will kill one worker instead. Do you pull the lever? Most people say yes, and most can explain why in a sentence. Five lives are more than one.

Here is where I want you to pause. Stop the video and decide. Pull the lever, or do nothing? Write it down, then keep watching. Did you pull? If you did, you are in the company of most people who answer this question. If you did not, you are in very large company too. Either way, you have just made the choice the scenario was built to test.

Now a second version, from the philosopher Judith Jarvis Thomson. This time the trolley is still running toward five people. You are on a footbridge above the track. Standing next to you is a very large man. If you push him over the rail, his body will stop the trolley, and the five will live. It is the same arithmetic, one death for five. But when people hear this version, far fewer say they would push. The numbers stayed the same while the answer changed. I would guess that you feel a pull in two directions when you hear the second version. That discomfort is the point. Your intuitions are telling you something, even if they cannot yet say what it is.

Notice what that shows. The outcome is identical, but the act is different. In the lever case, the death is a side effect of redirecting the trolley. In the footbridge case, the death is the means by which the trolley is stopped. Thomson made that difference uncomfortably clear, and it has been argued over ever since.

Then came the brain scans. In 2001, the neuroscientist Joshua Greene and his colleagues put volunteers in an fMRI scanner and gave them dilemmas like these. The personal dilemmas, such as pushing someone, seemed to engage brain areas linked to emotion more strongly than the impersonal ones, such as pulling a switch. That was a striking result. It suggested that some of our moral judgements come from a fast emotional response, and others from slower reasoning.

I want to be careful here. A brain scan shows correlation. It shows which areas get busier during a task. It does not tell us which answer is morally right. Finding an emotion in the scanner does not prove the emotion is wrong, and it does not prove the emotion is reliable either. A lot of people have overread these studies, and I think that is worth saying out loud. When I first read about them, I assumed the scanner would settle the question. It did not, and that is the honest lesson. The brain is busy in both cases, and busy does not mean right.

So where do the real choices show up? Look at the hospital again. Triage teams must decide who gets scarce treatment. Engineers building self-driving cars must write code for crashes that cannot be avoided. Those decisions are not one-off thought experiments. They are policies, applied to thousands of cases, and they carry the same tension between doing harm and allowing harm. Think about the car. If you were designing it, would you want it to swerve to save the most lives, or to protect the person inside? You may find that your answer changes depending on whether you are the passenger or the pedestrian.

That brings us to the largest version of the experiment, which was run online. In 2018, researchers at MIT published the Moral Machine study, which asked people to judge driverless-car crash scenarios. Millions of people from around the world took part, and the answers varied by country and culture. Some groups leaned toward sparing the young over the old, others toward sparing the many over the few, and others toward following the law more closely. These were patterns in preferences, not answers about what is correct.

That is a caveat we have to take seriously. Online surveys are not random samples of the planet. People who choose to answer a quiz about driverless cars are a particular group. And preferences are not proof. A large majority might prefer an answer that still turns out to be wrong. Popularity is a clue to human intuition, not a verdict on ethics. I remember being struck by how the results differ between groups of people who all read the same question. Your culture, your age and your experience with traffic may all shape the answer you give, which makes the study a mirror as much as a map.

Now we reach the wall. Philosophers have spent more than half a century on these cases, and they still disagree. Some argue that outcomes are what count, so five lives beat one, full stop. Others argue that the rules we follow matter too, such as never using a person merely as a tool. No experiment, scan or survey settles that disagreement by itself, because each one still depends on a choice about what morality is for. If you ask me which view is right, I will admit that I lean toward thinking each view picks up something real. Outcomes matter enormously, and so do the lines we refuse to cross. Most of the difficulty lies in holding both at once.

Here is the point I think matters most. You do not need to resolve the whole debate to act well. But you do need to decide in advance what kind of rule you are willing to live by. A hospital that writes a triage policy is making a choice about who counts and why. A car company that writes crash code is making one too. The trolley is not a single moment on a track. It is a rule that someone must write before the emergency arrives.

You can test your own rules with a simple habit. Pick a case, write down your answer, then change one detail, such as who is on the bridge. If your answer flips, ask yourself why. That question is more useful than any single answer you give.

So go back to the doctor with one ventilator. The clear answer is not a formula. It is a set of values written down, explained, reviewed and open to challenge. The trolley problem keeps coming back because it asks who gets to decide, and when. Those are questions about people, not only about levers.

Maybe the strangest thing about these cases is that the same person can answer differently in two versions with identical numbers. That tells us something about us. The feeling of right and wrong is not a calculator. It is a messy human process, and we can shape it, but only if we pay attention to it, and to each other.

Thanks for watching.
