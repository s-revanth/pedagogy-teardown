## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Milk is white, and for most of history it has been the first food of every young mammal, which gives it a strong reputation. Yet we hear two confident verdicts about it, one calling it a healthy drink and the other calling it a white poison. So which one is right? Let's take the question apart, piece by piece.

Start with what milk is. It is a liquid that mammal mothers make to feed their young, and about 87 percent of cow's milk is simply water. The rest is fat, protein, sugar and minerals, mixed together so that a newborn calf can grow fast. That combination makes milk one of the most nutrient dense liquids in nature.

Now let's look at one cup, about 240 millilitres of whole cow's milk. It carries roughly 8 grams of protein and around 300 milligrams of calcium, which is a useful share of daily need. It also holds about 12 grams of sugar, in the form of a type called lactose. Vitamin D and vitamin B12 are often added on purpose.

So where does the poison idea come from? Partly from a label. The phrase white poison has long been attached to refined sugar and white flour, foods that deliver a lot of energy with very few nutrients. Milk got swept into the same phrase. It is white and sweet, and some people's guts react badly to it. But a label is not a test, and the real question is what milk does inside a body, at a given amount.

The sugar is where the story gets interesting, because lactose needs its own enzyme. A baby mammal makes lactase, which breaks lactose into two simpler sugars that the gut can absorb. Most mammals make far less of it after weaning, and most adult humans do too. Roughly two thirds of adults worldwide now have reduced lactase activity, which means that for them the sugar arrives in the gut largely unprocessed.

In some human populations, though, a gene that keeps lactase switched on became common. Those were the populations that herded animals and drank their milk into adulthood. Over the last 8,000 years or so, that version spread across parts of Europe, Africa and Asia, and in parts of northern Europe more than 90 percent of adults still keep it. So the gut you have depends partly on your family history, which is why one person can drink a litre without trouble while another gets cramps from a small glass.

That is the first clue, and the second clue is the amount. A large glass can cause bloating, gas and diarrhea in someone with low lactase, and that is uncomfortable, but it is not poison. It is a mismatch between a gut and a sugar. The fix is usually simple, because you can drink less, switch to lactose-free milk, or choose yogurt and hard cheese, which contain far less lactose than a glass of milk does.

So let's sort the people into rungs, because each rung has a different answer. Rung one is adults who keep lactase, and for them milk is a cheap source of protein, calcium and vitamins. Rung two is adults who lost most of their lactase, and many of them can handle small amounts or lactose-free milk without trouble. Rung three is the two or three infants in every hundred who have a cow's milk allergy, an immune reaction to milk protein rather than to sugar, which can be serious enough to need a doctor. Rung four is anyone with a medical reason to avoid milk altogether, and for them the safe answer is a different source.

Let's look at those four rungs together. Milk is not one thing for everyone. A verdict that fits rung one can be harmful on rung three. Every serious answer has to name the rung first, which is why a single yes or no tends to mislead us.

Now let's look at the research. Most of the evidence on milk comes from observational studies, in which researchers follow large groups of people for years, record what they drink, and watch what happens to their health. These studies are useful, and they are also limited, because people who drink more milk often differ in income, exercise, smoking and diet. Researchers adjust for those differences, but never fully remove them, so the study shows a pattern, not proof.

The pattern itself is mixed. Some studies link milk to stronger bones and fewer fractures, while others find no clear fracture benefit at all. Some link high intake to a slightly higher risk of prostate cancer, and others find that full-fat dairy has little effect on heart disease over the long run. Results depend on the population, the study design and how carefully the amounts were measured. Our honest summary is that the signals disagree, and most of the effects that do show up are probably small.

That uncertainty is real, but it does not hide a clear fact. The body does not handle milk the way it handles a true poison, because poisons work at small doses and harm the body whatever else you eat with them. When we eat milk at normal amounts, inside a normal diet, it feeds the body instead of attacking it. Any harm that does come from milk probably follows the dose and the person, which is exactly what a poison does not do.

So here is your bet, and it is worth making before you read the next line. Is milk a white poison, or is it a healthy drink for you? Take a moment and decide.

Here is what most nutrition researchers would say. For an adult who digests lactose, milk is a healthy drink in normal amounts,. For someone with lactose intolerance, milk is a drink with side effects rather than a danger, and the fix is about amount and form. For someone with a true milk allergy, milk can be dangerous, and that question belongs to a doctor. For almost nobody is it a poison in the sense the label suggests.

This is the same rule that applies to salt, coffee and water, and that is the lesson worth keeping. Too much of anything can harm you, and the dose decides almost everything. Poison is a word about dose, not a word about food. Water can kill if you drink enough of it fast enough, yet nobody calls water poison. Milk does not work like that, and neither does most food.

Notice the trick in the label. A food described as natural is not automatically safe, and a food described as white is not automatically dangerous. Labels are shortcuts, and they save you the work of thinking, but they are wrong often enough to matter. If we want to judge milk properly, we need the amount, the body and the rung, which a label can never give us.

Diets without much milk can still work well, and we should be clear about the alternatives. Leafy greens, tofu made with calcium, canned fish with soft bones and fortified plant milks all carry calcium as well. Milk is one route among several, which is why the bone question is so hard to settle with a single food.

What should we do with all this? If milk sits well with your stomach, the evidence does not say to stop. If you get cramps, try lactose-free milk, or yogurt and hard cheese, which are gentler on the gut. Drink the amount your body handles well.

If you suspect an allergy, do not guess, because allergy needs testing by a doctor and the result can change what you eat for years. If you are not sure whether you lack lactase, simple tests exist, including a breath test and a short diet trial. Those tell us more than any label does.

Let's go back to the four rungs one last time. Rung one, lactase kept, is where milk is simplest. Rung two, lactase lost, is where the answer depends on amount and form. Rung three, allergy, is where a doctor decides. Rung four is where milk is not for you at all, and no amount of argument changes that. The question is never simply whether milk is good or bad.

So, let's settle it: white poison or healthy drink? For most adults, neither label fits, and the honest answer is a food with a dose, a gut and a body on the other end. Drink the amount your body handles well, and skip it if it hurts you. And now we know why the label was never the answer.
