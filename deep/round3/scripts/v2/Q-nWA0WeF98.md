<<<NARRATION
Quick word from our sponsor first. It's fifteen seconds, and then we go back to the war that refuses to end.

Here's the claim, up front. Hannibal won almost every big battle he fought in Italy, and he still lost the war. That sounds impossible. How do you lose a war after winning everything? The one thing he never did was take Rome itself, and that's the whole story in one sentence. The rest of this video is the proof, or at least the argument.

Let's test that claim, because it's a strong one. Historians argue about whether Hannibal could have taken Rome at all. Some of them think he could have, if he had the men, the siege equipment and political support back home. So treat this as an argued thesis rather than a proof. Keep that in mind as we go.

Start in the south. You've seen cities switch allegiance before, and it's never clean. After Cannae, Capua, a wealthy city, switched to Hannibal's side. Picture the situation from Capua's point of view. Would you stay loyal to a republic that just lost an army, or switch to the winner? Plenty of people chose the winner. Then the city changed hands again, and that's the pattern of the next few years.

In 211 BCE, Rome recaptures Capua, and it does not do it gently. Hannibal, in response, tries something bold. He marches toward Rome itself, and the city panics. He camps near the walls, and for a moment it looks like he might actually do it. Then he turns back. Why? He has no siege train, Roman armies keep coming back, and he is always waiting for help that doesn't arrive.

Hannibal kept asking Carthage for more soldiers, and Carthage kept saying later.

That's the pattern that defines the second half of this war. Rome keeps raising armies. Hannibal keeps winning in the field. And Carthage keeps promising reinforcements that never quite make it across the sea. I'd argue the problem was never Hannibal's skill. It was the distance between his army and the government paying for it.

Meanwhile, Rome plays a longer game. Fabius, the cautious old commander, continues his strategy of delay and attrition. Marcellus takes on Syracuse, which has switched sides to Carthage, and the siege drags on for a long time. The Syracusans have clever engineers, and the stories about their inventions are a mix of fact and legend. Would you want to be the one who had to face those machines? I wouldn't. Rome isn't in a hurry, and that's the point. You can see why the impatient people in the Senate hated it. I'd argue the Senate deserves more credit than it usually gets.

Here's the comfortable belief: Hannibal was unbeatable, and he lost only because Carthage let him down. It's a satisfying story, and I think a lot of people believe a version of it. The flip is that winning battles is not the same as winning a war. Cities, allies, supplies and patience all matter, and Rome had more of all of them. I'd say that's a lesson you'll see in every long war, including the ones on the news.

Now look at Spain, because that's where Rome opens its second front. The young Scipio is sent west, and he is very good at making friends and very ruthless about enemies. He takes New Carthage, the main Carthaginian base in Spain, in a single assault. Then he returns hostages to their Spanish families, and that act wins him loyal allies. You can see the lesson already. A war of alliances is a war of trust, and Scipio understands that better than most. Would you trust a commander who gave your hostages back? I think most of the Spanish towns did.

How would you feel if your brother was marching toward you with an army? By the time Hasdrubal, Hannibal's brother, sets out from Spain to join him in Italy, Rome is ready. Hasdrubal has to cross the Alps again, with a huge army and a lot of noise. The Romans know where he is going. The two consuls, Nero and Salinator, intercept him at the Metaurus, in 207 BCE. The battle is decisive, and Hasdrubal is killed. According to Livy, his head is thrown into Hannibal's camp. That image is one of the most famous moments of the war, and you should treat it as a vivid account rather than a verified detail.

Hannibal kept asking Carthage for more soldiers, and Carthage kept saying later. This time, Hasdrubal did come, and he came too late, in pieces.

Here's the cast, because it's now very busy. I think this is the moment the video stops being about one general and starts being about a whole system. Scipio, the Roman who learns from Hannibal. Fabius, the delayer. Marcellus, who takes Syracuse. Masinissa, the Numidian prince who changes sides. Syphax, the Numidian king who switches sides. Nero and Salinator, who win at the Metaurus. Hanno, the Carthaginian who keeps trying to build peace. And Hasdrubal, the brother who never arrives. That's more than six, so keep one thread: Hannibal's reinforcements. Nobody is coming, and the more Rome knows it, the more confident it gets.

At Ilipa, in 206 BCE, Scipio beats the last major Carthaginian army in Spain. Carthage loses the peninsula, and with it the silver mines that paid for so much of the war. It's a huge swing, and the Senate notices. Scipio comes home with a reputation, and the question becomes what to do with him next.

Here's the decision. Should Scipio take the war to Africa, or should he stay in Spain and finish the job there? Pause and pick. Got it? Good. Africa is the answer. Fabius objects, and says it's reckless, and he is not entirely wrong. But Scipio goes anyway, and the Senate lets him. That's the moment the war moves across the water. Do you see how that one choice flips the whole board? I would have had a hard time choosing.

Scipio lands in Africa in 204 BCE. Syphax, the Numidian king who has already switched sides more than once, is courted by Rome, and that's not going to end well for him. Masinissa, the Numidian prince, switches to Rome and changes the balance of the whole campaign. There's a famous night when Scipio burns the camps of Syphax and his allies. I'd say that's the kind of story that gets retold for two thousand years, whether or not every detail is accurate.

Carthage, in a panic, recalls Hannibal from Italy. It's the first time in fifteen years he has had to leave. Think about that. He had been in Italy since 218 BCE, and now he's going home, to a city that never sent him enough men. You can picture the look on his face.

At Zama, in 202 BCE, the two great commanders finally meet on the same field. Hannibal brings his elephants and his veterans. Scipio brings discipline, flexibility and a very good sense of when to open a gap. Roughly forty thousand men are on each side, though those numbers are estimates. The result is Hannibal's first major defeat of the war. He escapes the field, but the war is effectively over.

The peace in 201 BCE is hard on Carthage. It loses its fleet, most of its elephants and its overseas empire. It pays an indemnity and accepts Roman terms. The city keeps its walls, for now. Rome has won, but it has also learned that empire is expensive.

So back to the claim. Hannibal won almost every big battle in Italy, and he still lost the war. Why? Because he couldn't take Rome, couldn't get reinforcements, and couldn't turn his victories into a settlement. Rome had allies, patience and a Senate that kept sending armies, and that combination beat a commander who was better than almost anyone on the field.

Here's the caveat I promised. Treat the thesis as an argument, not a proof. You should be suspicious of any story that comes with a perfect quote. The sources are Polybius and Livy, and they were writing with hindsight and with their own purposes. So some of the vivid details, like the head in the camp, deserve suspicion, even when they're wonderful stories.

Let's pause on the peace for a moment. Let's finish with a skit, because the Senate has one more scene. A senator asks Fabius whether all this delay has been worth it. Fabius says the army is still alive. The senator asks whether that's a plan. Fabius says it's the only plan that worked. The senator goes quiet, because the plan did work, even if nobody liked the way it looked.

So, the claim we started with: Hannibal won almost every big battle he fought, and he still lost the war. Rome lost most of the big battles for years, and still won the war. That's the whole lesson, and it's the reason you should never judge a war by the scoreboard alone.
