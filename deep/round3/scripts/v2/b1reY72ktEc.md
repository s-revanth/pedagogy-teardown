## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Quick word from our sponsor first. Yes, it's a sponsor, no, we won't be doing a bit about it, and I promise we'll be back in Petrograd in fifteen seconds.

April 1917. A man with a suitcase boards a sealed train in Switzerland. He's going home to Russia to start a revolution. The Germans, who are at war with Russia, let him travel through their territory in a locked carriage. Why would the enemy do that? Because they figured a few revolutionaries back home would make life harder for the Russian government. Think about that for a second, because you'd be surprised how often it happens. The enemy is helping him. Would you bet on that logic? Germany did, and it likely worked better than anyone expected, for a while.

Let's catch up, because you've probably lost track by now. The Tsar abdicated in March 1917. The country now has a Provisional Government, a group of liberals who want elections and a constitution. Alongside it sits the Petrograd Soviet, a council of workers and soldiers that acts like a second government. Two power centres, one country, and nobody completely sure who is in charge. Sound familiar? It's the same problem you get when two people both think they're the boss.

The Provisional Government's big problem is the war. It inherited the war from the Tsar, and it doesn't want to leave it, because leaving looks like a betrayal of the allies. So its war minister, Kerensky, bets on a big summer offensive to rally the country. Spoiler: it doesn't. In June the attack collapses, soldiers desert in huge numbers, and the streets fill with angry workers.

In July the Bolsheviks try to seize the moment. The uprising is crushed within days. Lenin flees to Finland, and the Provisional Government looks like it's back in control. For about five minutes.

Now the cast list, because the next part gets crowded. Lenin is the Bolshevik leader, and he is never more dangerous than when he's out of sight. Trotsky is a brilliant speaker who will run the military side. Kerensky is the lawyer turned prime minister. Kornilov is the army commander who wants order at any price. Martov leads the Mensheviks and keeps saying this is a mistake. Chernov speaks for the Socialist Revolutionaries and keeps losing the room. That's six names before we even get to the generals and the foreign armies. Everybody thinks they're the main character. Everybody's wrong about something. Everybody in Petrograd claimed to be in charge, and nobody had a plan.

So keep one thread. Who controls the soldiers and the guns? Hold on to that question, because it answers almost everything that happens next.

In August, General Kornilov marches on Petrograd. Kerensky says it's a coup. Kornilov says Kerensky invited him. Let's replay this from both sides, because both stories have a moment that feels true.

From Kerensky's side, he is the democrat under attack by a general who wants a dictatorship, and he needs help fast. From Kornilov's side, he's the only man who can stop the soviets and the chaos, and a prime minister who has lost control of his own capital. Replay A, from Kerensky's side. And here's the adjudicator's verdict, which I'm going to repeat as many times as it takes. Kerensky armed the people, and then wondered why they were armed.

I'll admit the Kornilov story always gets me, because it's so messy. Replay B, from Kornilov's side. His troops are stopped partly by railway workers, and partly by Bolshevik agitators who were released from prison to help defend the city. Kerensky takes the credit for stopping the coup, and the Bolsheviks quietly rebuild their strength. The adjudicator says the same thing. Kerensky armed the people, and then wondered why they were armed. Everybody in Petrograd claimed to be in charge, and nobody had a plan.

Here's the comfortable belief, and I'd guess a lot of people still hold it. The Bolsheviks won because they were the people's choice. It's a tidy story, and I want you to notice how tidy it is. Hold it for a second, and you'll see the cracks. I think the Assembly vote is the most underrated fact of the whole revolution. Let's check the numbers.

In October the Bolsheviks take Petrograd. Trotsky runs the military committee, Red Guards take the key buildings, and the Winter Palace falls with surprisingly little fighting. Then the Congress of Soviets declares that power now sits with the Soviets. If you were a soldier in that trench, you'd know the slogan by heart: peace, and land for the peasants. Who doesn't want that, when you've been sitting in a trench for three years? Would you?

Okay, quick guess. Peace, or keep fighting the war for the Provisional Government? Which slogan wins a tired soldier's vote? Pause, make your call. Peace, obviously. Land too. The Bolsheviks knew that was probably the whole pitch, and they were right.

Now let's check the belief against the vote. In November 1917 Russia holds elections for a Constituent Assembly. The Socialist Revolutionaries win the biggest share of the vote, and the Bolsheviks take about a quarter. A quarter. So did they have the people behind them? Not in the ballot box. They had the guns, and in this story the guns were enough.

In January 1918 the Assembly finally meets, and the next day armed guards shut it down. So the democratic experiment lasts about one day. I'd argue that's the moment the revolution stops pretending to be a vote and starts being a government.

In March 1918 the Bolsheviks sign a peace with Germany at Brest-Litovsk. It's brutal. Russia gives up huge areas, including land with a lot of people and a lot of food. The Bolsheviks justify it as survival, and their critics call it surrender. Both are right, and that's what makes it such a hard call.

Then comes the Civil War. The Reds, led by the Bolsheviks, face the Whites, a messy mix of monarchists, liberals and generals. Foreign armies land from Britain, France, Japan and the United States, each for its own reasons. Multiple armies, multiple agendas, and a country that's starving. In July 1918 the Tsar and his family are executed at Yekaterinburg. If you're hoping for a happy ending, you're in the wrong video. Nobody in this story gets one.

A quick caveat before the hardest part. The dates are in the old Julian calendar until early 1918, the vote figures are estimates in some sources, and the totals for the deaths are argued over. Treat the shape as solid and the figures as careful guesses.

Okay. This is where the jokes stop. I'd say the Red Terror is the moment this story stops being a sketch. The Red Terror begins in 1918. The secret police, the Cheka, take hostages, shoot people on suspicion, and put class enemies into camps. The totals are contested, and historians argue about the exact numbers. But the scale is not in dispute. These are real people, not characters in a sketch, and we owe them more than a punchline.

Then comes War Communism. The state takes grain from the peasants, sometimes at gunpoint, to feed the cities and the army. Peasants hide what they grow, harvests drop, and the countryside pays for the revolution with its food. Famine follows into 1921. Millions die across the civil war and the famine years, and the total is still debated.

In March 1921 the sailors at Kronstadt, the same men who helped make the revolution, rise up against the Bolsheviks. The uprising is crushed. And Lenin makes a retreat: the New Economic Policy lets some markets come back. It's a survival move. The revolution survives by stepping back a little.

So why does this matter? A party that wins the guns sets the terms for everyone else. You can argue about whether the Bolsheviks could have done anything differently, and plenty of people have. But once the Assembly was shut down, the question of who was in charge had a very clear answer.

A man with a suitcase boards a sealed train, and he goes home to start a revolution. The country he gets off in is not the one he left, and the one he builds is not the one he promised. Everybody in Petrograd claimed to be in charge. Lenin had a plan, though. It just wasn't the one they had in mind.
NARRATION>>>
