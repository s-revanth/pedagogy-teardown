## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Take a coin and hold it under a bright lamp. You know what the shadow looks like. It is dark all the way across, with a sharp edge, and the middle is as dark as the rest. That's what you'd expect, and you would be right almost everywhere. But here is the surprise, and I'll give it to you right away, and I'll be direct about it. The middle of that shadow can be bright. Not dim, bright.

The sharp edge you expect is the rule that breaks here. The reason is that light is a wave, and waves bend a little around edges, in two directions at once. Waves going around the rim of a disc meet in the middle, and where they arrive in step, they add up to light. Why would they arrive in step? Because the rim is symmetric, every path around it has the same length to the centre. So the answer is in the wave nature of light, and the centre of the shadow is bright.

Let's look at it on the bench. Here is a small disc, about two centimetres across, sitting one metre from a point of light, with a screen three metres behind it. Look at the centre of the shadow, not the edges. What you see is a bright spot, right where the simple picture says there should be darkness. It is small, and I'd bet most people miss it if they only look at the edge, so I want you to look at the centre on purpose.

The simple picture is the ray model, a model people have used for more than two thousand years. Light travels in straight lines, so anything blocking it casts a shadow with a sharp edge, and everything inside the shadow gets no light at all. That model works for almost everything you do in daily life. You can see the edges in your kitchen, and they look crisp. The ray model is a good approximation for most shadows, and it is the picture most of us carry around. For example, the sharp shadow of a hand on a wall is close enough for almost any purpose.

But light is a wave, and waves don't stop dead at an edge. Think of water rolling past a post in a stream. The ripples curl in behind it, and they fill some of the space the simple picture called empty. For light, the same thing happens around the rim of a disc. The effect is small, but it is probably more than most people expect, and it is there. If you add up all the bent light arriving at the centre, the middle of the shadow gets something.

In 1818, the French engineer Augustin Fresnel presented a wave theory of light to the Académie des Sciences in Paris. One of its members, the mathematician Siméon Denis Poisson, took Fresnel's equations seriously, and then followed them to a conclusion that seemed absurd to him. If the theory were true, a circular shadow would have a bright spot at its very centre.

Poisson thought this was a reductio. I'd say that is the right instinct for a scientist, but it has to be paired with a test. A bright spot in the middle of a shadow looked so wrong that he believed it showed the wave theory had to be false. That is a powerful move in science. When a theory predicts something ridiculous, you test it, and you do not argue your way out of it. Would you have believed the spot was real without the test?

So François Arago ran the test, and I find this part of the story thrilling. He set up a small disc, shone light on it, and looked at its shadow with care. And there it was. The spot appeared, bright and centred, exactly where the wave theory had put it. The absurd consequence was real, and the committee's worry had turned into the strongest evidence for the wave picture.

Let's replay that with the disc on the screen. Watch the edges first. They blur slightly, as the waves bend in, over a band about five millimetres wide. Now watch the centre. The bent light from every direction around the rim reaches the middle with the same timing, so the waves add up, and the spot brightens. That is the whole mechanism in one clip.

A pond gives a good picture of this. Drop a stone, wait two or three seconds, and let the ripples run into a post in the middle of the water. The waves go around both sides of the post, and if they meet behind it in step, the water there moves more than anywhere else. Light is doing the same thing on a much smaller scale, with wavelengths of roughly 400 to 700 nanometres instead of ripples.

Now let's change the disc, and try three sizes. Make it bigger, and the spot survives, though the picture shifts. Make the disc tiny, and the diffraction spreads, so the shadow looks less sharp. Each change tells us something about the waves, and I'd expect you to see the pattern quickly. The spot is not a flaw in the experiment. It is a fingerprint of the wave nature of light.

A caveat before we go on, and I should be honest about it. The bright spot in this clip comes from an idealised setup, a point-like source and a very clean disc. Real lamps have some width, and real edges have imperfections. Those two things blur the spot and make it harder to see. The principle holds, but the picture on your desk will be fainter than the one in a textbook. So how much of the spot would you actually see? Probably less than the textbook shows, and we should be careful about that.

There's also a limit on where you can see it. In an ordinary room, the spot is small and faint, and light from walls and furniture swamps it. You need a clean source, a dark background, and two or three minutes of patience. That is why the experiment is more of a bench test than a party trick. If you try it yourself, use a dark room, a coin and a torch, and give your eyes ten minutes to adjust.

The same bending affects every image you see. Telescopes and microscopes both have limits set by how light spreads around their apertures, so this little spot is connected to the sharpest views we can get. Wave bending is not a curiosity at the edge of a shadow. It shapes what instruments can resolve. So why do the best microscopes need such careful optics? I find that connection remarkable.

It also shows why sharp shadows are only an approximation, and I would not call them wrong, only limited. When the object is about a thousand times bigger than the wavelength, the ray model is nearly perfect. Make the object comparable to the wavelength, and the wave picture takes over. That is why most everyday shadows look sharp, and why the sharp edge you expect is so convincing.

So here is the payoff. Poisson saw an absurd spot and thought it killed the wave theory. Arago tested it, and the spot was there, which I find one of the best examples of a bold prediction paying off. The thing that looked like a mistake was the test that confirmed the theory. That is why the middle of a shadow can be bright, and why you should look at the centre, not the edge.

The middle of the shadow is not dark after all.
