## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Okay, it's a Sunday morning, and I'm setting out on a bike with no real plan except to see where the streets go. I've got a rough route in my head, heading east to west and mostly following the river. In a normal London, this would be a slow exercise in staying alive. So what does a city look like when it's something else?

First, the basics of riding in the city. You're in a long stream of traffic: buses, vans, taxis, the occasional cyclist who looks like he's in a race. The air smells of diesel and the sound never stops. The lights are long, the junctions are crowded, and every time you stop you're third in line behind something very large.

So the first junction on this route is the one I expected to be terrible. It's a big crossroads by a park, with four lanes of traffic and a light that takes its time. I stop at the red. I wait. The light turns green. Nothing comes. I wait a little longer, out of habit, and the light turns amber, then red again, then green. Nobody has come.

That's the first thing I want you to notice. The light is still doing its job, cycling through its colors on a timer. It's just changing for nobody. Why keep a light running for an empty road? I cross, and the light changes again, for nobody again. It's a small thing, but it's the first sign that this isn't an ordinary ride.

The next stretch is a long road with a bus lane. Normally that lane is a parking space for buses and the impatience of their drivers. Today the bus lane is just a lane. There's a bus sitting in it with the driver and one passenger at the back. It's stopped because nobody has told it where to go. I cycle past in the lane that's usually full of cars, and nobody honks.

Then the river. The Thames is wide here, and the bridges cross it in long, unhurried lines. On a normal day, the bridges are a traffic jam with a view. Today the view is the whole thing. I can see the water, the buildings on the far bank, and the curve of the river without a bus filling the frame.

I ride onto one of the bridges and stop in the middle, which I would never do on a normal day. Nobody is behind me. Nobody is in front of me. There's a gull, a lot of sunlight, and the sound of wind. It's the first time I've heard the river from a bridge in this city without an engine covering it.

Now let me tell you what the gap is, because it's the reason for the whole trip. This was the spring of 2020. The first lockdown had started, most people had been told to stay home, and most of the traffic simply stopped. The city was still there. It just wasn't moving the way it usually does, and I'd decided to ride through it while it was like that.

I didn't lead with that, on purpose. I wanted you to see the streets first. A street you know well looks different when there's no noise in it, and I wanted the first part of the ride to be the normal London frame, the one you'd expect, so the missing parts could show up on their own.

Here's a quieter thought, which came to me on that bridge. Most of the road space in this city is for cars. Lanes, junctions, parking, the wide turning circles, the barriers that keep people on the pavement. I knew that in the abstract. But with the cars gone, the space they use was suddenly visible, the way a room looks bigger once the furniture is moved out. A road that normally feels cramped looks like a lot of tarmac for very few people.

Now the middle of the city, where the empty streets are even stranger. Here's a square I know well. On a normal day it's a crush of tourists, pigeons, and people trying to take the same photo of the same lions. Today I count maybe a dozen people in the whole square. A couple of them are taking photos too, which is the one thing that hasn't changed.

The light at the next crossing changes for nobody again. I stop, because it's still the rule, and I wait for it. Then it changes, and I laugh, a bit, at myself for waiting. The rule is still the rule. The reason it exists has gone, and the light doesn't know that. Who is the rule for, then?

Let me be careful about how much I claim from one ride. I was on one bike, on one Sunday, in one part of the city, and I'm not a traffic engineer. What I saw is a sample of a sample. It tells me how the streets felt on that day. It doesn't tell me how many cars were missing, how many people were out, or how long the quiet lasted. Treat it as an impression, not a survey.

Still, the impression is strong. You can feel how much of the city's noise is traffic, and how much of its shape is designed around it. You can also see how many corners the city has that nobody would ever stop at, except when the traffic makes them crowded.

Next stretch: the long road toward the old docklands, where the offices are glass and the bike lanes are newer. There's a painted lane that normally holds a line of bikes, and today it holds me. I pass a couple of delivery riders, heads down, working the same way they always do. They are the other part of the city that didn't stop. Kitchens and shops and warehouses probably kept running. Someone had to bring the food.

That's a reminder I want to keep. A pandemic didn't pause a city for everyone. Some jobs got harder. Some people probably got busier, and some got sick. The quiet street I was enjoying was not a holiday for everybody. It was a strange, uneven time, and some of the people I passed were working through it, in masks, at the same hours they always had.

Back to the lights, because it's the thing I keep coming back to. A traffic light cycling for nobody is a small, silly image. But it's also the purest version of the question the quiet raised. Those lights were set up for a city full of cars. When the cars are gone, the lights keep doing exactly what they were built to do, which is manage a flow that isn't there. It's a little like a bell ringing for an empty church. The system keeps running on habit.

Let's stop on a bench near the water, where I looked at the map on my phone. The route I had in my head was mostly fine. But I noticed how often my plan had relied on being able to share the road with much bigger things. Without them, I had space, and I used it. I went wider than I meant to, and I didn't mind.

Then let's turn toward the park. Parks are different in a city like this. They're where the quiet looks most natural. Trees, grass, and a path that people normally cut through quickly on their way to work. That Sunday the paths were slow. People walked their dogs, sat on benches, read, and waved at each other from a distance. It was a version of the city I hadn't known was there.

Let's ask the honest question, and I'll try to answer it without too much hope in the answer. Did the streets go back to the way they were? For the most part, yes. The traffic returned. The buses filled up. The lights started changing for the crowds again. Most of the city went back to its old habits, the way a river returns to its bed after a flood.

But not entirely. Some things stuck. More people cycle now than before, on some routes at least, and the lanes are more visible. A few quiet streets stayed quieter longer than the lockdown required. And a lot of people noticed, as I did, how much of the city was built for cars, and how much of it could be used differently.

So I don't think the lesson is that the city was secretly perfect when it was empty. Empty streets are a strange state, and nobody wants a city like that. The lesson is narrower and more useful. When the normal pressure lifted, the city showed what it could look like without that pressure, and that's worth remembering when the traffic comes back.

I finished the ride near the river, where I had started the loop. The light at the crossing was changing for nobody. I stopped anyway, because it's still the rule, and because it's the last picture I wanted from that day. Then the light changed, and a few people started to appear on the other side of the road.

They weren't a crowd yet. But they were coming back, and the city was coming back with them, and the sound of it was starting to return. The quiet had been real, and strange, and it had ended. The part I'd keep is the bridge, the empty square, the bus with one passenger, and the light that kept changing for no one.

That's the ride. Take a bike through a city and you see the parts that the traffic usually covers. For one spring, London was quiet enough to show them to me.
NARRATION>>>
