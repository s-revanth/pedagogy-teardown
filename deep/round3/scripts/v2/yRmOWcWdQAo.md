## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Quick word from our sponsor before we set sail. It's fifteen seconds, it's fine, and I promise we're not sinking anything in the next ten minutes.

So here's the question. Why would two of the biggest powers in the western Mediterranean go to war over a town run by ex-mercenaries? That's a strange way to start a war between Rome and Carthage. But it's exactly how this one begins, and it tells you a lot about how these empires thought.

Let's set the scene. Messana sits on the Sicilian side of the narrow strait that separates Sicily from Italy. A band of mercenaries, the Mamertines, took the city by force. According to our main ancient source, they killed the men, took the property and married the women. You can picture how popular they were with their neighbours. Would you want to live next door to them? No, not really.

Syracuse, the big Greek city on Sicily, decides to attack Messana. The Mamertines are in trouble, so they ask for help. First they turn to Carthage, which has its own reasons to like a strait it can use. Then they turn to Rome. And that's the moment everything changes, because Rome now has to decide whether to help a band of thieves or let Carthage take the strait.

Rome decides to get involved in 264 BCE. The Senate is not thrilled, and I'd say most senators would have preferred a quieter life. But once the army crosses the strait, there's no going back. The first campaigns are messy, with shifting alliances and a lot of local politics. Hiero, the ruler of Syracuse, eventually switches sides and makes peace with Rome. I'll admit I find the local politics hard to keep straight. And just like that, the map changes. The strait is Roman business now.

Next up is Agrigentum, a major Carthaginian stronghold on the south coast of Sicily. Rome lays siege to it in 262 BCE, and the siege drags on for months. Carthage sends help. Rome keeps digging. The city's defenders are running out of food, and the walls get weaker by the week. How do you think this ends? Agrigentum falls, and Rome has its first big prize. I think that's the moment the war starts to look like a real war.

But here's the problem, and it's a big one. Carthage rules the sea. Rome has no navy to speak of, and every island town is only as safe as Carthage allows. We can't fight a war for Sicily if the supply ships keep arriving from Africa, so Rome needs to get into the water. The question is how. What would you do with a navy you had never had?

Now the cast gets crowded. You've got Roman consuls who need a win to look good back home. Carthaginian admirals, who know the sea better than anyone. Hiero, who has already switched sides once and will do it again. And the Mamertines, who started this whole thing and would like to be left alone. That's more than six names, so I'll keep one thread: the boats. Everything in this part of the war comes back to the boats.

Here's where Rome gets creative, and by creative I mean brute force. The story goes that Rome found a stranded Carthaginian warship, copied it, and built a fleet of its own. The timeline is probably hard to believe, with the ancient source claiming about sixty days. Treat that figure as a rough estimate, not a receipt. But the basic move is clear. If you don't have a navy, build one from the nearest thing that floats.

And then Rome added a boarding bridge. It's called the corvus, a heavy plank with a spike that drops onto the enemy ship and pins it in place. Once the bridge is down, a sea battle becomes a land fight, and Rome's soldiers are much better at land fights than Carthage's sailors are at close combat. Rome's entire navy strategy was basically: put a plank on it.

In 260 BCE, at Mylae, the corvus does its job. The consul Duilius leads the Roman fleet into battle, and the bridges come down one after another. Carthaginian ships get pinned, boarded and taken. It's the first real Roman naval victory, and it sends a very clear message. Carthage no longer owns the water. Rome has a navy, and it knows how to use it.

The corvus returns in the next battles, and the Carthaginian line breaks at the bridge again and again. I'd say that's the detail to remember. Carthaginian losses are heavy, and the sea is contested now in a way it wasn't before. The Senate, naturally, wants more ships and more victories. Who wouldn't want more? Who would stop now, when the last battle went so well?

So here's a quick guess. Rome has a navy now, and it has to decide what to do with it. Build a bigger fleet and keep fighting at sea, or stop chasing the water and go home? Pause and make your call. Got it? Good. They build, and they win again.

A quick caveat, because the numbers matter. Ship counts from ancient sources are rough, and they're often exaggerated. Sizes of fleets and armies vary between accounts, and the battle totals should be treated as estimates. Keep the scale in mind, but don't take the exact tallies as gospel.

Then comes Ecnomus, in 256 BCE. It is one of the largest naval battles on record, with hundreds of ships on each side. The Roman fleet holds together, the corvus does its job again, and the battle opens the way to Africa. It's the moment where Rome stops being a visitor on the water and starts planning to invade the other side of it.

Now let's flip the story. The comfortable belief is that Rome is a land power and simply can't fight at sea. It's a tidy story, and I think people like it because it sounds like common sense. The flip is that Rome won sea battles by making a land fight happen on water, with a plank and a lot of determination. Common sense was never the whole recipe.

Here's the thread again, because the cast is about to get bigger. Regulus is the Roman general who lands in Africa. Xanthippus is the Spartan mercenary who trains the Carthaginian army. The Carthaginian senate wants a deal, Hanno wants a fight, and Regulus wants glory. Keep your eye on Regulus's army, because that's where the story goes next. I'd keep an eye on him too.

In 256 BCE Regulus lands in Africa. The early results look great. Carthage is shaken, and Regulus offers terms that sound generous enough to be tempting. Carthage refuses, and then it does something smart. It hires Xanthippus, a Spartan mercenary who knows how to train an army for open battle. I'd argue that one hire changes the whole war.

Xanthippus trains the Carthaginian army, and it comes out fresh, disciplined and ready to fight in the open. The army marches against Regulus. Here is the mood in the camps: the Romans assume they are winning, and the Carthaginians have finally found a plan they like. Would you bet on the side that just got a better general? The Romans didn't think they had to bet at all.

At Adys, in 255 BCE, the Carthaginian army crushes Regulus. The Roman army is destroyed, and Regulus is captured. The sources say he died in captivity, but the details are contested, and some of the stories are probably designed to make a point. So treat the ending of this man's life as a set of competing accounts.

On the way home, the Roman fleet is lost in a storm. Hundreds of ships are reportedly gone, and the sea that Rome had just fought so hard to control takes the fleet back. Who wins then? Nobody, really. Think about how that must feel. You win the water, you lose the army, and the weather takes the rest.

Now, a short skit, because the Senate deserves one. A senator asks a shipwright how much a new fleet will cost. The shipwright answers in timber, bronze and sailors. A timber merchant asks who is paying. The senator says the state. The merchant says the state has no money. Everybody looks at the treasury, and the treasury looks back.

That's why the sea matters so much. An army in Africa can't win without ships to bring supplies, reinforcements and replacements. Regulus had a plan that depended on the water staying calm, and the water didn't cooperate. Rome's entire navy strategy was basically: put a plank on it. Unfortunately, planks do not help you in a storm.

The boarding bridge was clever, and I'll give the Romans that. The storms were not clever, and nobody had a plank for them. So the lesson is simple. A navy can win battles, but it can't control the weather.

Rome, in the next year, plans a new fleet. The treasury has to pay for it, the sailors have to be found, and the ships have to be built. So, yes, two fleets are at the bottom of the sea, and a general is in a Carthaginian prison. And yet, the Senate says, we'll build another one. Would you?
