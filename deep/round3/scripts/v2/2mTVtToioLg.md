<<<NARRATION
So what's a selfish gene? It's one of the most misread phrases in biology, and the name does a lot of damage on its own. Here's the answer up front. A selfish gene is a gene that spreads because of how it gets copied. The word selfish is shorthand for copy success. It doesn't mean the gene wants anything, and it doesn't mean the body it sits in is out for itself.

Let's define it minimally, and only as much as we need. A gene is a stretch of copied information. Each time a body reproduces, its genes get copied into the next generation, sometimes with small errors. If a gene ends up with more copies in the next generation than its rivals, it's favored, and over time it becomes more common. That's the whole definition, and nothing in it requires intent. It's a bookkeeping idea first.

Here's the picture I'd like you to hold. Imagine a colored tag in every blob. The tag is the gene. Blobs with a red tag leave more tagged offspring, and blobs with a blue tag leave fewer. Over generations, the red tag spreads. The blobs didn't decide that, and nobody chose red. The copying did the work, and that's what the word selfish is pointing at.

Here's where the trouble starts. When I first heard the phrase, I took it literally. I pictured each gene as a tiny agent, scheming for its own survival. That's a natural reading, and it's not a stupid one. People read words by their everyday meanings, and selfish is an everyday word with a moral edge. But the literal reading is wrong, and the way it's wrong matters a lot for understanding the idea.

The gene has no mind. It doesn't plan, prefer, or know anything. It has no goal, and it can't look ahead. What it has is a property. When it's present in a body, that body's copies tend to carry it forward, or they don't. So the right question isn't what the gene wants. The right question is which genes end up with more copies, and why. The answer to that question is the whole theory.

So here's the name, and only now do we use it. We called it selfish after we saw the copies spread, not before. A gene that helps itself get copied will spread even if it harms the body around it. A gene that helps the body but not its own copies can disappear. The word fits that pattern, so we keep it. We keep it knowing that the pattern is the thing, and the motive is not.

Let's check that with a quick choice. Two blobs are in front of you. Blob A shares its food with the blobs around it. Blob B hoards its food and keeps it for itself. Which blob carries the gene that spreads? Pick A or B, and hold on to it. Think about who receives the food, not about which blob looks nicer. Make your call before we work it out.

Here's the answer, and it depends on the receivers. If blob A's food mostly goes to relatives who carry the same gene, the sharing gene spreads. If blob B's hoarding costs it nothing, and its food never reaches a relative, the hoarding gene spreads. So the answer is that it depends on the copies, not on which blob looks nicer. That's a surprising result, and it's the reason the word is useful at all.

Notice what that says. A gene that makes its body generous can spread, if the generosity lands on the gene's own copies. A gene that makes its body stingy can spread too, if the stinginess costs the gene nothing. So selfish isn't a verdict on the animal. It's a label for which copies end up being counted. That's the thing to remember if you ever hear the phrase in a debate.

I'd guess a lot of you have heard this phrase as a moral verdict, and you're probably not alone in that. I did, and it took me a while to let go of it. Maybe the easiest test is to ask where the food lands, and not how the blob behaves. If the food reaches a relative, the gene that produced the sharing gets counted. If it reaches a stranger, the gene gets nothing from the gesture.

Think of a case you already know. A parent who shares dinner with a child is, in this sense, passing along shared copies, because the child carries about half of the parent's genes. A stranger at the table carries none of them. The copy logic predicts that difference without any appeal to love or intent, and that's the reason the idea is useful. It also tells you what the phrase can't do.

Now a caveat, because it matters. This is a metaphor, and metaphors break. The gene doesn't feel selfish. It doesn't have a self. The word is useful for remembering the copy logic, and it's dangerous if you carry the moral sense in with it. If we forget that, we'll reach for a motive that isn't there. So keep the metaphor, and drop the motive every time.

The same logic helps explain behaviour we see in animals. Parents feed children who share their genes. Siblings defend each other in some species. Those patterns look selfless from the outside. The copy logic explains them without assuming any kindness at the level of the gene. The gene's success is measured in copies, and the behaviour follows from that.

So here's where we ended up. A selfish gene is a gene whose spread is driven by copy success, not by intent. Selfish is shorthand, and it's a good one, as long as you keep the quotation marks in your head. Nothing in the body is out for itself in the human sense. The genes are simply counted. The ones that get counted most are the ones we see most often, and that's the entire idea.
