## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Here is a snake in a rainforest canopy, and I want you to hold a simple question in mind while we watch it. Why are venomous animals so concentrated in warm places? Many of the most dangerous snakes, spiders and scorpions live near the equator, and you might assume that heat is the answer. I wouldn't be so quick. Heat is one possible factor, and it sits alongside several others that are at least as plausible.

I'd also like you to look at the numbers before deciding. There are roughly 4,000 species of snake, and something like 600 of them are venomous. Among spiders, the count runs to about 50,000 species, and nearly all of them are venomous, though only a small share can seriously harm a person. If you were betting on which group would show the pattern, you would want those counts first.

I'll start with what venom actually is. Venom is a mixture of toxins that an animal delivers into another animal, usually through a bite, a sting or a spine. It is not the same as poison, which is absorbed or swallowed. That distinction matters, because it tells you venom is a delivery system, and a delivery system costs energy to build and to use.

Venom is far more common across the animal kingdom than most people realise. Snakes have it, and so do some lizards, spiders, scorpions and cone snails. Even the male platypus carries two venom spurs on its hind legs. Cone snails alone number more than 700 species, and scorpions add about 2,500 more. I find that count surprising every time. Why would that be? Venom has evolved at least dozens of times, in lineages that have no close relationship to one another. So the first thing you should notice is that venom is not a rare trick that evolved once and spread.

Venom also serves two different jobs, and the job matters for the explanation. Some animals use venom mainly to subdue prey, while others use it mostly to deter predators. A cone snail is a hunter, and a stinging insect is often a defender. Each job pays off under different conditions, so one explanation for all venomous animals would be too simple. That split is the first thing to check.

A helpful analogy is a specialised tool. A plain spear is cheap to carry and easy to make, while a finely built harpoon takes effort, skill and maintenance. A harpoon pays off only when the hunt is frequent enough, and the target hard enough, to justify the trouble. Venom works the same way. It is worth the cost when the prey is difficult to subdue, or when a predator is dangerous to approach at close range. If you were choosing a tool for a hunt, you would ask the same thing: how often will I use it, and what will it cost me?

That gives us the first candidate, and it is diversity. More species in a place means more chances that one lineage will stumble on venom and keep it. The tropics hold far more species than cold regions, and in some groups the difference is dramatic, so if venom evolves at a steady rate per lineage, you would expect more venomous species there. That picture is simple, and it is also incomplete, because it explains how many lineages there are, not why any particular lineage keeps its venom.

Picture a branching diagram of animal families, with the branches thicker in warm regions. Each branch is a lineage. Some of them end in a venom tip, and most of those tips cluster where the branches are dense. The diagram is a useful picture, but it can hide something important. Some regions have been studied far more thoroughly than others, and that changes what the data really show.

So let's be careful about that. The diversity hypothesis makes a prediction, and the prediction can be tested. If it is right, the share of venomous species should track the number of species in a region, and not the temperature itself. If the share follows temperature more closely than species count, then diversity alone cannot be the whole story, and you should say so.

The second candidate is ecology. Warm places often contain more predators, more prey, and more interactions, which means more reasons for an animal to be armed. A venomous snake in a dense forest may need to subdue a fast lizard, a frog or a rodent in under five seconds, and a quick acting toxin can make that possible. In a cold place, say with three times fewer prey species, that pressure may be weaker. You would expect venom to be harder to justify there, and that is a prediction you can check.

But ecology has a catch. Many predators in cold places are venomous too. Some northern snakes, for example, carry venom that works well against small mammals and birds. So the ecological story has to explain why venom would be more useful in the tropics, not just whether it is useful somewhere. That is a harder claim to test than it first appears, and I think it is the weakest link in the chain.

The third candidate, and the one I'd check first, is energy. Producing venom takes metabolic resources, and cold-blooded animals have an energy budget that depends heavily on temperature. Warm-climate animals run faster metabolisms, so the cost of making and replacing venom may be easier to afford. Metabolic rate often roughly doubles for every ten degrees of warming, though the exact figure varies between species and groups. I would want to see that measured directly before I relied on it. That is a plausible story, and it has the advantage of being a physical one. But it is not established, and the energetic argument has been made more often than it has been measured.

Let's pause and tally what we have after three candidates. Diversity explains the count of lineages. Ecology explains the payoff in a particular place. Energy explains the affordability. None of the three is settled, and each one predicts a different pattern across species, so each one can be wrong in its own way. That is the honest state of the question, and it is less satisfying than a single cause, which is exactly why it is worth saying out loud.

Here is what I find most useful about this problem. The three candidates do not compete so much as they stack. A large species pool gives lineages the chance to evolve venom. Ecology sets how much a lineage gains from it. Energy sets how easily it can be kept. You could be right about one and wrong about another, and the data would look messy in exactly the way we see.

The next question is whether the pattern holds up across groups. Researchers look at four major groups separately, namely snakes, lizards, spiders and fish, because each group has its own history. If the same climate trend shows up in several independent lineages, that is much stronger evidence than a single striking case. If it shows up in only one group, you should be suspicious of the story, however neat it looks. Researchers compare many lineages, and I would look for the pattern to repeat in at least three independent groups before I believed it.

Take the cone snail on a coral reef. It fires a tiny harpoon loaded with venom into a fish, and the fish is subdued within about two seconds, and one snail can carry dozens of different toxins. This is a clean example of the ecology hypothesis at work, because the snail hunts fast-moving prey. But a single example cannot tell us why cone snails are common on warm reefs. It only tells us that venom can be a hunting tool in a warm place. When you see a striking example, ask whether it is a pattern or an exception.

I want to be clear about two limits. Most of the venom research has focused on a small number of groups, perhaps a handful out of the dozens of animal lineages that make venom, and many tropical species are poorly known. If you read a survey, ask where it was done. A species list is only as good as the survey behind it, and surveys are uneven across the globe. So the warm-climate pattern could be partly an artefact of where scientists have looked, and that is worth remembering whenever you see a neat map.

The same logic applies to any trait that shows up more often in one climate. You can ask the same three questions. Is it the number of species? Is it the payoff in each environment? Is it the cost of producing the trait? If you can answer those questions with data, you have a real explanation. If you cannot, you may still have a useful lead, and you should go and measure it. If you can only answer them with a story, you have a hypothesis, and you should label it as one.

Venom has also been useful to people. A blood pressure medicine derived from snake venom came out in 1981, and a painkiller derived from cone snail venom was approved in 2004. The gap between those two dates is more than two decades, and I find that telling. Those cases are a reminder that studying venom is not only a puzzle about climate. If you have ever taken a blood pressure medicine, you may already have benefited from this kind of research. It is a source of molecules that evolution has already tested for us, across millions of years of trial and error.

Let's bring the pieces together one last time. Warm places hold more species, which gives venom more chances to evolve. Those places also hold more predators and prey, which can make venom worth its cost. And warm metabolisms may make that cost easier to pay. The answer, so far, is a combination of factors, not a single cause. I'd want you to take three things from this: venom is a delivery system, the candidates make different predictions, and the map is partly a record of effort.

The rainforest snake from the start is not the answer. It is the question, and researchers are still working through it. The next time you see a striking map of venomous animals, remember that the colours mark where we have looked as much as where the animals live.
NARRATION>>>
