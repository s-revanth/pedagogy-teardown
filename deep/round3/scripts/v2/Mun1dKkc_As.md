## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Quick word from our sponsor before we head into the mud. It's a short one, we'll keep it quick, and you can get back to the trenches right after.

Picture yourself at nineteen. Your whole job is to walk a few hundred metres across a field that artillery has churned into soup. On the other side, people with machine guns are extremely good at their jobs. Not a great day at the office, is it? And you don't get to clock out at the end of it.

By 1916 the Western Front has turned into a very long argument with shovels. Both sides have dug in so deep that nobody can move without a fight. The German chief of staff, Falkenhayn, has a plan. He isn't trying to win one big battle. He wants to bleed the French army white. So he picks a fortress town called Verdun, because he knows the French will fight like mad to hold it, and then he feeds in more men than they can replace.

So Verdun becomes a meat grinder. It goes on for months, and the casualties add up to something like 700,000 across both armies. Think about that number for a second. Those are men from farms, factories and classrooms, spread across a few square kilometres of shell holes.

Nobody told the generals that mud is not a plan.

I'll be honest, when I first read the Somme numbers I had to put the book down for a minute. I'd argue anybody would.

Then the British decide it's their turn. The Somme, July 1916. The plan is a massive artillery bombardment, and then the infantry walks forward in neat lines. The bombardment was supposed to cut the German wire. It mostly didn't. On the first day, the British suffer around 57,000 casualties, and nearly 20,000 of those men are dead. That's the worst day in the history of the British army, and it happened in a single morning.

Okay, this is where the jokes stop. Those are not numbers on a spreadsheet. Those are teenagers from towns you've probably never heard of, and most of them never got to see the next season.

The battle grinds on for months. The line moves maybe ten kilometres. Millions of shells get fired, and the front looks much the same as it did before. So would you call that a win? The generals kept calling it progress, and the men in the mud did not agree. I keep coming back to that word. You can measure progress in kilometres, but here the kilometres were being bought with graves.

Now, the viewer turn. Do you order the next wave over the top? Yes or no? Pause for a second and decide. Yes? Yeah. That's the problem. Every general in this war thought the next wave would be the one that worked.

Here's a thing nobody puts on the postcards. Trenches were disgusting. Rats, lice, trench foot, water you really didn't want to drink, and a smell you would never forget. Every soldier learned the same lesson the hard way: the enemy isn't always the most dangerous thing in the trench. I'd argue the boredom was worse. I'm not going to pretend it was all grim, because soldiers joked, sang and wrote home about the weather. The jokes were a coping mechanism, and I think that's worth saying out loud.

By 1917, Germany has a new idea. Stop chasing armies and starve Britain instead. Unrestricted submarine warfare means U-boats sink anything heading toward Britain, including neutral ships. Why does this matter? Britain imports a huge share of its food, so starving the island wins the war without beating the army in France. The logic was not crazy. It was just badly timed. I think the lesson is that a plan can be logical and still be a disaster, if it ignores what the other side does next.

Germany's planners calculated that Britain would crack before the United States could matter. They were wrong, and one of the reasons they were wrong was a telegram. The Zimmermann telegram is a German offer to Mexico: help us attack the United States, and we'll help you win back lost territory. British intelligence intercepts it, decodes it, and hands it to Washington. Americans are furious. The United States declares war in April 1917. Did the Germans really think that would go unnoticed? Would you trust a telegram like that? Plenty of Americans didn't, right up until it was printed in the newspapers, and then they were angry.

Meanwhile, Russia is falling apart. The Tsar has gone, the new government is struggling, and the Bolsheviks promise peace and land. In March 1918 Russia signs the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk and gives away huge areas to get out of the war. That frees up German divisions for the West, and suddenly Germany has a window. I'm not going to tell you the Bolsheviks were heroes or villains here. I'm going to tell you what they promised, and what that promise bought them.

Ludendorff, the German commander, uses it. In the spring of 1918 he launches a massive offensive, hitting before American troops arrive in big numbers. It works for a while. German soldiers advance further than they have since 1914. Then the supply lines can't keep up. Men are exhausted, horses are dying, and the food runs short. Nobody told the generals that mud is not a plan. Why would an army that can't feed itself keep marching? Because the generals told them to.

Tanks, fresh American units and better artillery co-ordination all arrive. By summer 1918 the Allies are hitting back in a series of attacks, and the German line starts breaking. Bulgaria drops out. The Ottoman Empire drops out. Austria-Hungary is falling apart. Germany is still fighting, but it's fighting alone and out of breath.

On 11 November 1918, the armistice takes effect at eleven in the morning. After four years, the guns stop. A quick caveat, because the numbers matter. Casualty figures for this war vary a lot by source, so treat the big numbers as estimates, not receipts.

So picture yourself at nineteen. The guns stop at eleven in the morning on the eleventh of November, and nobody tells you where to go next.
NARRATION>>>
