<<<NARRATION
Blobs don't plan anything. So how could teamwork ever show up in a world full of them? Here's the short answer, and the rest of this video is about earning it. Teamwork evolves when sharing costs a little, and when the blobs that share mostly meet other sharers. Nobody decides to cooperate. The sharing just gets copied when it pays.

Let's start with the world we've been building. Each morning, pairs of food appear on the plane, and blobs leave their homes around the edge to go find them. A blob that eats one piece of food survives to the next day. A blob that eats two pieces survives and also reproduces. A blob that finds nothing before its energy runs out simply dies. That's the whole rulebook, and none of it has changed since the earlier videos.

The new wrinkle is that food comes in pairs. When two blobs head for the same pair, they have to decide what to do with it. The first option is to split it, one piece each. We'll call that sharing. The second option is to grab what you can, and we'll call that greedy. Those are the only two strategies in this video, and everything else gets built out of them.

Before we add anybody else, let's run the baseline. One blob, alone on the plane. Nobody to share with, so it takes both pieces every time it finds a pair. It survives, it reproduces, and the population climbs in a clean, steady line. This is the dumbest version of the world, and that's exactly why it's useful. We know what it does. It also tells us nothing about teamwork, because teamwork needs at least two blobs.

So let's add a second blob and let them meet at a pair. Two sharers split the food, and both walk home fine. Neither reproduces, though, because one piece isn't enough. Two greedy blobs tug at the same pair, and the fight burns so much energy that both end up with nothing. A greedy blob that meets a sharer does well. It takes one and a half pieces, and the sharer is left with half a piece.

Here is what those meetings are worth, and everything in this video comes back to these numbers. Sharer against sharer, each one gets 1.2 pieces, because nobody wastes energy on a fight. Greedy against greedy, each gets zero. Greedy against sharer, the greedy blob gets 1.5, and the sharer gets 0.5. Write those down, because we're going to need them.

Now notice the catch. If blobs wander the plane at random, a sharer is just as likely to meet a greedy blob as another sharer. In that random crowd, the greedy strategy wins. The sharers slowly disappear, and the plane fills up with greedy blobs fighting over pairs. We can watch it happen. Let's run a mixed field where the strategies are spread at random. Sharers fall behind, and teamwork never gets started.

So what would have to change for teamwork to win? The answer is who you meet. Imagine that instead of wandering alone, the blobs leave home in little puddles of siblings. A puddle goes out together, so when it reaches a pair, the siblings are the ones standing next to each other. Siblings came out of the same parent, so they tend to carry the same strategy. A sharer in a puddle of sharers mostly meets sharers. A greedy blob in a puddle of greedy blobs mostly meets greedy blobs.

That changes the arithmetic completely. A sharer's payoff is now mostly the sharer-against-sharer number, 1.2, with a small share of meetings against strangers. A greedy blob's payoff is mostly the greedy-against-greedy number, which is zero. Before we run it, though, let's make a prediction, because that's the only way to know whether we really understand the rule.

Here are three outcomes for a half-and-half start in the puddle world. Outcome one: the sharers die out, and the greedy blobs take the plane. Outcome two: sharers and greedy blobs settle into a stable mix. Outcome three: the sharers take over. Pick one, and hold onto it. Which one do you think the run will pick? Let's unpause and find out.

Okay, the run is going, and the answer is outcome three. The sharer puddles keep finding each other at the pairs, so they walk home with food, reproduce, and their puddles get bigger. The greedy puddles keep fighting each other at every pair and running out of energy. Within a few dozen days the sharers hold most of the plane. If you guessed outcome one, you're in good company, since that's what the random crowd does. The difference is entirely in who the blobs meet.

I'll admit something about this part. When I first set up this model, I expected the sharers to win outright, because they look like the nice option, and nice things feel like they should win. That guess was half right, for a boring reason. Niceness wins only inside the puddle, and the puddle is the whole mechanism. Dropping the idea that niceness should win on its own is most of the lesson.

That was a pretty extreme puddle, so let's ask how much siblings have to matter. Here's a ladder with four rungs, and each rung gets one number. The number is how often a blob's pair-mate is a sibling. Rung one is a loner, with a number of zero. Nobody to share with, so sharing never pays. Rung two is a pair of siblings, which puts the number at one half. Rung three is a crew of three, at about two thirds. Rung four is a crew of ten, at nine in ten.

Before we go on, check that the crew-of-ten result makes sense to you, because the next part depends on it. If nine in ten of your meetings are with siblings, sharing should win by a wide margin. If it doesn't, go back to the payoff numbers and find the step that doesn't add up.

Okay, let's run each rung and watch what sharing does. On rung one, sharing dies out right away, because there's nobody to share with. On rung two, sharers hang on for a while, but they fall behind the greedy blobs in about half the runs. On rung three, sharers win in most runs. On rung four, they win almost every time, and the greedy blobs get pushed to the edge of the plane, where they fight each other over the leftovers.

Now a caveat, and it's an important one. This is a blob world. Real animals don't live on a flat plane with one pair of food at a time, and they don't sort themselves into neat puddles of siblings. The model is a stripped-down picture of one mechanism, which is that relatives tend to live near each other. Real ecosystems have many more ways for cooperation to pay, and many more ways for cheats to get caught. Keep that in mind when we say what the model shows.

Here's the payoff, the reason any of this matters. Sharing evolved in this world because the payoffs have a built-in structure. The sharers that win are the ones whose meetings are mostly with other sharers. That's all teamwork is in this model: a strategy that pays off when your partners play it too. Nobody in the world has to know that. The arithmetic does the knowing. So we can call it teamwork, because that's the behaviour we watched spread.

Now a common objection, and it's a fair one. Doesn't teamwork need a plan? Doesn't some blob have to decide to be nice? Not in this world. Nothing in the rules tells a blob to plan. A blob is simply born sharing or greedy, and its descendants inherit the same habit. The planning happens in the numbers, over many generations, and no single blob is doing it in its head. That's the honest reading of every run we've made.

There's a wall, though, and it's where teamwork gets fragile. Imagine a greedy free rider that wanders into a sharing crew. It takes its pieces and never shares back, so it does better than its neighbors. Let's add a few of them and watch. At three in ten free riders, the crew holds. At five in ten, it breaks. Somewhere near four in ten, the sharers stop winning, and the crew falls apart.

That's the wall. Teamwork holds only while the cheaters stay a minority near the crew. Let's run it right at the edge. The difference between three in ten and five in ten looks small on the graph, but it's the whole story in two numbers, and it's worth remembering. It also tells us the claim has a limit, and we should say so out loud.

The same logic shows up well beyond blobs. Any time an action helps the group but costs the one who takes it, you need two things. The helpers have to find each other, and the cheaters have to stay rare enough that the helpers keep winning. Packs, herds, and even small human groups run on some version of that bargain. We'll come back to some of those in later videos, and we'll be careful about how far the blob version carries.

So here's where we ended up. Two blobs meet at a pair. Sometimes they share, and sometimes one grabs everything. Whichever habit leaves more copies behind is the habit that spreads. Nobody planned it, and nobody decided to be a team. The sharing that worked got copied, and the sharing that didn't work died out. Teamwork is just the leftover pattern of meetings that kept happening. Nobody planned it. The pair just kept showing up.
