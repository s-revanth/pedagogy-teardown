That is the start of Rome's navy, and it is one of the best examples of learning on the job in ancient history. Spoiler: Rome learned fast, and Carthage did not expect it.

So where were we? The First Punic War began in 264 BC, and it had moved out of the land and onto the sea, and Rome, which had built its power on legions marching over other people's fields, suddenly had a problem. It did not own any warships worth the name. Carthage did, and Carthage had been the sea power of the western Mediterranean for generations. If you wanted to win the war, you had to win the water, and Rome had never been good at that.

Here is what Rome did. It took a wrecked Carthaginian warship, a quinquereme with five banks of oars, and used it as a model. Rome then built a fleet, from scratch, in a rush. I like the idea of a navy built out of a shipwreck. Timber was cut, hulls were pinned together, and rowers were trained on land before they ever touched the sea. I find that remarkable. Most of the men who built those ships had never been at sea in their lives. How many ships did they build? According to the ancient historian Polybius, Rome built 100 quinqueremes and 20 triremes in a remarkably short time. Did they know what they were doing? Not at first. Did they learn quickly? Very much so. I would have been nervous boarding one of those ships, to be honest.

The fleet was not perfect. The ships were heavy, the rowers were clumsy, and the captains were learning the basics while everyone watched. But the Romans did something the Carthaginians did not expect. They added a new tool to the battle, which we will get to in a moment. Before that, let's take a breath and look at the scale of what they were attempting.

Building a fleet is not just about hulls. You need oars, rope, sails, and enough skilled men to row them in unison. You need harbours to keep the ships in, and you need food to keep the rowers alive while the ships are at sea. Rome had none of that infrastructure at the start. It had to invent it while fighting a war, which is hard enough in peacetime.

Your enemy owns the sea, and that is a problem you cannot ignore. Would you accept that as a fact, or would you start looking for a way to change it? Rome chose the second option, and it is worth asking why. The answer is that Rome did not think of itself as a naval power, and so it had nothing to lose by trying something strange.

The strange thing was the corvus. It was a boarding bridge, a long plank with a heavy spike at its end, mounted on the bow of a Roman ship. When the ships came close, the crew dropped the corvus onto the enemy deck, and the spike pinned it in place. Suddenly a sea fight became a land fight, with Roman soldiers charging across a plank into Carthaginian ships. If you were a Carthaginian captain, what would you think when you saw those planks come down? What would you do if one landed on your deck?

The first big test came at Mylae in 260 BC. The Carthaginian captains were confident, because they had been winning at sea for years. They sailed in expecting a familiar style of fight, where ships rammed each other and the faster crew won. They did not expect the corvus. When the Roman ships closed in and the planks came down, the Carthaginian line was hooked, boarded, and broken.

Carthage lost a large number of ships, and it lost them in a single afternoon. That is a bad day in any navy, and it was a shock to the Carthaginians, who had thought of themselves as the masters of this kind of war. Rome celebrated, and the commander who had designed the fleet's approach was honoured with a victory monument. I think that is a fair reward for an idea that worked. Did Carthage change its approach afterwards? Yes, but its captains were still used to the old style. That is the problem with being good at something for a long time. You stop asking whether the rules have changed. I have seen that pattern in plenty of other fields, and it is never pleasant to watch.

Just before that, at Ecnomus in 256 BC, more than 300 ships on each side fought what may be the largest naval battle of the ancient world. Rome won. Then Rome made an ambitious choice. It decided to take the war to Africa. In 256 BC, a Roman army under one of the two consuls, Regulus, crossed the sea, landed near Carthage, and began winning battles on land. The Carthaginians were rattled. Several of their cities on the coast fell or changed sides, and the war looked as if it might end within a year. The mood in Rome was ecstatic. Did anyone think the war would last another 15 years? Certainly not. You do not win a war in a single season, and Rome was about to learn that the hard way.

But Regulus made a serious mistake. He kept pressing his advantage, and he did not allow for the reinforcements that Carthage was quietly gathering. The Carthaginians hired a large force of Greek mercenaries, trained them, and then attacked Regulus's army with a combined strike of infantry and cavalry. The Roman army was defeated, and Regulus himself was captured. If you had been a general who had already won several battles, you would expect to be treated kindly. He was not.

What happened to Regulus next is one of the most famous stories in Roman history. According to later writers, he was sent back to Rome to negotiate a peace, promised to return to Carthage if the talks failed, and then went back to his death. The story is dramatic, and the sources for it are late and embellished. I would treat the details as a moral tale rather than a clean record, because the sources were writing with a clear lesson in mind.

After Regulus, the war became a grind. Rome lost a number of fleets to storms, and the sea was not forgiving to anybody. Each storm was a reminder that Rome's victories were fragile, and that a fleet on paper was not the same as a fleet at sea. How many ships were lost? Sources give different totals, and the most honest answer is that the losses were heavy. Would you keep paying for more ships after that? The Senate kept funding new fleets, which tells you how serious the situation was. Ships ran aground, rowers drowned, and the ones that survived had to be replaced. Rome rebuilt each time, which says a great deal about its stubbornness, and it also says a great deal about how much money and manpower the Romans were willing to spend.

Sicily became the main battlefield, and it stayed that way for years. I would not have wanted to be a sailor there. Carthage held a number of coastal strongholds, and Rome tried to take them one by one. The most important target was Lilybaeum, a fortified city on the western tip of the island. Rome laid siege to it, and the siege dragged on for years. The city refused to fall, and the Roman navy kept losing ships to the same unpredictable sea. How long can a siege last? Years, it turns out. The defenders were well supplied from the sea, and every time Rome sent a fleet to block them, the storms got in the way. I find that infuriating, and so did the Romans.

In 249 BC, the Roman admiral Publius Claudius Pulcher sailed out against the Carthaginian fleet at Drepana. Before the battle, the sacred chickens were said to have refused to eat, which was taken as a bad omen. Claudius reportedly ordered them thrown into the sea, saying that if they would not eat, perhaps they could drink. The battle was a disaster. Most of the Roman fleet was destroyed, and the admiral was disgraced. Why did the omen matter so much? The Romans took religion seriously, and a bad sign before a battle could shake an entire army. Did Claudius believe in the omen? Probably not, but he did not need to. The men around him did, and that was enough.

Then Hamilcar Barca took charge on the Carthaginian side. He was a bold commander who understood the value of a strong position, and he chose Mount Eryx, a steep hill above the town of Eryx in western Sicily. From there he could threaten Roman supply lines and resist every attack the legions could muster. For years, the two sides sat in a grinding stalemate. Did anyone expect it to last that long? Not really. The Romans assumed a few more campaigns would finish it, and the Carthaginians assumed the Romans would eventually give up. Both assumptions were wrong. Who blinks first in a standoff like that? Usually whoever has the most money left.

A stalemate is a tug of war where both teams are too tired to pull. I know that sounds odd, but it is how the years went. Rome could not dislodge Hamilcar, and Hamilcar could not drive the Romans off the island. Both sides kept sending men, money, and ships, and both sides kept losing them. I admire Hamilcar's nerve, even though I would not have wanted his job. Years on a hill is a long time to hold a position. The war looked like it would never end, and some Romans were starting to wonder whether it was worth the cost. I can understand why. Every year brought another funeral, another fleet, and another tax bill. Would you keep paying for a war that seemed to have no end? Many Romans said no, and the Senate had to work hard to keep them on board.

In 241 BC, the Roman consul Gaius Lutatius Catulus took a different approach. Instead of besieging cities, he went after the supply fleet that kept Carthage's forces in Sicily fed. He met it at the Aegates Islands, off the western coast, and won a decisive naval battle. The Carthaginian fleet was cut off, its supplies were lost, and Carthage was left with no realistic way to keep fighting in Sicily. How many ships did Carthage lose there? Sources vary, but the defeat was decisive enough that the war effectively ended. I think that is the moment when the sea stopped being Carthage's ally and became its enemy.

Carthage did what any exhausted power does at that point. It sued for peace. The settlement was harsh. Carthage gave up Sicily, agreed to pay a heavy indemnity to Rome over a number of years, and promised not to attack Rome's allies. If you were a Carthaginian senator, you would be furious. I have always found the terms harsh, and I think most people would. Modern estimates of the payment vary, and the exact figures depend on the source, so be careful with any precise number you see quoted online. What we can say is that the payments were spread over many years, and that the debt weighed on Carthage for a long time. Did the treaty make Rome safer? For a while, yes. Was it fair? That depends on whom you ask, and I think you should ask both sides.

I think the treaty tells us a lot about how this war ended. Neither side was completely defeated, but Carthage was exhausted, its treasury was drained, and its army in Sicily was stranded. Rome, meanwhile, had paid heavily in ships and men, but it had also learned how to fight at sea, and it had shown that its legions could be turned into marines. That lesson would matter for the next war.

The First Punic War lasted about 23 years, which is a long time in the 3rd century BC. It began over a dispute in Sicily, and it ended with Rome as the dominant power in the western Mediterranean. The city that had once been a land power had become a naval power in the process. I think that is the real story of this war. A land power learned the sea by force, and it never really went back. Do you think Rome would have stopped there? The answer is no, and the next chapter makes that clear.

There is a problem, however. Carthage did not accept the peace quietly. Its own mercenary army had been fighting the war on its behalf, and when Carthage could not pay them, the mercenaries revolted. That revolt turned into a brutal civil war, and it nearly destroyed Carthage from the inside. So the story is not over yet, and I promise the next part is even messier.
