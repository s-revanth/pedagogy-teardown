## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Within the next decade, machines can be trusted to choose their own targets, and you might not notice the moment it happens. Many weapons already have an automatic mode, and some can decide to fire faster than you can read this sentence. I think the question of who pulls the trigger has quietly become one of the most important debates in technology, and we should give it far more attention than we do.

Researchers and diplomats call these systems lethal autonomous weapons. The phrase means weapons that search for, select and attack targets without meaningful human control over each individual attack. You can hear the disagreement in Geneva, where delegates still argue about the exact definition. I think that disagreement tells us something, because if you cannot agree on the words, you will struggle to agree on the rules.

On the night of 26 September 1983, a Soviet early warning system reported that the United States had launched missiles. The officer on duty, Stanislav Petrov, judged the report a false alarm, and he was right. Put yourself in that chair. You have two minutes, a screen that says missiles are coming, and a rule that says report it up the chain. What would we expect a machine to do with the same report? I keep returning to that question, because Petrov's decision to wait was a human judgment.

Now imagine that alarm handled by software designed to respond within seconds. Speed compresses the time available for judgment. Once a decision is made in milliseconds, the people nominally in charge may only be confirming what a program already decided. If you were that person, you would be a checkpoint, not a decision maker, and checkpoints are easy to skip when everything is moving quickly. We should not pretend that is a minor change.

Automation is not new in war. A close-in defence system on a warship can be set to fire automatically at incoming missiles, because no person could react fast enough to a threat that arrives in seconds. I have never thought of a warship as a robot, but in that moment it is one. If you have watched footage of one firing, you will understand the speed. The line between automatic and autonomous is blurry, and that is where the real argument begins.

Concern grew after 2013, when a coalition of groups launched the Campaign to Stop Killer Robots. Diplomats began informal talks under the United Nations Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons in 2014, and those talks have continued for years without a binding treaty. Progress has been slow. We have seen this pattern with other weapons, and we should expect it to repeat unless the major powers agree on what must be banned.

In July 2015, more than a thousand researchers in artificial intelligence and robotics signed an open letter. They warned that autonomous weapons could start an arms race, and that cheap, scalable machines could reach the black market quickly. You do not need to be an engineer to see why that worried them. I think that letter mattered more than its authors expected, because it made scientists talk about the military uses of their own work.

Reports from the field now show what this can look like. A United Nations panel of experts described a small drone that, according to the panel, attacked retreating forces in Libya in 2020, without an operator directly controlling the strike. The report came out in 2021. The details are disputed, and it is hard to verify what anyone knew at the time. If you read the report yourself, you will see how carefully it hedges, and perhaps that caution is the right tone for the whole debate.

Consider what cheap drones and fast software can do together. One operator can manage twelve machines, which share what each one sees and coordinate an attack faster than any human commander could follow. None of that requires a breakthrough in artificial intelligence. It requires three things: reliable sensors, decent software and a willingness to accept mistakes at scale. If you add up those ingredients, you can see why the technology is spreading so quickly.

Defenders of these systems make serious arguments, and I want to take them seriously. Machines do not get angry, tired or afraid. If you have ever made a bad call while exhausted, you know how much clarity matters. Some argue that autonomy can reduce civilian harm if it is designed carefully and tested honestly, and I find that argument stronger than many critics admit.

The counterargument is about accountability and error. When a machine kills the wrong person, who is responsible? The programmer, the commander, the company that trained the model, or nobody at all? I find that gap troubling, because the law of war depends on a person who can be held to account. A machine that cannot be punished cannot be deterred, and that changes the calculation for everyone involved. We need a clear answer before the first serious mistake forces one on us.

There is a quieter risk too. Cheap autonomous weapons may lower the threshold for starting a conflict, because a government can send machines instead of soldiers and feel that the political cost is smaller. You can recognise the pattern from other technologies that made war look cheaper. That is an argument about incentives rather than technology, and it is one I think we underestimate.

Can any of this be verified? In practice, it is very hard. A drone that flies autonomously looks the same from above as one piloted remotely, and a software update can change its behaviour overnight. Inspectors would need access to code, logs and training data, and states rarely offer those. If you are a policymaker, you have to plan for that gap now, because verification is one of the biggest unsolved problems in this debate. We need to ask for that access before it is too late.

We can already see some of the answers. Most experts agree that meaningful human control should remain over any decision to take a life. Where they disagree is on what meaningful means. Does a person who approves a broad target set count as in control? Does a person with five seconds to veto a strike count as in control? If you were asked to write the rule yourself, what would you say? Those questions are not purely technical. They are about what kind of judgment we are willing to hand over, and to whom. I would add that the answer will differ by country and by mission.

So go back to the opening. Within the next decade, machines can be trusted to choose their own targets, and you will not notice the moment it happens. The real question is not whether the technology can work. It is whether we will still insist that a person, and not a program, carries the decision to kill. I think that answer will be settled over the coming years, and everyone following this debate, including you, has a say in it.
