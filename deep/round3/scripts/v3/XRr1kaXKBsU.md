## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Gravity is the force you feel every day, and several of the things most people believe about it are wrong. You feel it when you stand up, when you drop a cup and when you climb a staircase. It shapes the orbits of planets, the tides on the coast and the path of every thrown ball. Yet most of us learned a few half-truths about it at school, and we never checked them.

I want to start with a confession. For years I repeated these ideas without checking them, and I suspect many of you have done the same. Why would a lesson about falling objects stick so firmly? Part of the reason is that everyday evidence seems to agree with the myths. We feel pulled down, and we see things land. We rarely ask what would happen in a different setting, and that is exactly where the surprises are.

This video is sponsored by a language-learning app that gives you short daily lessons on your phone. If you want to practise a new language a few minutes at a time, you can look into it for yourself, and I would take a look.

Why do astronauts float if gravity is still there? I ask that question every time someone shows a video from the International Space Station, and I think the answer surprises most people. The station orbits roughly 400 kilometres above the surface. At that height, gravity is still around nine tenths as strong as it is on the ground. So the astronauts are not floating because gravity has switched off. They are floating because they are falling, all the time, and missing the Earth.

I think the most useful thing to do is test the claim before accepting it. We can ask how altitude changes the pull, and we can ask what happens in a lift that has been cut loose. Once we do that, the floating stops looking like a mystery. It becomes a clear consequence of falling, and I find that much more satisfying than the usual story.

That idea is called free fall, and you have already experienced a small version of it. If you have ever felt your stomach lift in a lift that drops quickly, you know the sensation. Everything inside the lift falls at the same rate, so nothing presses against the floor, and you feel weightless. An orbit is a fall that keeps going sideways fast enough that the ground curves away beneath it. The station is always falling toward the Earth, and always missing it.

Have you ever wondered why astronauts look so calm? I wondered that myself, and the answer is simple. Their bodies are falling with the station, so nothing presses on them the way the floor presses on you. We feel weight only when something pushes back, and in orbit very little does.

Now for a second belief that many people hold. Heavier objects fall faster, so a bowling ball should hit the ground before a feather. Legend says Galileo tested this idea by dropping weights from the Leaning Tower of Pisa, though the story may be embellished. What we know for certain is that, without air, a hammer and a feather fall together. In 1971, the astronaut David Scott dropped both on the surface of the Moon, and they reached the ground at the same moment. You can try a version of this at home, although the feather needs a vacuum to behave as Galileo predicted.

The next step came from Isaac Newton. In 1687, he published his law of gravity in the Principia, and it stated that the pull between two bodies depends on their masses and on the square of the distance between them. Double the distance, and the pull drops to a quarter. That inverse-square rule is why the Moon stays in orbit at its distance, and why a satellite in low orbit feels a much stronger pull than one far away. I find that simple rule remarkable, because it describes the Moon, the planets and a falling apple with one equation.

What would happen to the Moon if the pull were weaker? I think the answer is clear. It would drift off its path, and the tides would change with it. We can measure how far the Moon moves each year by bouncing lasers off reflectors left on its surface. That measurement moves about 3.8 centimetres a year, and it confirms the law in striking detail.

Newton's law also told us something about the Earth itself. In 1798, Henry Cavendish measured the tiny attraction between two lead spheres in his laboratory. From that small pull, he worked out the density of the Earth, and from that, its mass. The experiment was delicate, and the forces involved were minuscule. Yet Cavendish showed that gravity is measurable on a bench, not just in the sky. How would you measure a pull that small? That is a question scientists have been refining for more than two centuries.

A third misconception is about direction. People often say gravity pulls things down, and in everyday life that is a useful way to speak. But gravity actually pulls everything toward the centre of the Earth. Down is local. If you stood on the opposite side of the planet, your down would point in a different direction, which is the same direction that, for you, is up. Once you see it that way, the shape of the world feels much more connected.

Think about how much your weight would change if you travelled from the equator to the poles. You would notice a small difference only on a very precise scale. I would not expect it to change your day, but it does matter for satellites and for sea level. We should respect small effects, because they accumulate over large distances, and you will see them everywhere once you look.

Gravity also varies, though much less than people expect. Higher up, you are farther from the centre, so the pull is slightly weaker. The top of Mount Everest, for example, is about 0.3 percent weaker than sea level. Latitude matters too. Because the Earth spins and bulges at the equator, gravity at the poles is about half a percent stronger than at the equator. These differences are real, and surveyors and geophysicists measure them carefully, but they are small compared with the average.

Gravity is also the weakest of the four fundamental forces, and that surprises many people. Think about a fridge magnet holding a paper clip against the door. The magnet is pulling upward against the entire gravitational pull of the Earth, and it wins easily. The Earth has an enormous mass, but gravity between ordinary objects is so weak that electric and magnetic forces dominate almost everything at small scales. It is a striking thing to realise, and I find it humbling.

Why does such a weak force dominate the cosmos? I think it is because gravity only pulls, and it never cancels out. Masses add up, so the pull grows with size, which is how planets and stars gather in the first place. You can see the same effect in a galaxy, where the pull gathers material into vast structures. I find that a powerful idea, and it helps explain why gravity matters at large scales.

In 1915, Albert Einstein offered a very different picture. In his general theory of relativity, gravity is not a force in the old sense. It is the curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy, and objects move along the curved paths that result. That picture predicted the bending of starlight around the Sun, and it has passed every test so far. Most people never see it at work, but you rely on it every time you use a satellite.

I find this one of the most beautiful ideas in physics. A mass bends the space around it, and a falling object simply follows the straightest path it can. You may find that hard to picture, and I think that is normal. Even Einstein spent years working out the mathematics, so you should not expect the picture to come easily.

GPS is the everyday example. Satellites carry clocks that run at a slightly different rate from clocks on the ground, partly because of their speed and partly because they sit in weaker gravity. Without correction, the satellite clocks would gain about 38 microseconds a day. That sounds tiny, but it would add up to kilometres of positioning error within a single day. Engineers include relativity in the software, and the system works because the theory is right.

Gravity has even produced its own waves. In 2015, the LIGO detectors recorded ripples in spacetime from two black holes that had spiralled together far away. The signal was extraordinarily faint, and it took enormous effort to pull out of the noise. Yet it confirmed a prediction that Einstein made a century earlier. We can now listen to gravity itself, and that opens up a whole new way to study the universe.

What would you need to hear a gravitational wave? You would need a detector sensitive to a change smaller than a proton, spread across arms about 4 kilometres long. We do not hear those waves with ears, of course. We see them as tiny shifts in laser light, and that is how the signal was recovered from the noise.

The tides are a gravity story too. The Moon pulls on the oceans, and the Sun does as well, and the combined effect raises water on the side facing the Moon and on the far side. You see the result twice a day on most coastlines. It is one of the most familiar phenomena on Earth, and it comes from the same pull that holds the station in its orbit. The difference is only one of scale and geometry.

I like that the tides are so familiar. I think of them every time I walk along a beach, roughly twice a day, about 12 hours apart. It reminds me that a law written in a book shows up at the shoreline. You can check the times in any tide table, and that is a small demonstration of how far the theory reaches.

Still, something important is missing. We have a very good theory of gravity on large scales, and we have a very good theory of the very small, quantum mechanics. Physicists have not yet found a theory that unites them in a consistent way. Gravity is hard to fit into the quantum picture, and that gap is one of the biggest open questions in physics. I think it is one of the most exciting problems in science, precisely because we do not yet know the answer.

Why does this gap matter to you? I think it matters because gravity is the one force we experience every day, and yet it resists the tools that work for everything else. We are still searching for the bridge between the two theories, and I suspect the answer will surprise us.

So go back to the astronauts. They are not floating because gravity has gone. They are falling around the Earth, missing it at every moment, while gravity remains nearly as strong as it is on the ground. You can see the same force in a dropped cup, a rising tide and a satellite that keeps your phone on time. Why do astronauts float? Because they are always falling, and the Earth keeps curving away beneath them.
NARRATION>>>
