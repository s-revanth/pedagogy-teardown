## Narration

<<<NARRATION
For most of history, diseases crossed the Atlantic in one direction. Smallpox went over, and so did measles, influenza, and a long list of other illnesses that had spent centuries circulating in Europe, Asia, and Africa. Almost nothing came back the other way. The record shows that this lopsided traffic killed a huge share of the people already living in the Americas, and it often did so before those people ever saw a European face to face.

So why only one direction? You may have heard the usual explanation, that Europeans were tougher or that the Americas were some kind of healthy paradise. That explanation is wrong, and you can do much better than it. The real answer is about animals, crowds, and time. It is less dramatic than a ship full of plague, but it is far more useful, because it tells you what kinds of places produce diseases in the first place, and why some places never did.

Start with what a disease actually needs. A germ is a passenger, and it needs a ride from one body to the next. It also needs enough bodies around to keep those rides going, because a germ that runs out of riders dies out. Many germs are perfectly happy in only one species, and they stay put inside it for their whole existence. The troublemakers are the ones that can jump from one species to another, and scientists call a disease that jumps from animals to people a zoonosis. A large share of the infections that affect people today started out in animals.

Consider how a germ moves through a set of transit routes. A passenger who can only ride one bus will visit the same few stops again and again, and that is the end of its travels. A passenger who can hop onto a bus full of new riders, then onto a train, then onto a ship, can reach almost anywhere you can name. The germs that mattered most in history were that second kind of traveler, and they had a great many buses to hop onto. So the real question becomes where those buses were, and who was riding them.

Now, we can take a village of about a hundred people, with a few pigs and some chickens moving through the yards at all hours of the day. The animals carry germs that people have never met before, and people carry a few germs that have never before been seen in animals. Every so often, someone in the village gets sick after handling a pig, or after a chicken pecks at a child. Most of the time nothing spreads far, because the village is small and the germ is likely picky about its hosts. But occasionally a germ learns to live in human bodies, and once it does, it stops needing the animal at all. That first jump is the hard part, and it happens where people live close to animals.

So which parts of the world had lots of animals to live close to? This is where Eurasia pulls far ahead. Over thousands of years, people there domesticated cows, pigs, goats, sheep, and horses. Every one of those species is a potential source of germs, and each one sat right beside human families in the barns and yards. The Americas had llamas and alpacas in the Andes, guinea pigs, dogs, and turkeys in Mexico. That is a much shorter list, and it has no cows, no pigs, and no horses at all. The horses that once roamed North America had disappeared thousands of years before anyone domesticated them on the other side of the world.

Count it out and the imbalance is stark. Eurasia had far more large animals that could be domesticated, and therefore far more chances for a germ to make the jump into people. So it should not surprise you that the germs that jumped came mostly from the Old World. The easiest way to put it is as a bank account. Eurasia kept making deposits, one domesticated species at a time, while the Americas made very few. Over ten thousand years, those deposits added up to a very large balance, and the balance eventually came due.

The effect shows up clearly in the story of 1520. Smallpox arrived in Tenochtitlan with a Spanish expedition. The city was enormous, with a population that may have reached a couple of hundred thousand people, and the germ spread through that crowd with terrible speed. The ruler who had only just taken the throne is believed to have died of it, and much of the city fell ill at the same time. Many historians argue that the epidemic weakened the city badly before the final siege. The germ probably did more of the work than the soldiers did.

Notice the crowd, though. Tenochtitlan was not a small village, and it was still hit hard. That raises the next piece of the puzzle, which is that some germs need a large population to keep going. Researchers who model measles estimate that it needs a population in the hundreds of thousands to keep circulating. Below that size, the virus runs out of people who have not already had it, and it fades out. Above that size, it keeps coming back, year after year, and you cannot easily wipe it out.

That is why we keep coming back to size and connection. Eurasia had big cities and long trade routes linking them, so germs could move from one crowd to the next and keep going. The Americas had big cities too, but the networks between them were far less connected, and the whole continent sat across an ocean from everyone else. A germ that cannot find a fresh crowd to infect is a germ that eventually dies out. For a very long time, the Americas were a set of isolated crowds, and that kept the germs trapped on the far side of the water.

We need to be careful here, because it is easy to swing too far the other way. The lopsided story does not mean Indigenous Americans were weaker. It does not mean their immune systems were somehow less prepared, in any simple sense. Their populations had their own diseases, their own medicines, and their own ways of living on the land, and those ways were sophisticated and well adapted. They were hit so hard because they had almost no history of exposure to the germs arriving on those ships. That is an accident of geography and animals, and it is not a verdict on any people.

Now, we should admit there is one famous candidate for the other direction. Syphilis is the usual name in this conversation. Some researchers argue it came from the Americas to Europe after 1492, and that it spread across Europe quickly in the years that followed. Others argue it was already present in Europe, and that the outbreak only looked new in the written record. Historians and biologists are still arguing about it. I think that honest disagreement is the right place to leave it, because one contested case does not overturn the overall pattern. It just makes the pattern messier.

We should also be honest that one-way traffic does not mean zero traffic. Some germs did cross back, and some of those crossings mattered. The lesson is not a clean rule that never breaks. It is a tendency, shaped by the animals each side had and by the networks that connected them. That tendency is strong, and it explains most of the record, but history almost never hands us tidy rules. If somebody tells you it does, they are probably trimming the messy parts, and you should be suspicious of how neat the story sounds.

Now, this is not only a story about the past. Modern travel moves germs in both directions, and it does so quickly. A respiratory virus can leave one continent and reach another in a day or two, and it does not care where the ships came from. The old pattern held because the ships were slow, the networks were thin, and the species that spread germs had been domesticated mostly in one part of the world. Change the networks, and the direction changes too. You have seen that happen more than once in the last few generations.

So what does this mean for you? Think about what would have to be true for a germ to travel both ways evenly. You would need animals living close to people on both sides of the ocean. You would need crowds big enough to keep the germ going, and enough traffic to carry it back again. The Americas had crowds in some places, but not the animals. Eurasia had the animals, the crowds, and the trade routes as well. Which of those do you think was the real bottleneck? Most people guess the crowds, and you may well be one of them.

Look at what the list tells us about the world. The diseases that crossed in one direction were not random. Many of them came from animals that people in the Old World lived alongside every day, some of them domestic and some of them wild animals that shared the same ground. They were carried by people who lived in crowds next to those animals, and then they were carried across the water. That is the pattern. The passengers were a product of the barnyard, and the barnyard was a product of which animals each part of the world happened to have.

It also explains why history gets strange when you look at it through disease. A single species domesticated thousands of years ago can end up shaping which empires survive a particular century. Nobody planned that outcome. Nobody in the village with the pigs knew they were starting a chain of events that would one day reach a city on the other side of the planet. They were just living next to animals, and they had no idea what would come of it. You can see the chain only because you can look back on it.

So check the numbers yourself, since you can. Count the large animals that were domesticated in each part of the world, and the gap is obvious. Count the groups of people who lived in dense settlements close to those animals, and the gap is obvious again. Then count the trade routes linking those settlements, and you see why germs kept moving in one direction. Each count points the same way, and each one is a fact you can look up for yourself.

So go back to the start. For most of history, diseases crossed the Atlantic in one direction, and that pattern shaped the lives of millions of people. The reason is in the barnyard, in the crowds, and in the long, slow history of which animals people chose to keep. The lopsided list is what you get when only one side of the world has a large supply of passengers. And the passengers came from the barnyard all along.
NARRATION>>>
