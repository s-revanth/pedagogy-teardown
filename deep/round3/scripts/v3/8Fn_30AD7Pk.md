## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Every highway in the United States has a number, and most people never stop to think about what that number is for. I used to assume it was just a name, like a label on a jar. It is not. A highway number is a set of coordinates. Read it properly, and a road tells you a great deal before it comes into view.

Start with the obvious fact. Names are arbitrary. A road can be called Main Street, or the old road to the lake, and the name tells you almost nothing about where it goes. A number can be different. If the numbering is done well, it carries information a driver can use while still watching the road. So the real question is how that information got packed into the digits, and why it was built that way.

Think back to what drivers had before. A road might carry a state number, a county name, a local nickname, and whatever the gas station painted on its sign. Nothing was consistent from one state to the next. If someone wanted to cross the country, they had to keep a stack of maps on the passenger seat and hope the signs agreed with them. I remember that kind of trip well, and it was miserable. By the middle of the fifties, the country was ready to fix the mess.

The big change came with the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956. That law authorized the Interstate system, a network of limited-access highways meant to connect the country's major cities. It paid for the roads, but the roads still needed numbers. In 1957, state highway officials put together a numbering plan, and that plan is the reason a sign can tell you something about the road beneath it. The shield on the sign is a distinct red, white and blue design, so the system announces itself. If you have ever wondered why the highways in one part of the country feel so different from another, the answer starts in those years.

Here is the core rule, and it is simple. Routes running east to west get even numbers. Routes running north to south get odd numbers. So an even number means the road crosses the country, and an odd number means it runs up and down. That one bit of information cuts the uncertainty in half before anyone has even looked at a map.

The second rule is about position. Numbers climb as you move in one direction. East-west routes start with the low numbers in the south and climb as they go north. North-south routes start with the low numbers in the west and climb as they go east. So the number tells you more than odd or even. It also places the road in general. A low even number is a southern road, and a high even number is a northern one. A high odd number tends to sit out east, near the Atlantic.

Why go to this trouble? The planners wanted people to plan trips without constantly unfolding a map. Need to go east or west? Look for even numbers. Need to go north or south? Look for odd numbers. Then the size of the number tells you where to look next. If you are driving at highway speed, you cannot stop and unfold a map. Good route choices can be made while still moving, and that was exactly the situation the planners had in mind.

Think of a small country laid out as a grid. It has four roads running east to west and four running north to south. Number the east-west roads with even numbers, climbing from the bottom edge, and number the north-south roads with odd numbers, climbing from the left. The map is now written entirely in numbers. Every intersection is a pair, a horizontal road and a vertical road. How do you find a spot in a grid with no map? You can find any spot without ever seeing the country.

That is what a coordinate system does. It turns a place into a pair of numbers, so that the place can be found, described, and compared with others. Real Interstate numbers are less tidy than that little country, but they follow the same logic. A driver who reads the number learns which way the road heads before a single sign appears. If you have ever tried to describe a location to a friend over the phone, you already understand the basic idea.

Now the real world has exceptions, and they are worth discussing. Interstates have three-digit numbers too, and those are usually branches or bypasses of a main route. A three-digit highway generally shares its last two digits with the route it connects to. The I-495 loop around Washington shares them with I-95, and the I-405 bypass in Los Angeles shares them with I-5. That is useful information, but it has to be read a little differently. So when you see a three-digit number on a sign, check its final digits against the route you already know.

The rules for three-digit numbers have their own details, and I will not pretend to have memorized every one. Some loops follow one pattern, and some spurs follow another. Some routes break the usual pattern entirely. Then there are the older US highways, which follow a similar scheme but predate the Interstates and do not line up with them neatly. Treat the grid as a strong guide, not an iron law.

A common belief is that the numbers were handed out in the order the roads were built. That sounds reasonable, and it is wrong. Why would planners do that? Because position mattered more than age. The numbers were assigned to fit the grid, not the calendar. An early road can carry a high number, and a late road can carry a low one. Assume the numbers track construction dates, and you will read the map backwards.

On one trip, I drove east across several states with the numbers on the signs as my only guide. I did not check the map once. Every time an even number went by, I knew the road was heading across the country. Every time an odd number came up, I knew it was running north or south. It was a small thing, but it made the drive feel less like guessing and more like reading. You would be surprised how often a number on a sign turns out to be the most useful thing you read all day.

The system is not perfect, and it does not have to be. A sign that shows the direction of travel at a glance is a modest thing, but it removes a whole category of small decisions. If you have driven for hours, you know how much those small decisions add up. The numbers do their work in the background, which is exactly where good design belongs.

Several other countries use grid-style numbering too, although the details vary. The core idea is the same. The name tells you where a road is, and anyone with a little knowledge can use the number as a compass. Nobody needs to memorize every rule to benefit from the basic pattern. Most of the time, the pattern is enough to get you where you are going.

So here is the question to carry along. Next time a sign goes by, which way is the road heading? Is the number low or high, and is it even or odd? How far does that tell us the road sits from the edge of the country? Which direction does the number point, and what could we learn if we read it the way the planners meant?

The planners were solving a simple problem. They wanted people to read the country without stopping for a map. Whether anyone uses the system deliberately or not, every number passed on a trip quietly offers a little more of the country for free. That is a small design choice doing a lot of work, and its fingerprints are on every road we drive.

So go back to the start. Highway numbers aren't names. They're coordinates, written so that a driver can read the country while moving through it.
NARRATION>>>
