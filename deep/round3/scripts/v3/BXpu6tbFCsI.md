<<<NARRATION
This video is brought to you by a privacy app that keeps your internet activity away from people who would rather see it. Use the link below to get a discount on a yearly plan, which is a good idea anyway. Okay, back to the birds.

"Sir, we have a problem." "Is it the Germans?" "No, sir, it is the birds." "What birds?" "All of them, sir, and there are about twenty thousand." "Right, well, get me some machine guns." "Sir, what exactly are we shooting at?" "Birds." "Yes, sir. Birds."

I wish I were making that up, but that was an actual military plan, and the birds, spoiler, won.

So let's go back to 1918, when the First World War was over and Australia had a people problem that nobody had planned for in the trenches. Hundreds of thousands of men were coming home, and they expected work, a house, and something close to peace after four brutal years of fighting. So the government made them a promise: they would be given land, lots of it, to grow wheat on. What could possibly go wrong?

The chosen spot was the Campion district in Western Australia. It was a huge, flat, dry stretch of scrubby bush that somebody decided was perfect farmland, even though it was mostly scrub and dust. Many of the new blocks went to men who had never farmed a single day in their lives. In fairness, they had just spent years in a trench. So a quiet field looked like a fresh start to most of them, and you can see why.

Here is how being a new farmer works. You clear the scrub with a borrowed axe and a lot of stubbornness, you plant the wheat, and then you wait. Then comes the loan repayment, and the crop is finally ready, only to find that the price has collapsed. In 1929 the world fell into the Great Depression, and suddenly nobody could afford to buy much of anything, so wheat that should have paid the bills barely paid for the sacks it came in. Many farmers had borrowed money to buy their blocks. The interest kept ticking over, whether the crop was good or not.

So the farmers were already having a terrible time, and then, just when things seemed as bad as they could get, the emus showed up.

Emus are big flightless birds that stand taller than most adults, and they can run at around fifty kilometres an hour while looking extremely pleased with themselves. They are also curious, stubborn, and very good at finding food. When the breeding season ended, the flocks wandered out of the bush and discovered that the farmers had planted an enormous buffet. The birds found this delightful, and the farmers found it considerably less so. Emus also travel in loose groups. They shift as a group whenever the feeding gets better. That is more than can be said for most armies.

Consider the situation from the farmhouse porch. Every penny has gone into clearing the land, a family needs feeding, and a bank is not particularly patient about any of it. A flock walks into the paddock, eats the harvest, flattens the fences, and wanders off to the next field. Then it comes back the next day, looking for more. Nobody on that farm was laughing. If you were the farmer, you would not be either.

The farmers complained to the government, which asked what exactly they wanted done. The farmers said, kill the emus, and the government said, fine. Here is the thing about governments, though. They love a plan, a budget, and a committee. I have sat through enough meetings to understand the appeal. Somebody had to decide whether the birds were an agricultural problem or a military one. Nobody wanted to be the person who said it was a bird problem. So the matter went to the army, which had guns and was happy to be useful. Why would anyone think that would work? Fences were discussed. They were mostly ruled out. A fence long enough to matter costs more than a bad harvest.

In late 1932, the army sent a unit to deal with the problem, under a commander named Major G.P.W. Meredith. His equipment included two Lewis machine guns, a small team of soldiers, a truck, and a great deal of confidence. Nobody had told the emus about that confidence, so the emus were not impressed. Did anybody ask the emus? The soldiers were not having a great time either. Standing in the heat for hours was not what most of them had signed up for. Cleaning jammed guns was worse. I would not have enjoyed it either.

Inside the camp, the conversation went roughly like this. "Sir, the birds are back." "Are they? How many?" "They have brought friends, sir." "Right, well, we have two guns." "Sir, the friends number several thousand." "Two guns, sergeant. Two guns."

The plan was simple: find the birds, set up the guns, shoot the birds, and repeat as necessary. I like a plan with clear steps, and this one had three of them. That is exactly why it was so painful to watch the second step fall apart. Here we go.

The emus did not stand still for the guns. When the soldiers approached, the birds scattered in every direction. Some ran north, some ran south, and a few just staring at the soldiers as if they were waiting for an explanation that nobody was going to get. The guns opened fire, the birds scattered even further, and then one of the guns jammed.

A Lewis gun is a light machine gun, which makes it easy to carry and not at all easy to keep working. It fires from a round drum magazine on top, and drums do not like dust, heat, or hurried fingers. The Australian bush has all three in generous quantities. The gun was designed to hold a line against soldiers, not to chase a fast bird across a paddock.

Then the other gun jammed too, which is a bad afternoon for anybody who has tried to fix a machine gun in a hurry. Meanwhile, the emus regrouped at a distance, walked on, and got back to eating. The soldiers chased them in a truck. That worked well until the truck was faster than some birds and slower than others, which is a difficult thing to plan around. The emus, to be fair, were running at full speed and did not appear to take it personally.

Let us pause to appreciate the emu. Standing next to a grown man, it is taller in every way that matters. It runs with long strides, turns quickly, and has a bad habit of looking straight at the threat before it leaves. Now try to draw a bead on that with a heavy gun while a truck bounces underneath, and it is a lot harder than it sounds on a recruitment poster.

At the end of the first attempt, the army had killed a small number of birds, wasted a good amount of ammunition, lost time, and learned something useful, namely that emus are very hard to shoot when they run in different directions. So what do you do when your guns keep failing? Do you give up? Do you ask the birds nicely? Do you buy better guns? Meredith's answer was to come back with more ammunition and try again, which, if I am honest, is the most stubborn military strategy I have ever come across.

The second attempt came a few weeks later. It had more ammunition, a slightly better plan, and the same two guns, and it also brought a little more luck. Some of the birds stopped near the guns, and the guns did what guns do, while others ran for the scrub and survived to annoy the farmers for another season.

Why not simply stop? Backing out looks like losing. Nobody in that room wanted to be the one who said it, and you would have felt the same. Let me ask you a question: if you were the major, what would you change? Be honest, because I certainly would want more guns, or a better plan, or maybe a different target. Keep that feeling in your head, because the army was about to try two of those things, and the results were not kind to either.

And that is where the story gets stuck. The emus kept coming back. They walked through the same fields in the same loose lines, and the soldiers kept finding them. Every time the army thought it had made a dent, the flock reappeared somewhere else on the map, like trying to empty a bathtub that keeps refilling from a tap nobody can see. Could it ever end?

That is twenty thousand birds, each one capable of running at fifty kilometres an hour, grazing through the wheat for breakfast. The size of the problem did not shrink because somebody brought a machine gun. If anything, the machine guns made the birds more interested in the trucks.

Now, a quick note on the numbers, because the numbers are a mess. Different accounts give different kill counts, with some saying a few hundred birds and others saying around a thousand. The honest answer is that nobody counted perfectly, and the birds were not filling in forms. So I would treat any precise number in this story with a little suspicion, including the ones I just used. Some of these figures get repeated without a source, so be wary.

By the end of the campaign, the army had spent a great deal of money and effort. It had shot a number of birds, but not all of them, and the flock barely noticed the difference. Eventually the soldiers packed up and went home. The emus went back to the wheat. The farmers were left with the damage, the debt, and a flock that had won the argument. Some families walked away from their blocks. The land went back to scrub.

The newspapers had a wonderful time with it. The phrase Emu War caught on, making the birds sound like a serious opponent, which, in fairness, they were. Some readers laughed. Some of the farmers did not find it funny at all, because the bills were still due and the birds had not left. The story has been retold ever since. Usually it comes with more feathers than the original.

I think this is one of my favourite stories in the series, mostly because nobody involved comes out looking great. There is a bigger point underneath the comedy. A government sent trained soldiers with machine guns against a wildlife problem that grew out of a bad economy and an ambitious land plan. The soldiers did what they were told. The plan itself was the real mistake. The people who paid for it were the farmers who had been promised a fresh start. I find that sad, honestly, and a bit ridiculous.

So the emus won. Nobody gets a medal for that, and nobody ever will. Next time we look at another mini-war, and I promise it is a little less feathery and a lot more human, so stay with us. Thanks for watching, and I will see you then.
