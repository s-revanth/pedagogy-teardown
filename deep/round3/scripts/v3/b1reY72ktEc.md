<<<NARRATION
This video is brought to you by a privacy app that keeps your internet activity away from people who would rather see it. Use the link below to get a discount on a yearly plan, which is a good idea anyway. Okay, back to Russia.

"Hey Nikolai, the bread is gone again." "Gone where?" "Gone, Nikolai. Ask the queue." "Then the Tsar will fix it." "The Tsar is at the front, playing with his toy soldiers." "Then who is fixing it?" "Nobody. That is the problem." "Right, then we fix it." "Who is we?" "Everyone, Nikolai. Everyone."

That is roughly how the Russian Revolution started, minus the soldiers and the pastries. Spoiler: the Tsar does not get his job back.

Let's go back to February 1917, or March by the Western calendar, and we will start in the queues. because Russia was still using the Julian calendar, which ran about two weeks behind everybody else. Petrograd, the capital, was cold, hungry, and angry. Factories were shutting down, bread queues were getting longer, and the war with Germany had already cost the country millions of lives. I think that is the best way to describe the mood, a city where everybody was waiting for something to break. I have read a lot of accounts of that winter, and the same image keeps coming back. People standing in line for hours in the cold, with little more than a ration card and a hope that the next shop would still have flour. You would be angry too. Would you wait in that queue for a fourth day? Most people did not have a choice, which is exactly why the anger spread so fast.

On International Women's Day, women marched through the streets demanding bread and an end to the war. Workers joined them, then more workers joined them, and soon the protest was far bigger than anybody in charge had planned. The police were ordered to stop the crowds. Then the soldiers, who had been sent to shoot the crowds, started refusing. It is hard to overstate how much this mattered. A government can survive a crowd, and it can survive a riot. It cannot survive an army that stops listening. Why did the soldiers stop listening? Many of them were peasants in uniform, and their families were the ones standing in those bread queues. I find that hard to ignore.

If you were a soldier ordered to fire on your own neighbours, what would you do? Most of them decided they would not do it. Within days the Tsar's troops had switched sides, and Nicholas II had no army left that would obey him. He abdicated on 15 March, and a royal dynasty that had lasted for three centuries was over in a single week. Nicholas wrote in his diary that day, and the entries were calm and very short. You might think a monarch would make a grand gesture, but the abdication was signed in a railway carriage, in a few quiet minutes.

A Provisional Government took over, promising elections and a new constitution. But it had a rival. The Petrograd Soviet, a council of workers and soldiers, had its own power and its own opinions, so the country ended up with two governments sharing one capital. Historians call this dual power, which is a polite phrase for a fight nobody had agreed to. Why not just pick one? The Soviet controlled the soldiers and the railways, and the Provisional Government controlled the paperwork. Neither side could afford to lose the other, and neither could afford to win outright. I think this is one of the most useful things to understand about 1917.

I should be fair to the Provisional Government, because its job was almost impossible. It wanted to keep fighting Germany, which made the soldiers furious. It also wanted to build a democracy, which takes years of patient work that nobody in wartime had. Every decision it made upset somebody, and every delay gave its rivals another chance to talk. I know it is easy to criticise a government from the comfort of a century later, and I am not going to pretend the job was easy. But you cannot run a war, a famine, and a democracy at the same time with the same tired ministers. You could not do it either.

Then Lenin came back. In April 1917, he arrived at the Finland Station in Petrograd, after the Germans allowed him to cross their territory in a sealed train. They hoped he would cause trouble for Russia, and he did. He arrived with a set of ideas that were far simpler than the Provisional Government's, and far easier to shout from a balcony. Who was he, really? The son of a civil servant, a sharp writer, and a man who had spent years in exile arguing with other exiles about the correct reading of Marx. He did not look like a revolutionary leader in a photograph. He looked like a man who was very sure he was right, and that is often more dangerous.

"Comrades, the war must end." "Who said that?" "Lenin, just back from Switzerland." "Did he bring any bread?" "He brought slogans." "What slogans?" "Peace, land, and bread. That is three things, and we want all of them."

You can see the appeal, and we can see why it worked. Peace would end the fighting, land would go to the peasants, and bread would feed the cities. The Bolsheviks had a slogan that fit on a poster, which is not a small skill in a country where many people could not read the paperwork. I would argue that the slogan did more for them than any single speech.

In July, a wave of protests turned violent in Petrograd. The Bolsheviks were blamed for the trouble, their offices were raided, and their leaders were hunted. Lenin fled to Finland, a little embarrassed and a lot more careful. For a few weeks it looked as though the Bolsheviks were finished, and some of their opponents were already writing their obituaries. They were wrong about that. Why did the Bolsheviks survive? We can point to a few reasons. Partly because their opponents kept underestimating how much anger was sitting in the cities. Partly because Lenin was prepared to act when others hesitated. I think that is the core of it. Would you have done the same? I hope you never have to find out.

In August, the army commander Kornilov marched on Petrograd to restore order, or so he said. The Provisional Government panicked, asked the Bolsheviks for help, and suddenly the people who had been jailed a few weeks earlier were handing out rifles to defend the capital. Kornilov's march collapsed without a major battle, and the Bolsheviks came out of the crisis with more weapons and more credibility than they had before. The men who had been plotting against the Bolsheviks were now asking them for rifles. Politics in 1917 moved fast, and nobody had a map that stayed accurate for more than a few weeks. That is the irony.

By October, the Bolsheviks were the most popular party in the big cities. Lenin came out of hiding and argued that the time had come to seize power. Some of his colleagues thought this was reckless, and they said so, loudly and in writing. Lenin did not care. He wanted the government, and he was willing to take it. What did the ordinary soldier want? Mostly, he wanted to go home. What did the ordinary worker want? Mostly, he wanted bread and a voice in his factory. The Bolsheviks spoke directly to both of those hopes, and you can understand why that message travelled so well.

On the night of 24 to 25 October by the old calendar, which is 6 to 7 November by ours, the Bolsheviks took the key points in Petrograd. The Winter Palace, where the Provisional Government was meeting, fell with surprisingly little fighting. The ministers were arrested, and the Bolsheviks announced a new government. Its early decrees were written in a rush, and I do not think anyone had a full plan for the next six months. Did they expect the war to last? They certainly did not expect it to last as long as it did.

- "Yes."

- "They were, until a few minutes ago."
- "Oh, dear. Well, we are taking over now."

A quick word on the famous storming. The film version has a dramatic siege, with cannon fire, shattered windows, and a crowd pouring through the gates. What really happened was messier, quieter, and in parts a bit staged. Some of the most famous images were made later, so treat them with suspicion.

The Bolsheviks then promised an election for a Constituent Assembly, a body that was supposed to write a constitution for the country. The vote in November 1917 was a shock to them. The Socialist Revolutionaries won the most seats, and the Bolsheviks came well behind. Lenin did not accept the result, and the democratic experiment lasted a single day. In January 1918, the Assembly met once, and the Bolsheviks sent in guards and shut it down. Why does this matter? Because it set a pattern. Every time an independent body got in the way, it was pushed aside, and the pushing became easier with practice. You can trace that habit forward for decades.

Then came the peace question. In March 1918, the Bolsheviks signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk with Germany, giving up a huge amount of territory to end the fighting. Many Russians were horrified, and some Bolsheviks were horrified too. Lenin thought it was the only way to survive. He was right about the survival part, and the price was enormous. The treaty cost Russia a large share of its western lands and industry. How do you justify that to a country that had just overthrown its king for bread? Many people found it impossible. The Bolsheviks argued that peace was the only way to save the revolution, and they were not wrong that Germany was about to collapse anyway.

Then came the Civil War, which lasted from 1918 until 1922 and was a messy patchwork of fronts. The Reds, led by Trotsky's Red Army, fought the Whites, a loose coalition of anti-Bolshevik forces, and several foreign armies joined in for their own reasons. Every side needed food, boots, and a reason to keep going. Nobody had enough of any of them. Look at it from an ordinary family's point of view. Your father is at the front, your town has no bread, and the nearest train is either full or not running. What is the plan for next week? Nobody could give a clear answer, and that uncertainty is what made the violence so widespread.

The Bolsheviks answered with War Communism, which meant requisitioning grain from the peasants. The peasants answered by hiding it. That is a conversation that does not end well for anybody. Cities starved, the countryside resented the state, and the state responded with more force. You may wonder how the Bolsheviks kept an army together. The answer is terror, backed by a secret police force called the Cheka, which kept a very close eye on everyone.

In July 1918, the former Tsar and his family were executed in Yekaterinburg. The local decision was taken with the knowledge of the central leadership, and the fear was that White forces might rescue the family. The bodies were hidden, and the remains were not confirmed until 1991. I find that detail chilling, because it shows how much of the revolution was run in secret, even at its most brutal moments.

By 1921, the Bolsheviks had the upper hand in most of the country, but the cost was staggering. Industry had collapsed, the towns were hungry, and the countryside was exhausted. The famine of 1921 to 1922 killed millions of people, especially along the Volga. The state survived, but it survived in a shape that nobody had planned, with a single party, a secret police, and a leadership that had learned to fear its own shadows. Historians still argue about how much of that was inevitable, and how much was chosen. I do not think there is a single answer, and I would be suspicious of anyone who says there is. Could a different leader have changed the path? The question is still open.

So the Bolsheviks won the war, and they lost most of their original ideals along the way. The revolution that began with bread queues ended with a state that controlled almost every aspect of life. How do you run a country after a war like that? Who inherits a mess this large? Those are the questions for the next part, and the answer is a man who was far more patient than Lenin, and far more dangerous.

We have a lot of ground to cover. Next time we look at that man, and I promise it is a darker chapter. Thanks for watching, and I will see you then.
