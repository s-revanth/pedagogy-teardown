## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Put a hand flat on any table and it feels solid. It is firm, it pushes back, and it would hurt if you slammed a palm into it. Yet that solid part turns out to be mostly an agreement between our brains and the world. Zoom in far enough and the table becomes a cloud of atoms, and those atoms are mostly empty space. So is anything real, or is everything we call real just a useful story we tell ourselves about the world? That is the question for today, and it is harder than it sounds.

Begin with the feeling of solidity. If an atom were blown up to the size of a football stadium, its nucleus would be about the size of a pea sitting at the center of the field. Everything else would be electrons, and those electrons are not little balls orbiting like planets, but clouds of probability spread through the space around the nucleus. So when a hand meets the table, what is actually pushing back? It is electrical repulsion between the outer layers of electrons, and that is a force, not a surface.

That already makes me suspicious, because something can be completely real in the sense that it does things, and still not be the solid thing we imagined. So maybe the test is not whether something looks solid, but whether it pushes back. Kick a stone and it hurts your toe. That tells everyone something about the stone that no argument can wave away, no matter what anyone believes about it.

There is a famous story about this. In the 1700s, the Irish philosopher George Berkeley argued that the material world exists only as perceptions in the mind. The writer Samuel Johnson reportedly kicked a large stone and said, I refute it thus. The story was probably dressed up over time, but it captures the idea perfectly, since the stone pushes back. Did it actually refute Berkeley? Not really, and that is exactly where the fun starts.

Around 2,400 years ago, Plato described people chained in a cave, watching shadows on a wall. They believed those shadows were everything there was, so their world felt complete. If you were one of those prisoners, the shadows would be the whole universe, and you would have no reason to doubt them. When one of them finally turns around and sees the fire and the objects casting the shadows, the light hurts his eyes. The shadows suddenly look like the lesser thing. I like this story, but notice what it assumes. It assumes there is a real world behind the shadows, and that a careful person can eventually reach it.

In 1641, René Descartes took the doubt even further by asking what would happen if a powerful trickster were feeding him every sensation he had. Maybe the sun, the trees, and his own hands were all created to deceive him. Maybe he was dreaming, with every dream feeling just as vivid as waking life. Descartes found one thing he could not doubt, which was that he was the one doing the doubting. From that small starting point he tried to build everything else back up, one careful step at a time.

Modern versions of that trickster are more concrete. In 1981, the philosopher Hilary Putnam described a brain floating in a vat, wired to a computer that feeds it experiences. If you were that brain, you would have no way to tell the difference. In 2003, the philosopher Nick Bostrom argued that if future civilizations can run detailed simulations of their own past, then it is quite possible that we are living in one right now. Is that a problem? I am not sure it is, because if the simulation is detailed enough to push back when a stone is kicked, then the stone is still doing real work, whatever it happens to be made of.

Here is another clue, and it is a strange one. Light has no colour. Light is electromagnetic waves at certain wavelengths, and our eyes respond to them. The redness of an apple, the one you might be holding right now, is built by the visual system and not stored in the apple itself. A bee sees ultraviolet patterns that a person cannot see at all, and a mantis shrimp has far more colour receptors than the three that most of us use. Each animal lives in its own colour world, and each of those worlds is assembled from the same light.

Some things we treat as real are clearly agreements, and the Pacific Ocean is a good example. It has no fence, no wall, and no painted line running across it. The boundary between one ocean and another was drawn by people who wanted to organize maps and shipping routes. If you have ever stood on a beach, you have looked at a boundary that nobody can see. The water is real, but the name, the edges, and the separation are shared decisions. Countries work the same way, and so do the lines between them, which we defend sometimes fiercely. That tells us they are very real in their effects.

What about the number seven? Seven apples are easy to hold, but the number seven itself is harder to pick up. Mathematicians have been arguing about it for more than two thousand years. Some say numbers exist in a separate world, waiting to be discovered, while others say they are tools that humans invented to describe patterns. Both sides agree that the patterns are not random, and both sides agree that seven apples and seven stones share something real. Let's take that shared thing as a fact, even if we argue about where it lives.

Dreams are a useful test case. When you are dreaming, the world feels fully real. Things look solid, people talk, and the rules seem to hold. Then you wake up and find that the table you were leaning on was never there, and the mystery is what changed. The experience did not feel less real while it was happening. Our feeling of reality can be wrong in both directions, and the feeling alone cannot be the final judge.

Each eye has a blind spot where the optic nerve leaves the retina, and we never notice it. The brain in your head fills in the gap using what surrounds it. The picture we experience is partly built rather than simply received. That does not make it fake. It makes it an interpretation, and a very useful one, which is why we tend to trust it without checking.

The physicist Richard Feynman had a rule for this. It is the best advice on the whole topic: do not fool yourself, because the easiest person to fool is yourself. That gives us a practical test. If you want to know whether something is real, ask what would change your mind. Then watch how the thing behaves when it is tested, whether you kick the stone, measure the orbit, or check the photograph against a second source. Sometimes the answer will surprise you.

Is the past real? It has no location anyone can visit, yet a fossil or a photograph is a trace in the present, pointing back. We rely on those traces constantly, which means we treat the past as real even though we can never touch it again. Maybe reality is less about touching and more about leaving marks that other things must respond to.

I should be honest that this does not settle everything. Philosophers still disagree about whether the world exists independently of minds. Physics keeps revising its picture of what matter is, so we may never get a final answer. But the process of testing is itself a way of getting closer, and each test removes a few illusions we were happy to keep.

So here is a question to take with you. What is the most real thing in your life right now? Is it the screen in front of you, the people you love, the numbers in your bank account, or the stories you tell about yourself? Rank them by how hard they push back when they are tested. Many of the things that matter most turn out to be not solid at all, while still changing what everyone does every day.

That might be the real answer. Some things are real because they resist us. Some are real because enough people agree to organize their lives around them. Some are real in the way a dream is real, vivid while it lasts. A world can be partly built and still matter enough to kick, to protect, and to share. Anything that changes what we do is real in its effects, and we get to decide how much weight to give the rest.
NARRATION>>>
