"Can you draw a face?" "Yes, sir." "This one looks like a potato." "It is a self portrait." "Well, architecture might suit you better." "I want to paint." "Next."

That is roughly how one of the most important painting careers of the twentieth century did not happen. Spoiler: the painting career did not work out, and the rest of the century paid the price.

Adolf Hitler was born in 1889 in Braunau am Inn, a small town on the border between Austria-Hungary and Bavaria. His father was a customs official, and he wanted his son to follow him into the civil service. That was a respectable plan, and it had a clear path. Why would a father want anything else? We can see the logic of it now. It did not match the plans of the boy who was growing up in the house. Did the boy agree? No. Did he say so? Often, and loudly.

School was a problem. Hitler was bright in some subjects and openly hostile to others, and he did not like being told what to do. He left school without finishing and spent a lot of time drawing, reading, and imagining a grander future. He wanted to be an artist. I think that is a common dream for teenagers, but most of them do not fight their fathers to make it happen.

In 1907, he moved to Vienna, which in the 1900s was the imperial capital and a magnet for ambitious young people. He applied to the Academy of Fine Arts, the city's most prestigious art school, and he was turned away. He tried again the following year, in 1908, and was turned away again. The academy's judgement was blunt. His drawings were competent, but he did not show the kind of talent that would make him a painter. The advice he got was to try architecture instead.

Did he take that advice? No. He stuck with painting, which tells you something about his stubbornness, and possibly about his judgement too. Whether you admire that or not, it was a choice with real consequences. The rejection was a turning point, because it meant that the path he had planned for himself was closed, and he had to build a new one. We should be honest about what the rejection did and did not prove. It did not prove he was hopeless. It proved that the academy did not want him, which is a different thing. I think the rejection stung far more than he ever admitted.

Have you ever been told that your dream does not fit you? It is an uncomfortable moment, and most people handle it with a little hurt and a lot of rationalising. The question is what you do next. For Hitler, the next few years were spent in a series of cheap hostels for men, where he slept in shared rooms and lived very simply. He painted watercolours, sold small pictures, and made postcards to earn a living.

Those were lean years. We know this partly from later accounts, partly from hostel records, and partly from what he chose to tell people afterwards. I find that mix of sources hard to trust fully. Vienna was a city of extreme wealth and extreme poverty, and young men without money could disappear into the crowd very easily. The records show a person who was constantly short of money, constantly moving, and constantly convinced that the world had failed to notice his talent. That sense of grievance would not go away, and it would matter later.

In 1913, he left Vienna for Munich, which was more lively and more open to new ideas. He lived in the city for more than a year before the war began. Then in August 1914, the great powers of Europe went to war, and Hitler volunteered for the Bavarian army. He was 25 years old, and he joined a regiment of volunteers rather than waiting to be called up.

Many young men felt the same excitement, and many of them would come to regret it. Hitler was assigned as a dispatch runner, carrying messages between units near the front. The job was dangerous, because messages had to get through shellfire and across broken ground. He was decorated for his service, including an Iron Cross, which was a meaningful honour for a man who had spent years feeling overlooked.

Biographical details from these years come from letters, military records, and later memoirs, and some of them were polished after the fact. So treat any dramatic account with a little care. I would not trust any single memoir, including his own. What is clear is that the war gave him structure, comradeship, and a sense of purpose that his civilian life had never provided. Why would that matter so much to a man who had struggled for years? Many people found their place in the army, and many never found it again. We can see that pattern in the years that followed.

In October 1918, Hitler was caught in a British gas attack. He was temporarily blinded and sent to a hospital in Pasewalk, in northern Germany. He was there when the armistice was signed in November, and he was there when the news spread through the country that Germany had lost. For a man who had been convinced of victory, that was a shock, and the shock turned quickly into anger.

Then came a myth that would poison German politics for years. It was called the stab-in-the-back legend, and it claimed that the army had not been defeated on the battlefield. Instead, it said, traitors at home, including socialists, pacifists, and Jews, had betrayed the troops from behind. Historians reject that story, and the evidence is overwhelming. The army was exhausted, the economy had collapsed, and the front had broken. But the legend was emotionally powerful, and it gave angry people a villain.

The peace treaty made everything worse. Why did it hurt so much? Partly because it was signed under heavy pressure, and partly because it was written by the victors. Did the Germans have a say? Very little. We should remember how that felt, even if we do not agree with where it led. The Treaty of Versailles, signed in 1919, forced Germany to accept responsibility for the war, to give up territory, to limit its army, and to pay large reparations. Many Germans saw it as a humiliation, and the treaty became a symbol of national shame. It was like an invoice that nobody wanted to pay, and the resentment about it outlasted the bill by decades.

So here is where we are. I want to be clear about one thing. The grievance and the myth do not explain everything. Plenty of people had the same grievances and did not follow him. I think that is the most important point in this whole story. A young man who was rejected by an art school, who failed to find his place in Vienna, and who spent four years in a war he seemed to love, arrives at the end of 1918 with a grievance, a myth, and a country that was angry with itself. What do you think a person like that does next? The answer is that he finds a party, a crowd, and eventually a microphone.
