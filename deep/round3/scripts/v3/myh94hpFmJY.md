<<<NARRATION
Venom is one of the most common weapons in nature, and it is far more common in warm places than most of us would guess. Snakes, spiders, scorpions, jellyfish and cone snails all use it, and many of them live in tropical regions where the heat seems to matter. Why would evolution invest so heavily in a chemical cocktail in some climates and ignore it in others? I think the answer is more interesting than you might expect, and it starts with a simple question about cost. You might not expect that, and I did not, until I looked at the data.

Start with the numbers. There are nearly 3,900 species of snakes, around 50,000 species of spiders, and about 700 species of cone snails, and a large share of those animals carry venom. Researchers think venom has appeared many times, in dozens of separate lineages. If it had evolved only once, we could treat it as a curious accident. Instead, it keeps reappearing, and each time it does, it is a solution to the same problem: how to disable prey, or deter predators, without getting hurt. Why would a trait this widespread keep appearing? You can ask the same question about almost any animal weapon, and the answer usually comes back to cost and benefit.

First, the terms, because they matter. Venom is injected, usually through a bite or a sting, and it works on the body of another animal. Poison is different. It is absorbed or swallowed, and it harms whoever takes it in. I find that distinction useful, because it changes which animals you need to worry about when you walk through a forest or along a beach. Have you ever wondered why a jellyfish sting is treated so differently from a mushroom meal? That is the distinction in action.

Now the key question. Making venom is expensive. A venom gland needs energy, raw materials and a steady supply of cells to build the proteins that make up most of the mixture. You are not just spending calories on a single bite. You are paying for an entire production line, and that line runs every time the animal uses it. So a venomous animal has to get something valuable in return, or the trait would fade away. You would not pay that price every day, and most animals would not either, unless the payoff were large. If you ran a farm and paid for every tool, you would want each one to earn its keep.

That cost question gives us a way to test ideas. If venom pays off more in some environments than in others, the pattern should show up in the map of where venomous animals live. We can test that against real species, and I think the tests tell us more than any single story could. The first idea concerns the variety of prey, and the second concerns the body temperature of the predator itself. What would we see if the pattern were random? Venom would be scattered evenly across the map, and that is not what researchers report.

The prey diversity idea goes like this. In a warm region with many species, a predator may encounter a much wider range of prey, from insects to small mammals to other reptiles. That variety can favour venoms that act quickly on many different body types, because a predator cannot afford to be specialised in one kind of victim. Warm places tend to be crowded with prey, and I think that steady pressure helps explain the pattern. A venom that works on only one kind of animal is a poor bet when your menu changes every day. Why should that matter for venom? Think about a predator that eats a dozen different kinds of prey in a single season. If you hunted a wide range of prey, you would want a flexible weapon.

The second idea is about metabolism. Snakes, lizards and many insects are ectotherms, which means they rely on the environment to warm their bodies. In warm climates, these animals can be active for longer, digest faster and hunt more often. If you hunt more often, you need a reliable way to subdue prey quickly, and venom is one of the most effective tools for that job. Warmth gives these animals energy, and energy gives them a reason to keep the venom system running. Does that mean warm climates create venom? Not exactly, but they make it more useful, and I think that is the subtler point.

There is a more surprising possibility, too. Some researchers suspect that venom acts faster in a warm body, because the chemical reactions it triggers speed up with temperature. A rise of 10 degrees can roughly double the rate of many chemical reactions, and that alone could make a bite more effective in hot conditions. I find that idea appealing. I should add that the evidence is still limited, and the effect may depend heavily on the species involved. Would that be enough to explain the pattern? I doubt it, and you should be sceptical of any tidy answer.

Consider the box jellyfish, which lives in the warm coastal waters of northern Australia and across the Indo-Pacific. Its tentacles can stretch up to three metres long, and they carry stinging cells that fire in a fraction of a second when touched. In severe cases, the sting can be fatal within minutes. You can see why this animal is studied so carefully by people who work on beach safety, and why its venom is so potent for such a simple body plan. Imagine you have just brushed a jellyfish: it can feel like a burn that spreads in seconds. I still find that image hard to forget.

Cone snails show a different strategy. There are around 700 species, mostly in tropical and subtropical seas, and each one produces a venom cocktail that can contain around 100 to 200 different peptides. Each peptide is tuned to a particular target in the nervous system, so a single snail is effectively a small pharmacy. I think that is one of the most remarkable things about venom. It is a library of molecules, shaped by a very long history of trial and error. I like to call this chemical evolution, a process in which each small change gets tested in the wild. I find that remarkable, and you may too.

Spiders offer another example. The Brazilian wandering spider lives in tropical forests, and it is known for a painful bite and an unnerving habit of hiding in places people reach without looking, such as shoes and fruit shipments. Its venom can cause serious pain and, in some cases, dangerous symptoms. Some spider venoms are being studied for their effects on pain pathways, and you may have heard about that research in the news. If you lived there, would you check your shoes before putting them on? We should respect that, and we should be careful around them.

Snakes deserve special attention. Snakebite is a major health problem in tropical and subtropical regions, and the World Health Organization estimates that roughly five million people are bitten every year. More than 80,000 of those bites end in death. Antivenom is made by milking venom from snakes and using it to stimulate an immune response in animals such as horses. If you have ever read about snakebite treatment, you know how much depends on access to that medicine. How many of those people had access to antivenom? In many places, the honest answer is too few. You will see why when you look at the numbers.

So is venom simply a weapon of convenience in hot places? I do not think so. Venom is shaped by what surrounds the animal, including its prey, its predators and its body temperature. When you put those pressures together, you get a set of traits that look like a tuned solution. We can test some of these ideas by comparing species across latitudes, and that is what researchers have started to do over the last few decades.

Here I want to be careful. Correlation across species is not proof of cause. Warm climates also differ in humidity, vegetation, competition and the history of the lineages that happen to live there. A pattern can be real and still have several overlapping causes, so the honest answer is that we do not yet have one tidy explanation. Anyone who claims otherwise is probably selling a simpler story than the evidence supports. We should not overclaim, and we should not dismiss the idea either.

There is also a sampling problem. Scientists have studied the venom of some groups in great detail and others barely at all, which means our maps of venomous animals are uneven. If you tried to count venomous species in a remote rainforest, you would almost certainly miss a few. That gap alone could distort the pattern, and it is one reason I think the field is still young. You should not trust a map drawn from such uneven data, and I certainly do not. I think that is a bigger issue than most readers realise.

A common myth is that venom is always deadly to people, and that is not quite right. Many venoms are painful but not dangerous, and some bites from venomous animals cause only local effects. The danger depends on the species, the dose and how quickly someone receives treatment. You should take every venomous bite seriously, but you do not need to fear every venomous animal in the same way. Have you heard the claim that every venomous bite is fatal? It is a common belief, and it is wrong. If you are ever bitten, you should seek help quickly.

Venom has also given us medicine. In the 1970s, researchers developed captopril, a blood pressure drug based on compounds found in the venom of a pit viper from South America. Cone snail venoms have produced a painkiller that acts on a specific nerve channel. Each discovery started with a careful collection of samples, a lot of patient lab work and a team willing to chase small differences in toxicity. The payoff can be enormous, and it is a reminder that a costly trait can also be a valuable one. Who would have guessed that a snake's hunting toolkit could lower blood pressure?

Cold places have venom too. We can see the same logic at higher latitudes. The European adder reaches far north, into regions with cold winters and short summers. So the story is not that venom only works in heat. It is that warm regions tend to host more venomous lineages, and the reasons are a mix of ecology, metabolism and history. If you look at the adder, you see a venomous snake that succeeds in a very different environment from the tropical species I have described. You would look for the adder in heathland, forest edges and rocky ground across northern Europe.

Let us return to the cost question, because it matters for how these animals use venom. A predator with a reliable venom system can afford to be selective, using a small dose when the meal is small and saving the rest. Many snakes do exactly that, delivering a dry bite when they want to defend themselves rather than hunt. You can see the logic. Venom is valuable, so the animal spends it carefully, and every drop has a job.

This also changes how we think about defence. Many venomous animals use their venom mostly on prey, and they rely on camouflage, speed or warning colours to avoid being eaten. A coral snake, for example, uses bold banded colours to advertise danger, and many predators learn to leave it alone. Venom is a tool for the hunt, and defence is often a separate system working in parallel. Keeping those two jobs apart is one of the reasons the whole story is so rich. How would you tell a dry bite from a full one? Often, you cannot, which is why caution matters.

So what would you expect if you studied a tropical snake and a temperate one? Would their venoms look different? Would the tropical one hunt more often, or would it defend itself more? These are good questions, and the answers will come from careful field work and lab tests, not from guesswork. Nobody has settled them yet, which is exactly what makes the topic worth following.

Go back to the question at the start. Why does venom appear so often in warm places? The answer is not one single cause. It is a mix of cost, prey, metabolism and temperature, shaped by a very long history of trial and error. Venom is a costly tool, and it is worth paying for only when the environment makes it pay off. Warm places, it turns out, often make it pay off very well. We still have much to learn, and we should keep asking.
