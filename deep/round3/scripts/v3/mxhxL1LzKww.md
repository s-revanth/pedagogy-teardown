<<<NARRATION
You can see farther than you think. You can also see less than you want. Stand on a beach and watch the sea run out to a thin line. Is that the end of the ocean? Of course not. It is only the edge of what your eyes can reach. So how much of the Earth can you see at once? I wanted a real answer, so I worked it out from the ground up. I found that the answer changes every time you change your height.

Begin where you are. Stand on flat ground and look straight ahead. How far away is the horizon? The answer depends on one thing, and I mean just one: how high your eyes are. For most adults, the eyes sit near 1.7 meters up. I will use that number. Is it really that simple? Almost. The rest is geometry, and I find geometry kind to us.

The math is simpler than it looks. Earth is round, with a radius of 6,371 kilometers. As you look out, the ground curves away, and the horizon is the spot where that curve hides everything beyond it. Do you need a formula? No. Here is the short version. Double your height, and the horizon moves out a good deal. Quadruple it, and the horizon only doubles. Why does it work like that? The distance grows with the square root of the height. Surprising, right? It surprised me too, and I kept checking it.

For you, standing up, the horizon is five kilometers away. Does that sound like much? It is less than you might expect. It is a small circle, and almost everything you see on a normal day sits inside it. I find that a little humbling. Now let's climb, and watch the circle grow.

Take a tall building. The top of the Burj Khalifa in Dubai is 828 meters up. From there, I calculate the horizon at 100 kilometers. That is many times farther than the view from the ground. Did you expect that? I did not. A single building changes everything I thought I knew about my own horizon. Keep going, because the next step is bigger, and I promise it gets stranger.

Now an airplane. A passenger jet flies at around 10 kilometers. Picture what that does to the view. The plane sits at the center of a huge circle, and the edge is the curve of the planet itself. I looked at that edge for a long time. Could a single flight really show me the bend of the world? It can. That is the first real clue that we are looking at a sphere, and it is hard to unsee once you notice it.

Then comes Mount Everest. The summit sits 8.8 kilometers above sea level. From there, the horizon is 330 kilometers away. On a clear day, a climber could see mountains, valleys, and sky in every direction. I think that would be breathtaking. But is that the whole planet? Not even close. The horizon marks the edge of what you can see, not the edge of the surface. Those are very different things, and we confuse them all the time, don't we?

Now let's leave the ground entirely. The International Space Station orbits 400 kilometers up. From there, the horizon is more than 2,000 kilometers away. That is an enormous circle. I looked at photographs from up there, and I could not stop zooming in. Astronauts can see weather systems, coastlines, and cities lit up at night. Do you ever wonder what that feels like? I do, every time, and I suspect you do too.

So what fraction of the surface is that? Let's work it out. The visible part of the planet is a cap, a curved piece of the sphere. The higher you go, the bigger the cap gets. From 400 kilometers, we can see only a few percent of the surface. That sounds tiny. But is it? I checked the math twice. That slice is still bigger than the entire area of Canada, and I still find that hard to picture.

If you want the exact share, the formula is simple. Take your height, and divide it by twice the sum of Earth's radius and your height. Plug in the altitude, and you get the same kind of answer. I like that formula, because it shows the trend clearly. The fraction climbs fast at first, then it flattens toward half. Does that sound familiar from the horizon rule? The same pattern hides in both.

Now go higher. A weather satellite can sit 35,800 kilometers up, in an orbit that keeps it over a fixed spot as the Earth spins. From there, the visible cap covers 42 percent of the planet. A large share of the Earth, at once. Why does that matter? It is why a handful of these satellites can watch storms form, and we can follow them all day long. I find that remarkable.

A trio of them, spaced evenly, can cover nearly the whole planet, except the far north and south. Think about how strange that is. A trio of objects, far above, sees almost everything. Have you ever checked a forecast? Then you have used that fact, even if you never noticed it. We rely on it constantly, and most of us never think about it at all. Did you know that? I did not, until I looked into it.

What about the Moon? It sits 384,000 kilometers away. From there, you would see just under half of the Earth at once. Notice what that means. Going from the satellite to the Moon adds very little to the view. Your eyes gain a huge amount of distance, but the planet never fills the whole frame. I was surprised by that, and I think you will be too. So why not? Keep reading, because that is the key.

I wondered whether the Moon would show us something different. It does, and it does not. The Earth turns beneath any single view, so over a day, the whole planet passes by. At any moment, though, we only see a single face. Could a view ever be better than that? No. Every view has a limit, and the limit is the same no matter how far away we go.

Many people guess that from far enough away, you could see the entire planet. I guessed that too. The truth is cleaner. The most you can ever see is half. Go out to infinity, and the view creeps toward the midpoint, but it never passes it. Why? Because a sphere can only show you a single side at a time. We never see both faces at once.

Here is a way to feel it. Hold a beach ball close to your face. You see a little less than half of its surface. Now step back. I watched the visible part grow, bit by bit, until it was almost exactly half. We can test this with any ball at all. Can you ever see the far side at the same moment? No. The ball has a back, and that back is always hidden from you. That is the whole trick, and I love how simple it is.

Let's connect this to a number from long ago. Around 2,200 years ago, Eratosthenes measured the size of the Earth. Could he really do that without any satellites? He did. It is geometry that we can see with our own eyes, and that is what I find so satisfying.

I have seen this many times, and it still feels strange to me. The planet is showing you its shape, a ship at a time. We should all get to see that once.

This is why cities put towers on hills, and why radio stations use antennas on high masts. A higher antenna reaches a farther horizon. A higher lookout sees farther. For centuries, lighthouses did the same job. Is height a decoration? No. Height is reach. I consider every high point we climb as buying us more of the horizon. Imagine how much signal a tall tower can carry.

Here is a quick challenge. Right now, look out a window, or step outside. Guess how far your horizon is. You will probably guess low, the same way I did. I bet you will too, and that is perfectly fine.

From the space station, more than 2,000 kilometers. From geostationary orbit, a large share of the planet. From the Moon, just under half. I wrote that list on paper, and it still amazes me. No matter what, the limit is half. Each new height gives more, but the gains shrink. Does that list surprise you?

Let me be careful about these numbers. They are rounded. They assume a clear day and an open view. Mountains, buildings, and haze can cut the view short. Bending air can stretch it a little. So treat the figures as careful estimates, not promises. Is the shape of the answer still right? Yes. I would bet on that every time, and I hope you would too. Still, I might have a slight error somewhere, so check my work.

Why does the horizon matter so much? It is the edge of what we can know directly. Everything beyond it, we learn from maps, from instruments, and from other people's eyes. Satellites, sailors, and astronauts all work the same way. They extend what they can see by climbing, then trust the math for the rest. I think we are not so different. We all see a small circle, and we all build a picture of what lies past it.

Here is the payoff, I think. Every time you look at the horizon, you are looking at the curve of the whole planet. The edge is not a wall. It is a sign that the ground keeps bending away. Your view is always a small piece of a much bigger sphere. And I believe you carry that sphere with you wherever you go. Isn't that a strange thing to realize while standing still? It is the most useful thing I learned while making this.

So how much of the Earth can you see at once? Standing on the ground, a sliver. From a mountain, a bigger piece. From space, a large share, but never all of it. We can never take in the whole thing in one glance. But we can see more than we thought, if we are willing to climb. That is a very human thing to do, and I think it is worth the trip.
