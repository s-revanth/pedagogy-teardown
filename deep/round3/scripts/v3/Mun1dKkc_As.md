"How long will the big push take?" "A week, sir." "Are you sure?" "Yes, sir. A week." "Then bring a week's worth of mud." "Sir, we have no mud." "Then bring some more."

That is the planning meeting for a war that everyone assumed would be over by Christmas. Spoiler: it took four years, and nobody got the week they were promised.

So let's go back to 1915. The armies on the Western Front had dug in, and the lines stretched from the North Sea to Switzerland. Between them lay barbed wire, shell craters, and machine guns that could cut down a line of men in minutes. Every attack needed artillery, time, and a great many men. Every attack achieved very little. I think the most unsettling thing about the early war is how predictable it was. We can read the reports now and see the same plan repeated, with the same result. Judging the commanders too quickly would be a mistake, but the pattern is hard to ignore.

If you were the general in charge, how would you break a line that keeps closing? Would you send more men? Would you send more shells? Would you try something new? Each option had a cost, and each cost was paid in the same place, the mud between the trenches. That is the central problem of the war, and it explains almost everything that followed.

In 1916, the Germans chose Verdun, a fortress town on the River Meuse, as their target. The idea was to bleed the French army by attacking a place they could not afford to lose. The battle dragged on for months, and the French held on at great cost. The Germans lost heavily too. Verdun became a symbol of endurance, and a grim reminder that a battle designed to kill the enemy can kill your own army just as easily. I have always thought Verdun is the hardest war story to tell well, because its purpose changed as the fighting went on. What began as a plan to break a fortress became a contest of stubbornness.

Then came the Somme. On the first day, in July 1916, the British army launched a huge offensive after a long artillery bombardment. The bombardment was supposed to cut the wire and crush the German defences. It did not, and the British infantry walked into machine-gun fire across open ground. Nearly 20,000 British soldiers were killed that day, and tens of thousands more were wounded or missing. It remains the bloodiest day in the history of the British army. We can say that with confidence, because the figures are well documented. What we cannot do is make those numbers feel as big as they were. I have tried, and I am not sure I have succeeded.

I still find those numbers hard to hold in my head. Somewhere behind every figure is a farmer, a clerk, a schoolteacher, or a student who had signed up expecting a short war. Some of them had never been abroad before. Many of them never went home. It makes the decisions made in staff headquarters look very different once you have seen what they cost.

I know it is a cliche, but trench warfare is often described as a tug of war in mud. Both sides pulled, both sides strained, and neither side gained much ground for all the effort. The rope was the front line, and the mud was the cost. Each new offensive promised a breakthrough, and each one ended with a few kilometres won and a mountain of dead left behind. We see the same promise in every generation of war planning. Each side was sure that this time would be different. Did anyone think the mud would win? Some did, quietly, and they were usually the ones who had seen the last attack.

On the Eastern Front, the picture was different. In 1916, the Russian general Brusilov launched an offensive that broke through Austro-Hungarian lines and captured huge numbers of prisoners. It was one of the most successful operations of the war, and it might have changed the balance. But the Russian army was exhausted, its supplies were thin, and its political system was coming apart. I find the Brusilov story oddly neglected in popular accounts. It shows that the war could be won by a skilful commander and an army that still had some fight left. It also shows how quickly a victory can slip away when the home front falls apart.

In March 1917, revolution swept through Petrograd. The Tsar abdicated, and a new government took over with a commitment to continue the war. The fighting went on, but the army was increasingly unwilling to take orders. By late 1917, the Bolsheviks had seized power, and in 1918 Russia signed a separate peace with Germany. That freed German divisions for the Western Front, which is a point the Germans were very keen to exploit.

Then the United States entered the war. America declared war on Germany in April 1917, after a series of provocations including unrestricted submarine warfare. The American army was small at first, and it took time to train and transport its soldiers across the Atlantic. But fresh troops, money, and industrial output gave the Allies a huge advantage in the long run. I think the American entry is often told too quickly, as if the troops simply arrived and won. That is not what happened. Why does that matter so much? Because the Central Powers were running out of men, and the Allies were not.

I should be careful with the numbers in this war. I would rather admit uncertainty than pretend to a precision that nobody had at the time. Casualty figures vary widely by source, by country, and by how the dead, missing, and wounded were counted. Some totals include prisoners, some do not, and some were revised years later. So treat any single figure as a guide, not a final answer.

In the spring of 1918, Germany launched a major offensive on the Western Front. The aim was to break through before the Americans arrived in large numbers. For a few weeks it looked as if it might work. German units advanced further than they had in years, and the Allied lines bent badly in places. But the attack outran its supplies, its men were exhausted, and the offensive stalled.

Then the Allies counterattacked. Their army had more tanks, more artillery, and fresh American troops. Through the late summer and autumn, the Allied advance pushed the German army back, and the Central Powers began to fall apart. Bulgaria surrendered, the Ottoman Empire collapsed, and Austria-Hungary broke up. Germany was left alone, and its government was in crisis.

On 11 November 1918, at 11 o'clock in the morning, the guns fell silent. The armistice had been signed in a railway carriage in the forest of Compiègne, and the terms left Germany defeated without an invasion of its own soil. Soldiers on both sides stood up in their trenches and tried to understand what had just happened. Many letters survive from those days, and the tone is often confused rather than triumphant. I believe that confusion tells us as much as the treaties do. I find that moment strangely quiet, especially when you think about the lives lost in the weeks before it. Did anybody feel like celebrating? Some did. Many simply felt tired.

The war that was supposed to end all wars had left behind millions of dead, empires in ruins, and a map that looked nothing like it had in 1914. Many of the borders drawn then remained contentious for decades. Nobody had a clear idea of what the peace would look like, and the peace conference that followed had its own agenda, its own rivalries, and its own sense of grievance. That is the uncomfortable truth. The guns stopped, but the arguments did not.

What would you have wanted from the peace if you had lived through the war? Would you have wanted revenge, security, or a fair settlement? Most people in 1918 wanted all three, and they did not agree on the order. That disagreement shaped the next twenty years, and it is hard to read the history without feeling the tension in every treaty.

Next time we look at the peace conference and the treaties that followed, and I promise it is a messy story. Thanks for watching, and I will see you then.
