## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Most patterns in the world don't last very long. A wave breaks, a sandcastle washes away and a campfire burns down to ash within an hour or two. Yet some patterns have persisted for billions of years, and a few of them have even spread across the whole planet. Why do things exist at all, and why do some of them stick around while the rest disappear without a trace? That question is the starting point for evolution, and in this video we're going to build the stage on which the whole story plays out.

Here's the roadmap for this video. First we'll build a toy world full of simple patterns, then compare two different ways a pattern can stay around. After that we'll name the three ingredients that turn persistence into something that can change over time, run a simulation, address a common misunderstanding about design, and finish with the limits of what a toy can tell us about real history, because you should know those limits as well as the results.

Let's build the toy world, and we start with a grid of 100 squares, and each square either holds a pattern or stays empty. During each step, a pattern can do two things. It can copy itself into a neighboring empty square, and it can fade away entirely. We give every pattern the same two chances, one for copying and one for fading, so the only difference between patterns comes from the chances they carry with them into the next step. If you have ever watched moss creep across a wall, you have seen a crude version of this.

Now let's sort the things we see around us into kinds. A rock is stable, and it can last for thousands of years, but it doesn't make new rocks, so it never spreads from its own corner of the world. Some zircon crystals in old rocks are more than 4 billion years old, and I find that remarkable. A crystal is a little more interesting, because it grows by adding to itself, and each new layer takes on the same arrangement as the layer below. A replicator goes one step further, since it makes new copies that can themselves make copies. Those three cases look similar from far away, but only the last one can fill a space over time.

Before we run the grid, I want you to pause and predict. Suppose the grid is crowded, and every pattern has to compete for room. Which kind of pattern do you think survives best, the stable one that never copies, or the copier that spreads but fades more often? Take a moment to commit to an answer, and then keep watching. The result is perhaps more subtle than it first looks, and the answer depends on the balance between the two chances, which we'll measure directly.

Let's run it. We start with a handful of stable patterns and a handful of copiers scattered across the grid. The stable patterns might persist for a long time, but they never spread, so they stay in their corners for the whole run. The copiers multiply quickly in the empty squares, and within a few dozen steps they fill most of the grid. Stability alone can't claim new ground, and that's the first thing the run makes clear. We ran this setup 50 times, and we recorded the share of the grid occupied after 100 steps. I'll admit the first few runs surprised me, because the copiers do not always win straight away. Sometimes a pattern with a slightly better fade chance grabs a corner early and holds it for dozens of steps. Over those 50 runs, though, the picture is clear, and we can build on it as we go.

There's a common instinct that points the other way, and many people look at a world full of survivors and assume that somebody or something must have chosen which patterns get to last. That instinct is understandable, because most things we build do have a designer behind them. But nothing designed the survivors on our grid. The grid simply removes the patterns that fade faster than they copy, and what remains is whatever happens to be left over. Survival is a filter, not a plan, and that difference changes how we should read the whole picture.

That filter is worth describing precisely, and every pattern has a copy chance and a fade chance, and the balance between them decides whether it grows or shrinks. If copying beats fading by a wide margin, the pattern spreads quickly across the grid. If the two chances are close, the pattern grows slowly and may vanish by chance before it gets going. A pattern that copies with a chance of 0.6 and fades with a chance of 0.5 probably grows, but only slowly. If fading wins, the pattern declines no matter how often it tries to copy. The whole story lives in that balance, and it's the same growth logic we used earlier when we looked at populations.

Now let's add crowding, because real worlds never have unlimited room. When the grid fills up, a new copy can only appear by displacing something that already exists. Copiers that fade slowly likely hold their ground, while copiers that fade quickly lose ground to their rivals. The result is a shifting mosaic rather than a single winner. In one run on a 100-square grid, copiers that faded at 10 percent per step held about a third of the squares, while faster faders lost ground within 20 steps. The patterns that persist are not necessarily the strongest ones. They are the ones that keep finding room to copy themselves before they fade.

We can now see the three ingredients clearly, and it helps to name them in full. The first is copying, which lets a pattern make more of itself. The second is variation, which means copies are not perfectly identical, so some of them differ in small ways. The third is selection, which means some variants leave more copies behind than others. When those three ingredients work together over many steps, the population changes, and that change is what we call evolution. Without variation, nothing new ever appears, and without selection, nothing sorts the new variants into a pattern.

Here's a wall that many people hit when they first think about this. If copying is the key, why doesn't everything copy itself? Real copying is hard to get right, and most attempts fail to reproduce a pattern accurately enough to keep it recognizable. In our toy, a pattern that copies too sloppily loses its identity after just a few generations. So the first copiers had to be accurate enough to keep a recognizable form, and that accuracy is one of the deepest puzzles in understanding how life began on this planet. How accurate must the copying be for a pattern of 20 units to survive? That answer is far from obvious, and we could test it in the grid.

The same spectrum shows up well beyond our toy, and a star persists for billions of years, but it doesn't make new stars in the sense that a cell makes new cells. A crystal grows by adding layers, which is a kind of copying, although it has very little room for variation. Cells copy themselves with remarkable precision, and they vary often enough to explore new forms. Each of these sits somewhere along the same spectrum of persistence, copying and variation, and the cell is the case where all three ingredients meet in one place. A bacterium can divide every 20 minutes under good conditions, so we can see the difference in speed as well as in variation.

I want to be careful about what the toy can tell us. It shows the logic of persistence, copying and selection, but it doesn't tell us how the first copiers actually formed. The chemistry of the early Earth is still an open question, and the grid is a simplification rather than a history book. I want to stress that the grid holds only 100 squares, which is a tiny world compared with the planet. What it does show is that you don't need a designer to get patterns that spread and change. You only need copying, a little variation and a filter, and those are ingredients a real world might supply on its own.

So what does this set up for the rest of the story? Variation, heredity and selection together create a stage on which the diversity of life can unfold. Each one is simple by itself, but together they generate a process that keeps producing new forms as long as the conditions allow. That's the foundation for everything that follows in this series, and it explains why a small set of rules can produce such a rich and surprising world.

The stage is set, and the question of why things exist turns into a more specific one. What happens when copies start to vary in ways that matter for survival? That's the thread that leads toward the main story of evolution, and it's where this line of reasoning naturally goes. We might even find that the first copiers were less mysterious than they seem.
