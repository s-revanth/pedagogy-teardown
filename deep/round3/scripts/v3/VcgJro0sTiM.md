<<<NARRATION
Every few months, someone asks which other math channels I actually watch, and for years my answer was a vague wave of the hand. This time we will write the list down properly, with three criteria attached, so that you can judge every recommendation for yourself. The goal is not to rank anything, since a good explanation depends on what you are trying to learn, and that varies a great deal from person to person.

Which channels deserve a place, then? The answer depends on taste, but in my experience the great ones share three qualities that the merely fine ones often lack. The first is care, meaning the presenter checks each step instead of gliding past the hard parts. The second is a visual sense, where the picture carries the argument and does not merely decorate it. The third is honesty about uncertainty, which means saying clearly when something is still unclear.

With those criteria in hand, we can sort the recommendations into four rough categories. The first is deep dives into proofs. These videos spend twenty minutes or more on a single result, and by the end you understand not only that it is true, but why it could not have been otherwise. If slow, careful reasoning appeals to you, this is the natural place to begin, and your patience tends to grow along the way.

The second category is applied mathematics, the kind that shows up in physics, engineering, and computing. These channels care less about abstraction for its own sake and more about what the mathematics lets us build or predict. A strong applied channel might show a simulation in the first thirty seconds, then explain the equation behind it, and then ask what happens when one parameter changes. You get the thrill of a working system and the understanding of why it works, in that order.

There is a practical caveat here, and it matters more than people expect. A channel can be excellent and still sit three levels above where you currently are. Before committing, check whether the opening minutes quietly assume knowledge you do not have. If they do, look for an earlier video in the same series, because the foundations are usually laid in the first few episodes rather than in the most impressive ones.

The third category is history and philosophy of mathematics. These videos tell us who found a result, what they were attempting, and why the idea took decades, or in some cases centuries, to settle. I find this storytelling valuable, because mathematics is often taught as a finished and frozen subject. In reality it is full of false starts, heated disagreements, and corrections, and these channels reveal that human side far better than most textbooks can. You may find yourself caring about a seventeenth century argument more than you expected.

The fourth category is puzzles and short-form problems. These suit a few minutes of focused thinking, and they often reward a single clever observation. The risk is that we consume them passively, nodding along to the solution without ever attempting the problem first. Pausing before the answer, even for two or three minutes, turns a pleasant distraction into real practice, and that is the difference you should be aiming for.

There are also warning signs worth knowing. Titles that promise a shocking answer in sixty seconds usually skip the reasoning that makes the answer meaningful. Comment sections full of people saying they finally understood something are encouraging, but they are not a substitute for checking the explanation yourself. If you see a channel that never admits uncertainty on any topic, give it a second look before you give it your time.

Different presenters use different tools, and that is perfectly fine. Some lean on animation, some on careful writing on a board, and some on live coding or physical models. The medium matters less than the reasoning, so we should judge a presentation by what it asks us to do in our own heads. A video that makes you stop and predict the next step is doing something valuable, even if its production is modest.

So how do we choose among all of these? My suggestion is deliberately simple. Pick one video from a channel that interests you, and watch the first five minutes. Then ask whether the presenter explained why each step happens, or only what the step is. If the why is missing, the channel may still be entertaining, but it will probably not build the kind of understanding you are hoping for.

This test works far beyond mathematics. Whenever an explanation is on the table, the real question is whether it gives you a reason you could reproduce on your own. A good explanation leaves you able to solve a nearby problem without the presenter at your side. That is a much sharper standard than whether a video felt pleasant, although entertainment and understanding can certainly coexist in one video.

The recommendation algorithm will happily feed you more of the same, which is exactly why a deliberate list like this can help. Left alone, a feed drifts toward whatever is most dramatic, not whatever is most instructive. Choosing a few channels on purpose is a small act of resistance against that drift, and it tends to pay off within a month or two, once you notice how much more you are learning.

Let me also say a word about balance. Nobody needs to watch everything, and there is no reason to feel guilty about dropping a channel that has stopped being useful. Many people subscribe to a dozen channels and then feel buried under the queue. It is usually better to follow two or three closely, and to add a new one only when a specific gap needs filling in your understanding.

Here is a concrete example. Suppose you want to understand probability better. A sensible plan is to start with one channel that is strong on intuition, add a second that works through formal proofs, and finish with a third that focuses on applications such as statistics in medicine. Each one covers a different angle, and together they give you a fuller picture than any single source could manage alone.

Think of the whole collection as a small library rather than a pile of subscriptions. A library is valuable because you return to certain books again and again, and because the shelves cover different needs. You want a few favorites you trust, plus a handful of specialists you consult when a particular topic shows up in your studies or your work. Curation matters far more than quantity.

So here is the short list of qualities to look for, restated plainly. Look for careful steps, a strong visual argument, honest admissions of uncertainty, and a clear reason behind every claim. If a channel has all four, it is almost certainly worth your time, whether or not it appears on anyone else's list, including the one we have been sketching here.

Which of the four categories would you start with? I would guess that many of the people who write to me lean toward proofs, but I am genuinely curious, and I suspect the answer depends on what you want from the subject. Pause for a moment and decide, then go and watch the first video, because the best way to judge an explanation is to see one in action.

If you have a favorite channel that is not in my head right now, we would both benefit from hearing about it. The most useful lists are built by many people sharing what actually worked for them, and I would genuinely like to see what the community has found over the last several years.
