Antimatter is the strangest substance most of us have never seen. Every antiparticle has the same mass as its partner but the opposite charge, and when matter and antimatter meet, both vanish in a burst of energy. Yet the universe seems to be made almost entirely of ordinary matter. So why is there antimatter at all, and why is there so little of it around? I think that question is one of the most important in physics, and it has a longer story than most people expect.

If you have ever wondered why textbooks treat antimatter as science fiction, you are not alone. I used to think of it that way too. But antimatter is measured every day in laboratories, and it even shows up in hospitals. The case can be built step by step, and you can check each step yourself.

The story begins with an equation. In 1928, the British physicist Paul Dirac wrote down a relativistic equation for the electron, and it fitted the known facts beautifully. But it also had a strange feature. It allowed solutions with negative energy, and an electron should not be able to fall into one. I find that the most unsettling part of the whole episode. Dirac proposed that those states were filled with a sea of negative-energy electrons, and that a gap in the sea would behave like a particle with positive charge.

A physicist who writes down an impossible-looking result needs strong reasons, and I think Dirac had them, because he trusted his equations more than his intuition. You can see the same instinct in many great discoveries, where the mathematics points somewhere the senses cannot follow. That instinct deserves respect, but it also deserves a test, and that is what happened next.

In 1932, Carl Anderson was studying cosmic rays in a cloud chamber when he saw a track that curved the wrong way for an electron. It had the mass of an electron but the opposite charge. We now call it the positron. You may find it strange that a theorist's equation predicted a particle before anyone saw one, but that is how it happened. Anderson's track was one of the first pieces of evidence that the mathematics was describing something real.

Anderson must have felt something close to disbelief when that track appeared on the film. A new particle was not something anyone expected to see in a cloud chamber. I can see him checking the magnetic field, the speed and the direction again and again, because a single misread track could mislead the whole field. You would do the same in his place.

The same pattern repeated for the proton. Physicists at the Bevatron in Berkeley detected the antiproton in 1955, and by 1995 the team at CERN had produced the first atoms of antihydrogen, a positron orbiting an antiproton. Those atoms lasted only a fraction of a second, because they touched ordinary matter almost immediately. Even so, the experiment showed that antimatter atoms could be made in a laboratory.

Each of these discoveries followed the same rhythm. A prediction came first, then a search, then a fleeting result that needed careful checking. I find that rhythm reassuring, because it shows how the scientific method can turn a strange idea into a measured fact. Why does that matter for antimatter? Because the next step always depends on the last one.

What happens when matter and antimatter meet? They annihilate, and the full mass of both particles turns into energy. Einstein's famous relation says that energy equals mass times the speed of light squared, and the numbers are staggering. One gram of antimatter meeting one gram of matter would release about 1.8 times 10 to the fourteenth joules. That is roughly three times the energy of the bomb dropped on Hiroshima. Few processes in nature release energy so completely.

The event is a flash that leaves no solid remains. The particles do not simply stick together. Instead, the rest mass of both particles is converted into radiation and other particles, which carry the energy away at close to the speed of light. Instruments detect those products, and that is how annihilation is confirmed in the lab.

Why does that matter for the question in the title? Because if the universe began with equal amounts of matter and antimatter, they should have annihilated almost completely. Would you expect anything to remain? I think that is the real puzzle, and it deserves a careful answer. Almost everything should have vanished, leaving a bath of light behind, and yet we are here.

If the question is treated as a puzzle, the pieces are clear enough. We have a law that demands antimatter, a surplus of matter that must be explained, and a small crack in the symmetry that points the right way. We have not yet built the whole picture, and the gap between those pieces is where the most exciting work happens. You can follow it in the news, and I would encourage you to keep an eye on results from CERN and from neutrino detectors.

The answer is that the universe is not perfectly symmetric. Observations suggest that for every billion or so antiparticles that existed, there were about a billion and one particles of matter. That tiny surplus is what remains after almost everything annihilated, and it is the reason stars, planets and people exist. One extra particle in a billion sounds trivial, but on a cosmic scale it is everything.

I find it helpful to treat this as a ledger. Every particle created in the early universe had a partner, but the books were not perfectly balanced. You can check the numbers yourself, and you will see that one part in a billion is enough to matter. We have no direct way to see that imbalance in a single cubic metre of space, yet the cosmic microwave background and the abundance of light elements both confirm it.

In 1967, the Soviet physicist Andrei Sakharov listed three conditions that a universe would need in order to produce that surplus. It would need interactions that do not conserve the number of baryons, a violation of charge and parity symmetry, and a period when the universe was out of thermal equilibrium. We can test each condition, and I think the first real evidence for one of them came earlier than most people expect.

In 1964, James Cronin and Val Fitch found that certain decays of kaons, particles containing strange quarks, did not treat matter and antimatter identically. That violation of CP symmetry was a surprise. It was small, but it proved that nature is not perfectly mirror symmetric. You can think of it as a crack in the symmetry, and cracks are what allow an asymmetry to grow. Cronin and Fitch later shared a Nobel Prize for the discovery.

I think that result still feels surprising, because the symmetry seemed so basic. You might have expected it to hold everywhere, as it does in most of the processes you see every day. You would need strong evidence before trusting a theory that broke a symmetry it was built on. Most physicists did not expect it either, and that is why the experiment mattered so much.

We now know that the Standard Model contains CP violation, but it is far too weak to explain the surplus we see. We need additional sources, and that is why searches continue at CERN and in neutrino experiments around the world. Each new result narrows the range of possibilities, but none of them yet closes the gap. The honest position is that we know one ingredient is missing, and we are not sure which one it is.

It is only honest to say that the missing ingredient is not a small detail. It could be a new particle, a new interaction or a new kind of condition in the early universe. We can narrow the options by testing one idea at a time, and I find that process encouraging, even when the results disappoint.

Antimatter is not only mysterious. It is extraordinarily expensive to make. Producing antiprotons requires huge accelerators, and the yields are tiny, measured in a few million particles at a time. One often cited estimate puts a gram of antimatter in the tens of trillions of dollars. You do not need a calculator to see the problem, because even a few atoms require an enormous amount of infrastructure.

You can see why the cost matters. Every gram would need a long chain of accelerators, cooling systems and power supplies, and much of that equipment runs only a few experiments at a time. We have to ask whether the science justifies the spending, and I think the answer depends on what we learn from it. You would have to think hard before paying that price for a single answer.

How do researchers store it at all? Antiparticles are held in magnetic and electric fields in devices called Penning traps, so that they never touch the walls of the container. I find that idea remarkable. The apparatus is a kind of magnetic bottle, and the bottle has to be cooled to very low temperatures to keep the antiparticles still enough to study. A single stray collision with ordinary matter would end the experiment in an instant.

Antimatter is already part of your medical life, if you have ever had a PET scan. A PET scanner detects gamma rays produced when positrons, emitted by a tracer, meet electrons in the body. You benefit from the same physics that Dirac predicted, and that is one reason the topic matters beyond the cosmos. The machine in the hospital is a working demonstration of the theory.

Physicists also look for antimatter in the cosmos. The AMS-02 detector on the International Space Station searches cosmic rays for heavier antinuclei, and a discovery of a large antimatter region would change the story completely. So far, no such structure has been found. We should be careful, though, because absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and the search is still young.

Here is a question worth asking. If there were an antimatter galaxy far away, how would we know? We would look for gamma rays from matter and antimatter meeting at a boundary. You can see why that search is so difficult, and why each null result still tells us something about the universe.

I think the most interesting result is the tension between symmetry and existence. If you wrote down the laws of physics in a perfectly symmetric way, you would predict a universe with almost nothing in it. The fact that we exist means that nature broke the symmetry somewhere, and we are still trying to find out where. That is a humbling thought, and it is one reason the question keeps drawing people back.

I like that tension, because it keeps the subject alive. Every answer seems to raise another question. If you ever lose interest in physics, the question of antimatter is a good place to start again, because it connects quantum fields, cosmology and the details of a single decay.

So why is there antimatter? The short answer is that the equations require it. Dirac's mathematics demanded a partner for every particle, and experiments confirmed that partner again and again. The longer answer is harder. Why is there matter at all? That is the open question, and it may depend on physics we have not yet seen. We can measure the surplus precisely, but we cannot yet explain its origin.

Go back to the opening. Antimatter is the strangest substance most of us have never seen, and the universe seems to be made almost entirely of ordinary matter. The two are linked by the same equation, and the same symmetry that predicted the partner also demands an explanation for the missing one. You can see the puzzle in a cloud chamber, in a hospital scanner and in the sky above you. Why is there antimatter? It is one of the questions that will keep physicists busy for a very long time.
