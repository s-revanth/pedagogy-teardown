"We need help." "From whom?" "Anyone with an army." "We have two options." "Rome and Carthage." "Which one?" "Both?" "Let's ask both."

That is roughly how the First Punic War began, and if you are wondering whether that was a good idea, you are not alone. Spoiler: it was not.

Let's go back to 264 BC. Rome and Carthage were the two biggest powers in the western Mediterranean, and both of them were quietly staring at the same small island. Sicily sits in the middle of the sea. It covers roughly 25,000 square kilometres, and it is close enough to Africa to be worrying and close enough to Italy to be tempting. Whoever controlled Sicily could control the trade routes around it, and that is a very good reason to fight.

The immediate trigger was a small city called Messana, on the northeastern tip of the island. The strait is 3 kilometres wide at its narrowest point. A band of Campanian mercenaries, known as the Mamertines, had seized the city decades earlier and made themselves its rulers. They were not popular with their neighbours, and they were worried about being attacked. So they did what desperate people often do. They wrote to both Rome and Carthage and asked for help. I find it striking how little these men had to offer. They had a fortress, a grudge, and a shrinking list of friends. And yet a few letters were enough to start a war between two of the biggest powers of the age. How many letters did it take? One each. Nobody would have guessed that a simple request could do so much damage.

Rome and Carthage were both happy to help, in the sense that both wanted to be the ones who got the city. The Carthaginians sent a garrison. The Romans had a harder choice to make, because helping the Mamertines meant sending an army across the strait, and Rome had never fought a war on an island before. If you were a Roman senator, would you send an army across the water? Trusting the sea was another question, and so was trusting the Mamertines. If you had never sailed, the idea of a long crossing would worry you. I would have asked a few uncomfortable questions before saying yes. Would you feel confident that your soldiers could even get there?

The Senate debated the question, and the debate was not short. I have sat through enough long meetings to recognise the pattern. Eventually somebody says the thing everyone was hoping to avoid, and the room suddenly agrees. Some senators argued that Rome could not afford a war with Carthage, because Carthage was rich, experienced, and had a navy. Others argued that a city of mercenaries was a dangerous precedent, and that letting Carthage control Sicily would be a much greater danger. The people who wanted war were louder, and the people who wanted peace were more sensible.

I think the choice of commander says a lot about the mood in the Senate. A cautious man was not the obvious choice. Rome wanted action, and you would have wanted it too, if your city were under threat. Rome got its action. Rome's soldiers had been trained for fighting on land, in formation, against other armies on flat ground. They had never been a serious naval power. Getting across a narrow strait with enough men and supplies was a real problem, and it was the first sign of what the war would cost.

Rome was a wrestler asked to fight in a swimming pool. He is strong, he is determined, and he has no idea how to move in water. Rome was a land power facing a sea power, and every step of the war would show that mismatch. The Carthaginians had the navy, the harbours, and the trade networks. Rome had the legions, the discipline, and a stubbornness that would take a very long time to exhaust.

The first Roman successes were on land. They were also surprisingly lucky. I think a lot of ancient wars were decided by one bad decision followed by another. Messana was secured, and a Carthaginian garrison was pushed out. How many men did it take? Enough to hold the walls, but not enough to hold the whole island. You can see where this is heading. Rome needed more, and it needed it fast. Then a bigger prize came into view: the city of Syracuse, ruled by Hiero II, a king who had been allied with Carthage. Hiero was a practical man, and he took a long look at the situation before deciding which side was going to win.

Hiero switched sides and joined Rome. I think a king who changes sides for practical reasons is one of the most common characters in ancient history. Would you have switched? I might have, if the grain was on offer. He became one of Rome's most reliable allies, and he sent grain, supplies, and money to the Roman armies on the island. He ruled for more than 50 years, which gave Rome a rare thing in this war, a stable partner. That was a huge gain, because it meant Rome could feed its soldiers without relying entirely on ships. It also meant Carthage had lost a powerful friend, and that Rome had a local partner who knew the island far better than the Romans did.

Then the war moved to Agrigentum. This was one of the largest cities on the southern coast of Sicily in the 3rd century BC, with strong walls, plenty of supplies, and a Carthaginian army that was determined to keep it. The Romans laid siege to the city, and the siege turned into a grinding test of patience. It lasted seven months, which sounds short until you are the one inside the walls. Both sides had men on the walls, men in the camps, and men running out of food.

The Roman siege lines were pinned in place by Carthaginian counterattacks, and the Carthaginians had a secret weapon of sorts: war elephants. Elephants could smash through formations and frighten inexperienced soldiers, and for a while they worked. But elephants need feeding too, and a siege is a contest of supply lines. The Carthaginian relief attempts kept failing, and the Romans kept the blockade tight. Why did the relief attempts fail? Partly because the Romans controlled the land routes around the city, and partly because a relief army has to get through a wall of soldiers before it can do anything useful. Inside Agrigentum, the situation got worse by the week. Food ran short, and the people who had stored grain in good times began to feel very popular. Did the defenders expect a long siege? Some did, and they had stocked up. Others did not, and they paid for it with their hunger. Which kind of city would you rather be in? Neither, I hope. Food ran short, prices went through the roof, and the people who had the most to lose began to panic. The Carthaginian commanders tried to bring relief from outside, but each attempt was costly and most of them failed. I would have watched that horizon every morning, hoping for a sail. Eventually, in 261 BC, the city was starved into surrender, and the Romans took it.

The fall of Agrigentum was a big deal. It was also an expensive lesson for everyone who had underestimated the siege. I have always found it funny how often wars are decided by who runs out of food first. Historians love dramatic battles, but hunger tends to decide the outcome quietly. It gave Rome a major prize overseas and proved that its armies could win a long siege, even against a sea power and its elephants. It also made the Romans more confident. Some of them began to think that the war could be won quickly, but that confidence was about to be tested in ways no legion had been trained for. I should say that the sources for the 260s BC are thinner than we would like, and historians fill in the gaps with informed reasoning. Here is the problem, though. Winning on land was not the same as winning the war. That was the hard part. Nobody liked that answer. That war would run for 23 years, and it was only getting started. Carthage still ruled the waves, and every Roman army on Sicily depended on ships that could be sunk, delayed, or cut off. A single storm could undo months of planning. I have never understood why anyone thought a navy would be simple to build, and the Romans clearly did not either. Rome could take cities all day long, but if it could not control the sea, the supplies would keep running out and the armies would keep getting stranded.

I find that a fascinating turning point, because it shows a basic truth about war. Land victories can look decisive, but they do not feed an army across a hundred miles of open water. Rome had to become a naval power, or it would lose the thing it had already won. Do you think a republic built on land could make that change quickly? The Romans were about to find out.

Rome had almost no navy worth the name. I would not have wanted to be the admiral who had to explain that to the Senate. It had some transport vessels, some improvised boats, and a lot of soldiers who had never spent a day at sea. The fleet it needed did not exist yet, and building one would require money, timber, rowers, and a lot of nerve. Every Roman general on Sicily could see the problem clearly. The question was who would solve it.

So what would you build first if you had to win at sea with no ships? Would you copy the enemy? Would you invent something new? Would you simply hope the storms stayed away? Most generals would pick the first, and the Romans picked a version of it, with a twist of their own. A bigger ship, a faster ship, or a completely new way to fight? The answer the Romans came up with was strange, and it changed everything that followed.
