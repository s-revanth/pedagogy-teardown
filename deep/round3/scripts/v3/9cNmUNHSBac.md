<<<NARRATION
A bicycle is one of the simplest machines ever built, and almost nobody can explain how it stays upright. Most of us learned to ride as children, and most of us still believe a half-true story about why it works. Ask around, and people will tell you that spinning wheels keep the bike balanced, like a top. I believed that too, until I started checking. The real answer is more interesting, and it is hiding in the shape of the front fork.

This video is sponsored by a meal-kit service that sends pre-measured ingredients to your door each week. If you want to cook more at home, it is worth a look for yourself.

The gyroscope story goes like this. A spinning wheel resists being tilted, and a bicycle wheel spins fast, so the wheel must be what keeps you up. It sounds convincing, and you can even feel a little of it when you hold a spinning wheel by its axle and try to turn it. So is the gyroscope the secret? Many people would be surprised to hear that the answer is mostly no.

I want to be clear that a bicycle wheel is not a large gyroscope. A typical road bike wheel is about 700 millimetres across, weighs a kilogram or so, and turns at roughly 160 revolutions per minute at 20 kilometres an hour. I can see why the idea is tempting, but the numbers are modest. A rider and bike together weigh around 85 kilograms, and the wheels are only a small part of that mass.

Here is the test that changed how people think about it. In 1970, the engineer David Jones built a bicycle whose wheels spun in opposite directions, so that their gyroscopic effects largely cancelled. Then he rode it. It was still rideable, although it felt strange. If the spinning wheels were the main reason for stability, that bike should have fallen over right away. So the gyroscope cannot be the whole explanation, and I think that is the first real clue.

I want to be careful about what the test shows. A counter-rotating wheel does not make a bike perfectly stable, and the ride was clearly different. Still, the experiment removes the main argument for the gyroscope. The effect is real, but it is small compared with the forces from steering and gravity.

So what does keep the bike upright? The answer lies in the geometry. Look at the front wheel and you will notice that the axle does not line up with the steering tube. The fork is angled, and the spot where the tire touches the ground sits slightly behind the point where the steering axis would meet the ground. That offset has a name. Engineers call it trail, and it is the key to the whole story, along with two other factors.

Measured on an ordinary bicycle, trail is typically 50 to 60 millimetres. That is only about the width of two fingers, but it is enough to matter. The steering axis is usually tilted at around 72 degrees from the ground, so the fork angles just enough to create the offset. We can measure these numbers in a few minutes with a tape and a protractor, and the results are reassuringly consistent from frame to frame.

You already know how trail works, because you have seen it on a shopping cart. A cart has casters that swivel, and each wheel trails behind the pivot. Push the cart sideways, and the wheels swing around until they line up behind you again. A bicycle does the same thing. When you lean the bike, the trailing front wheel steers itself back under you, and the bike rolls on in a straight line. Can we feel that happen on a quiet road? Most riders can, once they know to look for it.

The effect is simple to test with a real cart. Roll it sideways across a car park and the front casters swing around within one or two seconds. A bicycle does the same thing with its fork. The forces are larger and the timing is faster, but the principle is identical, and it is one of the cleanest examples of feedback I know.

Can we see the catch with the steering? If the wheel always trails, the bike should always want to turn back toward where it is leaning. That is exactly what happens, and it is why you can let go of the bars at speed and the bike keeps going for a moment. Why does speed matter here? Because the self-steering works better when the wheels are rolling fast enough to respond.

How do we know geometry is the reason? A 2007 study modelled the bicycle mathematically and found that, with the right geometry, a riderless bike can be stable at speed. It can roll along, nearly upright, without anyone steering. The gyroscope was in the model too, and it played a smaller role than many people expected. Trail and the way the mass is spread across the frame did most of the work. That result surprised me, and I expect it will surprise many readers too.

The model also shows a threshold. Below a certain speed, a riderless bicycle falls over, and above it the bicycle stabilises itself. The threshold depends on geometry, which is why small changes in the fork angle matter so much to frame builders. Our intuition says a faster bike should be harder to balance, and the model reverses that intuition for speed.

Now try something harder. If the bike wants to stay upright, how do you turn it? Most of us think you steer toward the direction you want to go. At speed, the bars move the opposite way first, and a rider will notice it right away. You push the left handlebar forward, and the bike leans left. Only then does the front wheel steer into the turn. Why would a bike need to lean before it can turn at all?

At 20 kilometres an hour, a rider leans only a few degrees for a gentle bend, and the steering input needed is tiny. A sharp corner at the same speed can call for a lean of 30 degrees or more, and the handlebars do the work in less than half a second. I have seen riders do this without thinking, which is part of the point. The control loop runs below conscious thought.

The answer is that turning needs a sideways force, and leaning gives you one. When the bike leans left, gravity pulls the bike and rider to the left, and the tires push back toward the centre of the curve. That push is what bends your path. The lean is not a side effect of turning. It is the mechanism. Steering is simply how you create the lean in the first place.

The lean angle follows a simple relation. The sideways force needed depends on the speed squared, divided by the radius of the turn. Double the speed, and a rider needs four times the sideways force, which is why fast riders lean so far into a corner. We can write that down in one line, and it matches what riders feel on every descent.

So the common myth gets the physics backwards. The wheel is not holding you up like a top on a string. It is a small part of the story, and the bigger part is the lean, the trail and the way you steer. Many people believe the opposite, and I find that surprising every time I read the old explanations. The gyroscope feels like the answer because it is the one effect you can feel with your own hands.

Real bikes are more complicated than any diagram. Tires deform under load, frames flex, and the rider's body shifts the mass as they move. For instance, a flexing fork changes the effective trail by a few millimetres. What happens when the road is wet? The simple model captures the main effect, but it does not explain every wobble you might feel on a rough road. Engineers still refine these models, and I think it is healthy that they do. Treat trail as the core idea, not as the entire story.

I think this is where the physics gets honest. A bicycle is a compromise between geometry, mass and the rider, and no single number tells the whole story. A modern frame might have a head angle between 71 and 73 degrees, and I find that narrow window remarkable. A typical bike frame weighs around 10 kilograms. That is why bike designers test prototypes on the road and in the lab, instead of relying on one model alone.

Slow down, and everything changes. At walking speed, around 5 kilometres an hour, a bicycle is very hard to balance, and that is why children wobble so much when they first ride. The self-steering from trail is weaker when the wheels are turning slowly, so the rider has to act. Your body becomes part of the control system, making small corrections many times a second. That is also why a bike is easy to ride fast and surprisingly hard to ride slowly.

A child on a balance bike, which has no pedals, learns this the hard way. Our nervous systems are remarkably good at balance, and most children can coast within two or three weeks of practice. That is not because they understand trail. It is because the feedback loop is fast, and the body learns the corrections before the mind can name them. I find that the hardest part of riding is those first few seconds.

Think about what you do when you hit a bump at 20 kilometres an hour. Your hands move a little, and you hardly think about it. Your body is making the corrections for you. The bike is doing part of the work, and you are doing the rest. Most riders probably never notice the balance of forces, yet the body has been solving that problem since childhood.

For example, racing bikes and touring bikes use the same basic geometry, but they make different choices. A racing bike may have less trail, with a race frame sitting near 45 millimetres, which makes it steer quickly and respond sharply. A touring bike has more trail, often above 60 millimetres, which makes it feel calm at speed, even with heavy bags on the back. The designers are trading stability for responsiveness. Every bike is a compromise between those two goals, and you can feel the trade-off the moment you ride one.

We can check the idea on almost any bicycle. Lift the front wheel slightly and let it spin freely for about 10 seconds, then tilt the frame and watch the fork swing toward the lean. The wheel turns itself into the direction of the fall. We do not need a laboratory to see the principle at work. A cheap bike in a garage will show it just as clearly as an expensive racing frame.

So go back to the bike you rode as a child. It was never held up by a spinning wheel. It was held up by a fork angled just so, a lean that you steer into, and a rider who keeps making small corrections. Almost nobody can explain how a bicycle stays upright, and now you can.
