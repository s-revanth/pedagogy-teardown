Some 40,000 people die on American roads every year, and 1.2 million die worldwide. I think most of us have stopped treating those numbers as a crisis, because they arrive so steadily. Driverless cars offer a real way to change that, and yet they make many people uneasy. Why would you want a machine behind the wheel, and why should you want one on the road now?

Start with the people behind the wheel. The United States National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has estimated that human error plays a role in nine in ten serious crashes. Those errors are rarely dramatic. A driver might glance at a phone for two seconds, misjudges a gap, or drives tired after a long shift. Can you think of a crash you have seen or heard about that did not involve a moment like that?

Take a closer look at the numbers. Speeding, impairment and distraction show up again and again in crash reports. You do not need to be a statistician to see the pattern. If you drive for an hour a day for thirty years, you will have spent thousands of hours behind the wheel, and every one of those hours is a chance for something to go wrong. You know how easily attention slips, because you have felt it yourself.

Machines do not get bored, drunk or distracted by a text message. A car with a good sensor suite can watch in every direction at once, which no human can do. I find that the most persuasive part of the case. You will still have doubts, and you should. Machines may fail in their own ways, and those failures can be strange, so the honest comparison is harder than it first looks.

I think it is worth saying what the car cannot do. It cannot read the mood of a crowd the way you can, and it cannot always predict a driver who is about to make an unusual move. Those limits matter. A system that performs well on average can still fail badly in a rare situation, and you should ask how it behaves when the rules of the road break down.

So how does a driverless car see? Most systems combine cameras, radar and lidar. Cameras read colour and texture, radar works through fog and rain, and lidar uses pulses of laser light to build a three-dimensional map of nearby objects. Each sensor has blind spots, so the software has to combine them. We can picture the car as a team of imperfect witnesses that must agree before it acts.

How would you design the software to resolve a disagreement between sensors? Most engineers would say the answer is redundancy, meaning that no single sensor is trusted alone. I find that principle elegant, because it mirrors how people cross-check their own senses. If we hear a car and see nothing, we look again before stepping off the kerb.

Engineers describe automation in levels, from zero to five. At the lower levels, the human still drives and the car offers help, such as cruise control or lane keeping. At level four, the car can drive itself within a defined area and set of conditions, and at level five it can drive anywhere a person could. Most of the public debate is about levels four and five, but many of the cars on your street sit much lower on that scale.

Here is a practical way to think about the levels. At zero, you do everything. At one or two, you share the work with the car. At three, the car drives in some conditions, but you must be ready to take over. You can see why the middle levels are the most difficult, because the human is asked to supervise a system that rarely needs help. We can all remember how hard it is to stay alert on a long, boring drive.

The idea is older than most people realise. In 2004, the US Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency, known as DARPA, ran a desert race for autonomous vehicles, and none of the entrants finished the course. A year later, a team from Stanford won a new race over a different desert route. That jump, from no finishers to a winner in about a year, is one reason the field moved so fast.

Think about how far the field has come since then. In 2005, the winning car finished a course of a little over 130 miles. Today, driverless services operate in several cities, although they cover limited areas and follow tight rules. I find that progress remarkable, and you will find it surprising too.

Would you trust a machine that never needed sleep? Many people say no at first, and I understand why. A human driver learns from mistakes over a lifetime, while a fleet can learn from what every car in it has encountered. That difference matters, and you should understand it before deciding. The question is not whether a machine is perfect. The question is whether it is better than the drivers we already have.

Think about the last time you took a ride in a car with automation features. How much did you check it, and how much did you let it do its job? I think your own comfort tells you a lot about where the technology stands in your mind, and that is worth paying attention to.

How much evidence exists so far? Companies have reported millions of miles of driverless operation in limited areas, and some of the safety data look favourable in many comparisons. The comparisons are tricky, though. Driverless cars operate mostly in cities, at lower speeds and in good weather, while human crash rates include highways, night driving and bad weather. We need to compare like with like before drawing a firm conclusion.

Here is a question worth asking every time you read a headline about safety. Which miles are being counted, and which drivers are being compared? If you cannot answer those, you cannot judge the result. I would also ask whether the company published its methods, because a good claim should survive being checked by someone else.

The most serious case so far is a reminder of how much the details matter. In March 2018, a self-driving test vehicle operated by a ride-hailing company struck and killed a pedestrian in Tempe, Arizona. The investigation pointed to software that did not correctly identify her as someone crossing the road, and to a safety driver who may have been looking down rather than watching the road. We can learn a great deal from a failure like that, and I think it should make all of us more careful about how tests are run. We need to hold the testers to the same standard we expect from everyone else.

So what does the honest case look like? Start with the scale. If a driverless system reduced crashes by even a modest share, the lives saved would run into the thousands each year in the United States alone. Can a machine really match that? I think the evidence so far points to it being able to in limited settings, but the evidence is still early, and I would not pretend otherwise.

The hard part is the edge case. A ball bouncing into a street is a classic example, because a child might follow it. A human driver reads context, such as eye contact and body language, that a machine can miss. If you have ever driven past a ball and a child, you know how fast that choice has to be. Engineers try to cover those cases with simulation, which can run billions of virtual miles, but no simulation covers everything.

We can all name a moment when a split second saved us, and that is exactly why these cases matter. Engineers know that the rare situations are the ones that define a system's reputation, so they spend a great deal of effort on them.

Why do people fear driverless cars more than ordinary cars? I think it is because the failures feel different. A human crash feels like a mistake. A machine crash feels like a system failing, and that feels more frightening, even when the numbers point the other way. You can see that bias in many public surveys, and it is worth noticing in ourselves.

Most people would not feel safer in a car they could not explain, and they say so. They would not, even if the car is safer in aggregate. I think that tells us something about trust. We rarely trust numbers alone, and we trust people we can question, which means the explanation matters as much as the result.

Who is responsible when a driverless car makes a mistake? The owner, the manufacturer, the software developer or the company running the service? Lawmakers are still working out these questions, and the answers will shape how fast the technology spreads. We need clear rules, not a patchwork of local decisions. I find that gap as important as the engineering itself.

Would you accept a rule that puts the blame on a company, even when the owner was not at fault? Many people say yes, and many say no. I think you should be able to hear both sides before deciding, because the answer shapes what gets built.

Some people bring up the trolley problem, asking whether a car should sacrifice one person to save five. It is a striking puzzle, but I think it distracts from the everyday issue. Most crashes are not moral dilemmas. They are sudden, messy events where a faster reaction matters more than a clever rule. The practical question is whether the machine avoids the situations that cause those crashes in the first place.

There is also the question of who benefits. Older adults, people with disabilities and anyone unable to drive can gain a new kind of independence. Their daily mobility depends on a licence they cannot hold, so a driverless service can change ordinary life. You can picture a neighbour who would benefit from that. We should count those gains when we weigh the risks, not treat them as an afterthought.

For example, consider a parent who cannot drive a child to school, or a veteran who needs a reliable ride to a clinic. For them, the technology is not a luxury. It is a way back into the everyday world, and you can measure that in appointments kept and jobs held.

Go back to the case itself. Why should you want driverless cars on the roads now, rather than later? Because each year of delay carries a cost measured in human lives. Some 40,000 people die on American roads annually, and while a better system waits on the shelf, some of those deaths will happen anyway. That is not a reason to rush. It is a reason to test carefully, and to test in public, with clear data.

What would a sensible rollout look like? I would start with limited areas, clear reporting rules and independent audits. You would want the data published, the failures investigated in the open and the software updated when problems show up. We can build that process now, instead of waiting for a perfect system. A phased approach lets the public see the evidence as it accumulates, and that trust is worth more than any single launch.

I want to be honest about the uncertainty. We do not yet know how driverless cars will perform across every climate, every road and every kind of driver. Some early systems have struggled with unusual road markings, and some have been confused by construction zones. These are real limits, and they should be reported as clearly as the successes.

Here is a number I keep coming back to. If a system can prevent even a fraction of those deaths, the benefit would be measured in thousands of lives each year in one country alone. We should be honest that this is an estimate, and we should also be honest that waiting has a cost. I think the debate goes better when people hold both of those truths together.

The question is not whether driverless cars are perfect, because nothing is. The question is whether they are better enough, and safer enough, to be worth the careful work of getting them right. I think the answer is yes, but only if we demand evidence at every step. Some 40,000 people die on American roads every year, and 1.2 million die worldwide. You can help make sure the evidence keeps coming, and that the numbers keep falling.
