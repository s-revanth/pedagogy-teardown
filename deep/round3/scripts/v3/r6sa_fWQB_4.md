Here's the roadmap for this video. First we'll count the people who have already lived, then we'll estimate how many babies are born each year and multiply that flow by how long humanity lasts. After that we'll look at a statistical argument that gives a range without requiring a confident forecast of the future, and finally we'll ask how much any of this deserves trust.

Let's begin with the past, because researchers who study population history have worked hard on it, and their estimates keep landing in a tight band. For the rest of this calculation, I'll use the round figure we started with, since it sits comfortably inside those estimates and it gives us a clean anchor. Everything that follows is built on top of that single number, so it's worth remembering that the past is the one part of this calculation we can actually check.

The history matters here, because the population didn't always look like it does today. Only a few centuries ago the planet held a small fraction of today's people, and most of the total came from very recent times. Most of the people who have ever lived were born in the last several millennia, since the population stayed small for most of prehistory. That means the past total is dominated by recent centuries, and the birth rates of those centuries are what really drive our estimate.

If we hold that rate steady and keep the population stable, the number of people born each year stays close to the same level. Why does that steady flow matter so much for the total? Because every future person has to arrive through it.

Here is where the calculation gets delicate. The flow of births depends on the population, and the population depends on how long people live. Suppose the average person lives 80 years. Then a population of 10 billion produces 125 million births a year, which is in line with what we see today. That consistency check matters, because it tells us the numbers hang together, so we can treat 125 million as a working figure for the rest of this video.
