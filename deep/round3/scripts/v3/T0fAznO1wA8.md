## Narration

<<<NARRATION
The pirate captain was not really the boss. I know, that sounds backwards. A captain is supposed to be in charge. But on many pirate ships in the early 1700s, the captain was elected by the crew, and the crew could vote him out. I think that is a strange setup for a criminal enterprise, and it is worth understanding why it worked.

The usual image is a black flag, a ship, and a captain who says what goes. That image is mostly wrong. I would say the captain was closer to a hired leader than a king. His job depended on the people under him staying satisfied enough to keep him there. That is a very different picture from the one most of us grew up with.

Start with the navy, because the comparison is useful. A navy captain got his job from above. The government appointed him, and the crew had no say in it. If he was cruel, the men could complain. They could not replace him. Discipline ran from the top down, and the sailors at the bottom had almost no choice about who gave the orders.

Pirates had a different problem. Nobody above them was going to appoint anyone. There was no navy office to send out a commission, and every man on board was technically an outlaw. So the crews had to build their own rules. They also had to make those rules stick without any government to enforce them. Whatever they came up with, they had to agree on it, or the ship would not hold together for long.

The rules were called articles. Crews wrote them down and signed them, and often every crew member put his name or his mark to them. The articles covered how loot would be shared, who got what share, what happened to men who were hurt, and which behavior would get you punished. Drinking and gambling were regulated. Stealing from your own shipmates was a serious offense, and I suspect that surprises people more than anything else on the list.

Now the captain. Captains were elected. The crew picked someone, because he had proven himself as a fighter or a navigator. Then the crew kept the right to remove him. This is the part that surprises people most. You might expect the captain to have absolute power, and you would be wrong. Instead the captain was a kind of manager, with a bigger cut of the loot and a bigger role in battle. His position was always open to a vote, and that is a fact worth remembering.

The money made the difference visible. In some sets of articles, the captain received about two shares of the loot, and the quartermaster received about one and a half. The ordinary crew member received one. That is a real advantage, but notice how small the gap is. A captain with two shares had something to protect, and not nearly enough to ignore his crew.

Two things made this work. First, the captain had real authority during a fight. When a ship was chasing a prize or defending itself, nobody wanted a committee debating every move. Second, outside of battle, the crew had the final say on most things that mattered. Whether to attack a ship, which way to sail, or whether to keep a prisoner, the crew often argued it out. The captain had to persuade them. If he could not, the crew had other options, and they did not hesitate to use them.

Then there was the quartermaster. In practice he had three jobs: dividing the loot, settling disputes between crew members, and checking whatever the captain wanted to do. Often he was the crew's voice on the ship. If you were a crew member with a complaint, the quartermaster was the person you went to. If you were the captain, the quartermaster was the person who could make your life difficult in a hurry.

How many people had to agree before a decision stood? Often the answer was simple. A ship of fifty or more men could not run on one person's whim for long, and every man on board knew it. The crew did not need a formal parliament. It needed enough people to say no at the same time.

One detail tells you a lot about how these crews thought. The articles often spelled out compensation for injuries. Losing a limb, or an eye, was a real risk on a pirate ship, and the rules gave a fixed payment for it. The crew was saying, in writing, that the men taking the risks would be paid for them. It is difficult to believe a navy would put that in quite the same plain terms. That alone should probably make you pause before calling the captain a tyrant.

Now, I do not want to oversell this. Calling a pirate ship a democracy would be a stretch. Pirate crews were not gentle places. Violence was common, punishments could be brutal, and a crew that voted badly could end up hanged. A vote does not make a ship safe, and it does not make the people on it nice. What it did was let the crew replace a bad leader without a bloody mutiny every time. Was that enough to make the system fair? Not always. That matters a great deal, even if the rest of the picture is ugly.

The sources need some care too. Most of what we know comes from a few places, including a book published in 1724 that describes these crews in lively detail. Its authorship has been debated, and some of its stories may be embellished. I would not stake everything on any one anecdote. Still, the surviving articles and the trial records point in the same direction. Which is why the overall picture is believable, even if the individual stories are not.

This is not only about pirates. Any group that depends on people choosing to stay has a reason to make its leader answerable to them. A ship at sea with a crew of a hundred men and no government behind it cannot afford a tyrant for very long. The pirates figured that out the hard way. They built a system that kept the captain useful and easy to replace. The same lesson shows up in unions, in clubs, and in plenty of workplaces today.

So ask yourself a question. If you were a sailor with no government to appeal to, how would you protect yourself from the people giving the orders? Would you trust one man with the whole ship? Would you want a say, a share, a written rule, and a way out? I suspect you would. The pirates wanted exactly those things, and they were willing to write them down.

Now put the pieces together. The captain led the fight. The crew decided whether the fight was worth having. The articles held the two together, so that neither side could simply take the ship from the other. Once you see that structure, the title makes sense. The captain was not really the boss. He was the person holding the job, on the crew's terms, and the crew kept the right to take it back.

So go back to the start. The pirate captain was not really the boss. He worked for the crew, and the crew kept the right to fire him.
