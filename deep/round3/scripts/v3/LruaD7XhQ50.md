## Narration

<<<NARRATION
The federal government of the United States owns more land than the entire state of Alaska. I know that sounds wrong. Alaska is enormous, and it is the largest state by a wide margin. So how can the federal government own more? I want to walk through the numbers, because when you see them, the claim holds up.

Here is the short version. The federal government owns roughly 640 million acres, which works out to roughly a million square miles. Alaska covers 665,000 square miles in total, including its water. So the federal holdings are bigger. Do the arithmetic again, and the result does not change. Is that a rounding error? No. The title is not clickbait, and you can check it yourself.

Before going further, we need to define the term. Federal land is land that the national government owns, and a set of agencies manages it on behalf of the whole country. That is not the same as federal buildings. A post office sits on a small piece of federal property, but most federal holdings are open land. You will find forests, deserts, grasslands, mountains, and wildlife refuges. When people hear the phrase, they often picture a government office. What they should picture is a landscape.

Here is a way to see the pattern. Take a map of the country and color every piece of land by who owns it. Federal land gets its own color, state land gets another, and private land gets its own shade. Then look at how the colors are spread. Did you expect the federal color to be spread evenly? It is not. It is thick in the West, thin across much of the East, and nearly absent in some states.

That lopsided picture is the heart of the story. So how did it get that way? Much of the answer goes back to expansion. The country acquired huge tracts of land in a series of deals, and each deal put land under federal ownership from the start. Think of the federal estate as a pile of purchases, not as a plan.

Start with the Louisiana Purchase in 1803, which added a massive stretch of the middle of the continent. Then came the Mexican Cession in 1848, which brought in a large band of the Southwest. The Gadsden Purchase in 1853 filled in a little more. Finally, the 1867 purchase of Alaska from Russia added the biggest piece of all. We can see the pattern clearly. Each deal added land, and the land stayed in federal hands for a simple reason.

Owning land is a separate matter. Describing it is another. The Land Ordinance of 1785 set up a grid for surveying the public domain, so that land could be measured and sold in neat squares. Have you ever wondered why so many western roads run in straight lines? That grid is part of the reason. We still live with its shape, even when we do not see it.

Why did so much of it stay federal? This is where the West comes in. The Homestead Act of 1862 offered settlers 160 acres of land, which was generous in wetter places with good soil. But much of the West is dry, and a homestead that size was far too small to farm in many of those areas. So the land was too arid, too rugged, or too poor for the homestead model to work. The government kept it, and the holdings grew into the federal estate we have today.

Now, who actually runs all this land? The answer is a handful of agencies, each with its own mission. The Bureau of Land Management holds the largest share, hundreds of millions of acres, much of it desert and rangeland in the West. The Forest Service comes next, managing national forests and grasslands. The Fish and Wildlife Service runs refuges, and the National Park Service looks after the parks. Each agency has different rules, and that matters a lot on the ground. Have you ever wondered why a forest and a desert next door can have different rules? That is why.

Nevada is the most striking case. Roughly four-fifths of the state is federal land, which means most of Nevada is not owned by private citizens or by the state government. Alaska is also heavily federal, with the federal share of its land running around 60 percent. Those are the extremes, but the pattern shows up across much of the interior West. In those places, the federal government is not just a neighbor. It is often the biggest landowner in the room.

I drove across central Nevada on a narrow highway, and for long stretches I did not see any fence line. Small signs marked the federal boundary, and I found it striking how quickly the landscape changed as I crossed from one kind of land to the next. You can feel the ownership on the road, in a way you cannot see on a map. That is the part I keep coming back to.

Now the misconception. A lot of people assume federal land is locked away, like a museum nobody can enter. That is not how it works. People hike, camp, graze livestock, hunt, fish, and mine on much of it. You can drive on many public roads that cross it, and you can camp for free in many places. Some areas are off limits, and some are heavily regulated. But the default is not a fence. I think the myth survives because it is easy to repeat and hard to check.

So why does this matter? Land is among the few resources where ownership decides a great deal. Who owns the land decides who can build on it, who can drill on it, and who gets to hunt there in the fall. Those decisions shape local economies, and they shape the lives of people who live right next to the federal boundary. Ask a rancher in Nevada what federal land means to them, and then ask a homeowner in Ohio. You will probably hear very different answers.

I should be honest about the numbers. The figures I have given are rounded approximations, and they shift a little from report to report. Agencies revise their acreage, land changes hands now and then, and different sources count water, islands, and leases in different ways. We rely on agency reports, and those reports are updated over time. You can check the latest ones yourself. I would not treat any figure as exact. The comparison with Alaska, though, holds up across every source I know of, because the gap is large.

The land split is also a story about geography and history, not one decision. Eastern states were settled earlier, and much of their land passed into private hands long ago. The West was settled later, and in many places the government kept the land because nobody else wanted it or could use it. That is why you can drive across an eastern state without leaving private land, while in parts of the West you can go an hour and see nothing but federal boundaries. Have you noticed that? It is a striking contrast.

Here is a harder question. What happens when a wildfire crosses from federal land onto a private ranch? The answer depends on coordination between agencies and owners, and we should not assume it is simple. Every boundary is a place where responsibility can blur. That is a big reason ownership matters so much in practice, and why maps like these are more than decoration.

So who should decide how this land is used? Should the federal government manage it from Washington, or should the states and the people who live nearby have more say? Would you trust a faraway office to understand what a mountain valley needs? Or would you trust the local rancher, the local hunter, and the local conservation group to get it right? Reasonable people disagree about this. We will not settle it here, but you can decide for yourself where you stand.

Now, put the pieces together. The title is true on the numbers. The federal government owns roughly a million square miles of land, and Alaska is smaller than that. The more interesting question is not the size of the gap. It is the set of decisions that come with owning so much of the map. Who gets to decide, what rules apply, and who lives with the results? Those are the questions that matter most.

So go back to the start. The federal government owns more land than Alaska, and that fact opens the door to a much bigger question about who should hold the land and who should decide what happens on it.
NARRATION>>>
