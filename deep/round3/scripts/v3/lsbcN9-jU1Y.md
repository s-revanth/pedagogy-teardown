"Hannibal is coming." "With what?" "Elephants." "Over the Alps?" "Yes." "Who thought that was a good idea?" "Him."

That is more or less how the Romans heard about the second of three Punic Wars with Carthage. Spoiler: he got the elephants over the Alps, and the Romans did not enjoy the experience at all.

Let's set the stage. The First Punic War had ended only a couple of decades earlier, and both Rome and Carthage had come out of it bruised, bitter, and short of money. Carthage had lost Sicily and paid a heavy price for the peace. Many Carthaginians believed the lost territory had been stolen from them, and they wanted it back. That kind of resentment does not fade quickly. It simmers, and then it boils over.

Hannibal was the son of Hamilcar Barca, the commander who had held Mount Eryx for years. You may know the name from the last war, and if you do, you already have a sense of the family grudge. Hannibal grew up in the shadow of that stubbornness, and he took it with him to Spain. There, he built up a powerful army and a loyal following. By 219 BC, he was looking for a fight, and he found one in a city called Saguntum. He was 28 years old, confident, and well supplied. I would have been very nervous as a Roman, and you would have been too.

Saguntum was a town on the Spanish coast, and it was allied with Rome. Hannibal laid siege to it. The siege lasted months, and it ended with the city's destruction and its people dead or enslaved. That was the trigger. If you had been a Saguntine, you would have expected Rome to keep its word. Rome, which had promised to protect its ally, demanded that Carthage hand Hannibal over. Carthage refused, and the Senate declared war. The siege had lasted eight months, and Saguntum had fought with a grim determination that most Romans never expected. I find that the ultimatum is always the moment when the diplomats stop talking and the generals start planning. Did anyone in Carthage believe the Romans would really fight? Some did, and some did not, and the difference cost them dearly. I would have liked to know what the Carthaginian council was thinking that spring.

Would you attack a city if the other side had just promised to defend it? It is a bold question, and Hannibal clearly thought the answer was yes. He was a gambler who understood that a decisive move could make a rival hesitate. The problem was that Rome was not hesitating. Rome was preparing to fight in Spain, in Italy, and in Africa, all at once. Consider how big that plan was for a state that had only recently finished one war. Rome had to raise armies, pay for ships, and keep its allies calm. You would need to be very confident to take that on. I would not have been. Yet the Senate did it, and that tells you how serious the threat looked to them.

Hannibal's plan was simple in outline and extraordinary in execution. He would march his army from Spain, across southern Gaul, over the Pyrenees and the Rhône, and then over the Alps into northern Italy. The Romans expected him to come by sea, and they had a navy to meet him. I think that expectation was one of the great misjudgements of the war, and it cost Rome dearly. Hannibal did not come by sea. He came by land, and that was the surprise.

Now, the details of the crossing are uncertain. Sources differ about the exact pass, the numbers in the army, and the number of elephants that made it across. Some of the accounts were written long after the event, and some were shaped by a writer's desire for drama. So treat the route on any map with a little humility, because the ancient sources do not agree.

What is clear is that the Alps were a brutal obstacle. Snow, cliffs, icy paths, and steep valleys took a terrible toll on the army. Men died, animals died, and supplies were lost. The elephants struggled on the narrow tracks, and only a few of them survived to reach the plains of Italy. If you had to march an army over those passes, you would have counted every step. I find that image hard to forget. Even so, the army that came down the mountains was still formidable, and the Romans had not expected that. Before we go on, I want to be clear about the scale of what Hannibal achieved. The crossing took roughly fifteen days by the most common account, and the army came out the other side changed. You would expect a march like that to break an army. Instead, it made Hannibal's men tougher, and the Romans were about to find that out.

The first major clash came at the Trebia River in late 218 BC. The Romans advanced across a cold, swollen river, in winter conditions, and they were ambushed. Hannibal had placed his troops and his elephants in a way that made the Roman position very difficult to hold. The Roman army was beaten, and a large part of it was destroyed or scattered. It was a sharp lesson, and Rome was not used to losing so badly. I think the Trebia is the battle people skip, and that is a shame. It shows how quickly confidence can curdle into panic. The Romans had marched out expecting to crush a foreign army on their own terms. Instead they were fighting in freezing water, with their formations broken. You would be shaken too. I would be, certainly.

Then came Lake Trasimene in 217 BC. A Roman consul named Gaius Flaminius led an army along a narrow route beside the lake, and he did not realise that Hannibal's men were hidden in the hills above. A morning mist covered the lake, and when it lifted, the Romans were trapped. Flaminius was killed, and the Roman army was almost wiped out. It was one of the worst defeats the republic had suffered. Polybius puts the Roman dead at around fifteen thousand, with thousands more captured. How many men can a republic lose before it starts to think twice? Rome was about to find out.

The Senate was alarmed, and it appointed a new strategy. Fabius Maximus, an older and more cautious general, argued that Rome should avoid battle and wear Hannibal down by delay. He shadowed the Carthaginian army, cut off supplies where he could, and refused to fight in open ground. Fabius became known as the Delayer, and his approach was deeply unpopular with people who wanted a quick victory. He was elected consul five times, which tells you he was respected, even when he was disliked. Did the people trust him? Some did, and some thought he was a coward. I think the nickname was meant as an insult at first, and it became a badge of honour later. Would you have preferred a general who promised quick results, or one who promised to avoid a disaster?

Fabius was a patient landlord, waiting out a bad tenant rather than throwing him onto the street. The tenant is dangerous, and every day he stays is a cost. But the landlord has the time and the resources to wait, and the tenant does not. That was the logic, and it was sensible. The trouble was that a war without visible results is very hard to sell to an impatient public. You may have seen the same thing in modern politics. A strategy that works slowly looks like inaction until the results finally arrive. I find that pattern depressingly familiar.

"Why are we not fighting?" "Because he is winning." "Then why do we not stop him?" "Because we tried, and he won again." "So we just wait?" "Yes, we wait." "For how long?" "Until he gets tired." "He does not look tired." It was the kind of argument that could make a senator lose sleep, and many of them did.

Hannibal spent years in Italy, marching across the peninsula, raiding farms, and defeating Roman armies whenever they came close enough to fight. He spent fifteen years in Italy, which is a remarkable length of time for an army far from home. You might think a long campaign would wear down the invader. In practice, Hannibal's army stayed strong, because it could live off the land and the Romans kept giving it new targets to chase. He never besieged Rome itself, which is one of the great puzzles of the war. Historians have argued about his reasons for years, and I find that the lack of a clear answer makes the story more fascinating, not less. Did he lack the resources? Did he hope for a deal? We do not know for certain.

The defeat that changed everything came at Cannae in 216 BC. The Romans raised a huge army and decided to crush Hannibal in a single battle. They formed a deep central line, confident that the Carthaginian centre would give way. Hannibal let it bend, then pulled in the flanks, and the Roman army was caught in a double envelopment, surrounded on both sides.

The slaughter at Cannae was catastrophic. If you were a survivor, you would carry that day for the rest of your life. Tens of thousands of Roman soldiers died, and many of the survivors were captured or scattered. Sources put the Roman army at more than 80,000 men, and some 50,000 of them are said to have died that day. Those numbers come from ancient writers who may have had reasons to inflate or shrink them. I would treat them as a sign of scale rather than an exact count. It is one of the most studied battles in military history, and it still stands as a model of how to destroy an enemy army. It was also a moment of crisis for Rome, because the Roman army had been its main defence. You can understand the panic. A city that had survived the Gauls and the first war with Carthage was suddenly facing a general roaming its countryside with no army left to stop him. What would you have done in the Senate that week? Would you have sent envoys, or dug in? The Romans chose to dig in, and that choice made all the difference. I would call it the single most important decision of that year.

The city was in shock. Allies began to waver, money was tight, and the political class was desperate for answers. Some of Rome's allies in southern Italy started to look at Hannibal with fresh interest. Would they stay loyal? Would the republic survive this? The answer was not obvious, and Rome had to decide how to fight a war that had suddenly become existential.

What would you have done with a general who would not stop winning? Would you have offered peace, sent more troops, or found a way to fight him without giving him what he wanted? The Romans had to answer that question while their army lay dead on the plain of Cannae. I think that question is the real hinge of the whole war. Winning battles was not enough, because Hannibal could not force Rome to surrender. Rome, meanwhile, could keep raising armies for years. Who would give up first? The answer is less obvious than the battles suggest. The war would eventually last 17 years.

Next time we look at the second half of the war, with Scipio, the siege of Syracuse, and the long road to Africa. I promise it turns on a few very bold decisions. Thanks for watching, and I will see you then.
