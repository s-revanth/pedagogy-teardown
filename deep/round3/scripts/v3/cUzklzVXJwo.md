For centuries, mathematicians asked a simple question about numbers, and most of them insisted the answer could not exist. Take any number and multiply it by itself, and the result is positive. Multiply a negative number by itself, and you still get a positive result. So what number could possibly square to negative one, if you went looking for it? I think the people who asked that question were right to be suspicious, and that suspicion is part of the story.

The story begins in 1545 with an Italian mathematician named Gerolamo Cardano. He was not trying to invent anything strange. He wanted to solve a puzzle that many people had already worked on. Split the number 10 into a pair of parts whose product is 40. That sounds like a simple school problem, and it is. Try it for a moment. What pair of numbers adds up to 10 and multiplies to 40? You will find that no pair of ordinary numbers works.

Pause here if you want to try it yourself, and see how long you might last before you give up. Most of the mathematicians who looked at this problem did not have the tools you have now, and they had to do everything by hand.

Cardano worked through it anyway. Using a familiar formula, he got a pair of answers, each built from a square root of a negative number. Their sum is the original 10, and their product comes out exactly right. The algebra works perfectly, and I find that pretty remarkable. But the answers contain the square root of a negative number, and Cardano admitted that these quantities seemed useless.

If you have ever solved a quadratic equation, you have already seen the machinery. Call the two parts x and y, and we can write down their sum and their product. Substitute one into the other, and the formula does the rest. You can check the answers yourself by multiplying the two parts out, and you will land on the original product every time.

This video is sponsored by an online course platform that lets you practice math one short problem at a time. If you enjoy puzzles, you may find it worth a look.

Why would anyone keep working with an answer like that? Because the algebra kept producing sensible results. Cardano's cubic method, published in his book Ars Magna in 1545, gave real answers to equations that had defeated mathematicians for centuries. Sometimes, though, the formula passed through the square roots of negative numbers on its way to a perfectly real answer. That was the strange part, and it was the part that bothered people most.

Cardano did not work out the cubic method entirely on his own. He had learned it from another mathematician, Niccolò Tartaglia, after promising to keep it secret. When Cardano published it in Ars Magna, Tartaglia was furious, and the two men spent years in a bitter public dispute. So the method that produced those strange square roots arrived in print with a quarrel attached to it.

The breakthrough came from an engineer named Rafael Bombelli, who worked in Italy in the 1560s. He took Cardano's puzzles seriously and wanted to know what happens when you follow the rules all the way through. Consider the equation x cubed equals 15x plus 4. We already know one answer, the number 4, and we can check it. But Cardano's formula produced something else. It gave an answer involving the square root of a negative number.

Bombelli noticed something that made the whole thing click. He found that the cube of 2 plus i is 2 plus eleven times i. In other words, the cube roots the formula needed were a pair of conjugate numbers. Add them together, and the imaginary parts cancel. What remains is 4, the real answer we already knew. The route passed through a number that did not exist, and it still landed on the truth.

I find that moment remarkable. Bombelli did not understand the square root of negative one in any deep sense. But he treated it as a quantity with its own rules. He used it like any other number, checked the arithmetic, and it came out right every time. The rules were consistent, and I think that was enough for him to keep going.

Bombelli was also unusually careful about names. In his book L'Algebra, published in 1572, he gave the square root of negative one a name, plus of minus, and its opposite a name, minus of minus. He then wrote out the rules for multiplying these quantities, one by one. In effect, he was building a small arithmetic for a set of numbers that everyone else was still afraid to touch.

Not everyone was convinced. In 1637, the philosopher and mathematician René Descartes gave these numbers a name that stuck in the wrong way. He called them imaginary. He meant it as a dismissal, a way of saying they were not really numbers at all, only useful fictions. Centuries later, that word still shapes how people feel about them.

Negative numbers had their own long fight. Indian mathematicians such as Brahmagupta described rules for them in the seventh century, but many European mathematicians resisted them for centuries afterward. A negative answer was often called absurd, because it had no obvious meaning for a shopkeeper. Imaginary numbers probably looked like the next step in that same argument, and the reaction followed the same pattern.

Why would a careful thinker dismiss a tool that worked? Part of the answer is that numbers had always meant amounts. You can count a flock of sheep, or measure a length of cloth. A number whose square is negative does not match anything you can point to in a field or a shop. For a long time, the honest answer was that these numbers lived only inside the calculation and nowhere else.

The notation improved over time. Later, Leonhard Euler popularised the letter i for the square root of negative one, and the shorthand spread quickly. Euler also found one of the most beautiful relationships in mathematics. It links the exponential function, sine, cosine and imaginary numbers in a single identity. I won't derive it here, but you will probably find the result hard to forget. e to the i pi, plus one, equals zero.

The next big step was geometric. Around the turn of the century, mathematicians including Caspar Wessel, Jean-Robert Argand and later Carl Friedrich Gauss realised that imaginary numbers could be placed on a plane. The horizontal axis holds ordinary numbers, and the vertical axis holds multiples of i. Suddenly, a number like 2 plus 3i is simply a point you can locate. Multiplying by i becomes a quarter turn. That one picture made the whole system feel less mysterious.

Here is a striking twist. Wessel's paper was published in 1799, and for almost a century, hardly anyone noticed it. Argand had a similar idea and published it himself. The picture only became standard after Gauss gave it his authority. So why did it take so long to catch on? The simple geometry that makes complex numbers feel obvious spent decades in obscurity, waiting for the right audience.

Try this for yourself. Start with the number 1 and multiply it by i. You have turned the point a quarter of the way around the plane. Multiply by i again, and you are at negative 1, halfway around. Do it once more, and you are at negative i, three quarters of the way. One final multiplication brings you back to 1. Four quarter turns, and you are home. Where would you land after five multiplications?

Complex numbers also tidied up a long-standing problem. A polynomial of degree n, whose highest power is n, has exactly n roots once you allow complex numbers. A quadratic has two roots, and a cubic has three. Carl Friedrich Gauss proved this in his doctoral thesis in 1799, and it is now called the fundamental theorem of algebra. Without imaginary numbers, that sentence would be false.

We can see why this mattered, and you can see it too. Once we have a picture, we can reason about rotations, waves and oscillations with it. We no longer have to pretend the numbers are fake. We can ask where they land, how far they sit from zero, and how they turn. That is a very different experience from staring at a symbol that refuses to mean anything.

A small experiment you can run on paper makes this concrete. Take 2 plus 3i and square it. Multiply out the brackets, and the i squared term turns into a plain negative number. The answer is negative 5 plus 12i. Notice that the calculation never asked whether i was real. It only asked you to follow the rule, and the rule kept working. Would you trust a rule that never once failed you?

The idea did not stop at two dimensions. In 1843, William Rowan Hamilton was out walking in Dublin with his wife when he suddenly saw how to extend the idea to four dimensions. Legend says he carved the key rules into a bridge on the spot. Whether or not the carving happened exactly that way, the result was real. You can use quaternions, his new numbers, to describe rotations in three dimensions, and computer graphics relies on them every day.

Today, imaginary numbers are everywhere in engineering. Electrical engineers use them to describe alternating current, where voltage and current swing up and down in time. They often write j instead of i, because i is already taken by current. Quantum mechanics uses complex numbers at its core. For example, the wave describing a particle carries a complex phase. Signal processing, fluid dynamics and control theory depend on them too. Could you design a modern radio network without them?

Engineers do something similar with circuits. The resistance and the reactance of a component combine into one complex number called impedance, and the rules of complex arithmetic then tell you how voltages and currents relate. In quantum mechanics, the Schrodinger equation has an explicit i in it. That is not a typo, and you cannot remove it without changing the physics. If you tried, the equation would describe something else entirely.

Complex numbers also generate some of the most famous images in modern mathematics. The Mandelbrot set is built by repeatedly squaring a complex number and adding a constant, then checking whether the result stays bounded. Its boundary is so intricate that zooming in never runs out of new shapes. Each zoom reveals structure that looks like a small copy of the whole. Did anyone in 1545 imagine that a number they distrusted would draw pictures like that?

The set is named after Benoit Mandelbrot, who studied these shapes for decades. The first computer images of it appeared around 1980, and they caught people off guard. Perhaps the most striking thing is how little it takes. A simple rule, applied over and over to complex numbers, produced a shape you could never fully draw by hand.

I want to pause on that point, because it says something about how mathematics works. A concept can start as an attempt to solve a puzzle, get dismissed as nonsense, and later become a core tool. Nobody decided in advance that it was useful. It became useful after people kept using it and checking the results. That is a slow process, and I think it is the honest one.

What would you have said in 1545? Would you have treated the square root of a negative number as a real answer, or would you have thrown it out with the other nonsense? I suspect most of us would throw it out, and that is exactly why the story is worth telling. It is easy to be right with hindsight, and much harder to be the person who keeps calculating when everyone else has stopped.

So go back to the question at the start. What number, multiplied by itself, gives a negative result? Centuries ago, most people said none could exist. Today the answer is i, and it sits on a plane, turning every time we multiply by it. The mathematicians who were suspicious were right to ask. They just did not yet know how far the answer would take us.
