# Narration

<<<NARRATION
A black hole is the one place in the universe where gravity is so strong that nothing can escape, not even light. Something that simple should be easy to describe, yet black holes keep surprising the people who study them. Let's walk through their whole life, from birth to death, and see what they do in between.

The short version is that black holes are born when massive stars die, they grow by feeding, and they slowly fade over unimaginable timescales. Each of those stages is stranger than it sounds, so it helps us to slow down.

Every object has an escape velocity, the speed you would need to break free of its gravity. For Earth, that speed is roughly 11 kilometers per second. For a black hole, the escape velocity at a certain distance exceeds the speed of light, which means nothing can get back out once it crosses that line. That boundary is called the event horizon. Notice that the horizon is not a solid surface. It is a point of no return.

Here is a useful thought experiment. If we squeezed the Earth into a ball less than 2 centimeters across, it would become a black hole. For the Sun, the same trick requires a ball 6 kilometers across. What would that look like? The bigger the mass, the bigger the event horizon, so a black hole's size depends on its mass, not on how tightly you pack it. You can check the numbers yourself, and they line up.

Most black holes start life as stars. When a star many times more massive than the Sun runs out of fuel, its core can no longer hold itself up against gravity. In a violent supernova, the outer layers blow away, while the core collapses. If that core is more than 3 times the mass of the Sun, nothing can stop the collapse, and a stellar-mass black hole is born. That process takes only seconds, but its consequences last for billions of years. Astronomers now have strong evidence for many such objects, and we can measure their masses from the way their companion stars move. For example, that is how we identified several black holes in binary systems in our own galaxy.

Once a black hole exists, it can grow. Gas and dust spiral inward and form a flattened, glowing disc, and friction heats that material to millions of degrees before it crosses the horizon. That glow is one of the brightest things in the universe, and it is how we spot black holes that are feeding. If you could watch that disc from a safe distance, you would see light bending around the edge of the shadow. The biggest ones, supermassive black holes, sit at the centers of galaxies. Our galaxy's central black hole has roughly 4 million times the mass of the Sun, and in 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope revealed its shadow.

A common myth is that black holes vacuum up everything nearby. They do not. From a safe distance, a black hole pulls on things just the way any object of the same mass would. If we swapped the Sun for a black hole of the same mass, the Earth would keep orbiting just as before. The danger appears only when you get very close, where tidal forces stretch matter into a long thread, a process people jokingly call spaghettification. So what makes a black hole dangerous is not its size, but how close you are.

In 2015, the LIGO detectors recorded a ripple in spacetime from two black holes spiraling together more than 1.3 billion light years away. The signal, called GW150914, let us hear a black hole merger for the first time. Why does that matter? Because it gave astronomers a completely new sense, one that works even when the black holes are silent.

And what happens at the end? Stephen Hawking showed that quantum effects make black holes slowly radiate energy, which means they lose mass over time. For a black hole with the mass of the Sun, that process would take 10^67 years, far longer than the current age of the universe, which is 14 billion years. Nobody has seen this radiation directly, so we can only calculate it from theory. What happens after that? Some black holes may be among the last objects still around, long after every star has gone dark.

One caveat. We do not yet know what happens to information that falls into a black hole, and the physics of the final stages is still under active debate. Our best theories disagree in some details, so anyone who sounds completely certain is probably overselling it.

So a black hole is not just a monster in the sky. It is what happens when gravity wins, and it may be one of the last things still shining when the universe grows old. Every time a black hole swallows a star, or two of them collide far away, we learn more about how the universe is built. Our instruments keep improving, and one day a single detection could change the whole story. You are living at a time when we can finally listen to the universe's black holes, and that is a remarkable thing.
