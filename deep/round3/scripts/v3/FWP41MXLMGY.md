## Narration

<<<NARRATION
For a few weeks in 2020, London had the quietest roads I have ever seen, and I went out on a bicycle to ride them. I want to tell you what that was like, because the experience taught me something about how a city usually works. It also taught me how fast a city can change when it has to. Most of us never see that happen in our own lifetimes, and I did.

Did you ever wonder what a city sounds like without traffic? I did, and I still think about it. For most of my life, the sound of London has been a constant low roar. Buses, engines, horns, sirens, and the hiss of tyres on wet tarmac all layer on top of each other until you stop hearing them at all. Then, in the spring of 2020, that roar dropped away almost overnight. I stood on my doorstep and listened, and for a moment I could not quite believe it.

The reason was simple. The first lockdown in the United Kingdom began on 23 March 2020, and most people obeyed the instruction to stay home. Shops closed. Offices emptied. Schools shut their gates. Public transport was the hardest hit, and the Underground, which normally carries millions of trips a day, was running a small fraction of its usual load. The city became a set of quiet streets with a few people walking dogs and a few delivery vans moving slowly through them.

Think about what that meant for a commuter like me. Normally the problem is not how to get somewhere. The problem is how to get there without sharing a carriage with forty strangers. Once the trains were the risk, I had to find another way to move around the city. Which way would you have chosen? For a lot of people, the answer was a bicycle. I had one in the garage, and I had not ridden it properly in years.

So I went out. The first ride was short, and I was nervous, mostly because I had not ridden in traffic for a very long time. I half expected cars to appear from every side street. They did not. The main roads were quiet enough to hear my own wheels turning, and the cycle lanes were empty, which felt strange and wonderful at the same time. I remember thinking that this is what the city could always have been, if we had made different choices. The first real route I took was about eight miles out and back, and it took me a little under an hour each way. I stopped twice to check my tyres and once to watch a heron standing perfectly still in the shallows. You would be surprised how much you notice when nothing is trying to run you over.

By the second week, I was riding every day. I rode out along the river, then cut through the parks, then followed a canal towpath for longer than I had planned. The towpath was busy with other riders, joggers, and the occasional narrowboat, and I found myself slowing down to look at the water. You see a city differently from a bike. Every corner is close enough to read, and every hill becomes a small decision.

Then I noticed the bike shops. You could not buy a decent bike anywhere. I walked into one shop after another, and the shelves that had been full for years were nearly bare. The mechanics were working through repair queues that stretched for weeks, and some owners were waiting a month just to get a flat tyre fixed. I had never seen anything like it, and I still find it hard to explain how quickly the demand arrived. Shops reportedly sold more bikes in a few weeks than they would normally sell in a year. If you tried to buy a bike that spring, you probably waited.

Why did it happen so fast? Start with the obvious demand. Millions of people who had been taking the train suddenly needed another way to move. Bikes were cheap to run, easy to keep apart from other people, and they could be repaired locally by a single mechanic in a small workshop. Add in a city that was emptier of cars than anyone had seen for decades, and you get a boom that nobody planned. The supply chain simply could not keep up. Here is a rough way to think about the scale. If only one in twenty daily Underground riders switched to bikes, that still means a great many new riders across a city of nine million. No shop was ready for that.

The city noticed, too. Transport authorities and local councils began putting in temporary cycle lanes, often with nothing more than a line of plastic cones and a fresh coat of paint. The programme was known as Streetspace, and it was controversial from the start. I watched some of those lanes go in within days, which was impressive, and I also watched some of them spark arguments that went on for months. As I rode, I passed more than a dozen new stretches in just a few weeks. Some were a few hundred metres long, and others ran for a mile or more. Each one changed how I thought about the road beside it.

Some people loved the new lanes. Others hated them, and their complaints were loud. Drivers worried about congestion, shopkeepers worried about deliveries, and residents worried about cones that stayed out long after anyone expected them to. I think you can argue with any one of those complaints, and you can still take them seriously. What I could not argue with was how much quieter and calmer some of those streets felt. You could find arguments on both sides of the same street. A driver would complain about a queue at one junction, and a parent would thank you for the quieter road a couple of blocks over. Both reactions were true, and neither one settled the question on its own. Perhaps that is why the debate kept going.

What I found most interesting was who was actually riding. I saw grandparents on sturdy hybrid bikes, parents towing children in trailers, and office workers in suits with their trousers tucked into their socks. I also saw teenagers with cheap second-hand bikes, delivery riders who had been doing this for years, and people who had not owned a bicycle since they were children. The boom did not belong to a single group. That surprised me more than anything else. Ages ranged from children riding on the back of cargo bikes to riders in their seventies and eighties, and I did not expect that at all.

Was it all good? No, and I do not want to pretend otherwise. The growth was uneven. Some riders had good bikes and quiet routes, and they could take their time. Others were stuck with heavy traffic, poor lighting, and a bike that had been sitting in a damp shed for a decade. The boom was real, but it was not evenly shared, and I saw that unevenness every single day on the roads. If you rode one of the busy routes in those months, you learned quickly which junctions were dangerous and which were fine. You learned to read a lorry driver's mirrors, and you learned to make yourself seen. Those lessons were hard-won, and they were not shared evenly across the city.

I want to pause on that point, because it is an old pattern in transport. The new lanes made the biggest difference where the roads were wide enough to protect them. People who could afford a decent bike had the easiest time, and people who could not often had to make do. The newcomers are often the ones with the most to gain from a better street, and the least power to change the street itself.

Now the numbers, because they matter. Traffic fell sharply in the first weeks of the lockdown. Bus and tube use dropped to a small part of its usual level at the lowest point. Cycling counts rose on many routes, and on some of them the counts more than doubled within a few months. Those are striking figures, and I have tried to report them carefully rather than inflate them.

Here is what I think those figures hide. A traffic count is a snapshot of one moment on one street. It tells you how many bikes passed a counter, not who was riding them, why they were riding, or whether they will keep riding next year. The numbers are useful, but they are not the whole story. You should treat any single count with caution, including the ones I just gave you.

The caveat matters. Some of the changes were temporary, and traffic began to creep back as the lockdowns eased. Commuters returned to the trains slowly, and some of them have not come back in full. We do not know yet how many of the new riders kept cycling once the weather turned and the roads filled up again. We will only find out with longer data, and I am still waiting for it. Some of the counts I saw covered two weeks, others covered six months, and the counting methods differed from one council to the next. If you compare them directly, you are comparing unlike things.

Meanwhile, the city changed in ways that were easier to see. Some temporary lanes stayed. Some were reviewed and rebuilt with better barriers. Some were removed after fights with residents or businesses. None of that was tidy, and I would not have predicted the final pattern from the first week. I found it hard to keep up with the changes, even as someone who rode through them every day.

This is the part I keep coming back to. Cities can change quickly when they have to. A crisis forced London to make choices that would normally take years of consultation, and for a few months the streets were effectively rewritten. I saw a road that had been a traffic jam become a quiet street with children on it. That is a remarkable thing to witness. The real question is what happens after the crisis, when the old habits start trying to come back.

So imagine the same situation in your own city. Could you ride across it on a quiet morning, if the cars were mostly gone? What would you need in order to feel safe doing it? Would you want a lane that stays in place, or a lane that gives way to parked cars the moment the emergency ends? Those are not small questions, and they do not have easy answers.

There is one more thing I noticed, and it has stayed with me. The people I met on bikes were friendly in a way I had not expected. We nodded at each other at junctions, and we swapped tips about quiet routes and reliable repair shops. A shared problem can build a kind of community quickly, even among strangers who would never have spoken before. Nobody planned that, but it happened anyway, and I was glad to be part of it.

Looking back, I think the lesson is about choice. The emptiness of those spring streets showed how much of a street is a decision rather than a law of nature. Lanes, speed limits, parking, and the simple width of a road are all choices, and they can be made differently. Which of those choices survive the crisis is the real question, and it is still being answered. If you ask me what I took from those months, I would say it was a lesson in how much of the street is up to us. The cars were not forced off the road. They were simply not needed for a while, and that gap showed what else the road could hold. What else could it hold? How many of those lanes will still be there in ten years, and who will have fought for them?

So go back to the start. London had the quietest roads I had ever seen, and I went out on a bicycle to ride them. The roads were empty for a while, and the question now is what the city does with the space that a crisis gave it.
NARRATION>>>
