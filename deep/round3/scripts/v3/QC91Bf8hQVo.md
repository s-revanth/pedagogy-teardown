Some blobs flip coins that land heads more often than they should, and the job in this game is to catch them. That's the premise of this game from Primer, and it sounds simple until we start making decisions. Every blob brings its own coin to the table, and the blobs take turns flipping while we watch the results come in. We never see the coin itself, and that limit is what makes the whole game interesting, because the hardest part turns out not to be the flipping at all. It's knowing how much evidence is enough before we accuse anyone.

Here's the roadmap for this video. First we'll go over the rules of the game, then look at what a fair coin should look like, then talk through the accusation decision itself. After that we'll examine the tradeoff between false accusations and missed cheaters, run a practice round with real flips, and finish with how scoring rewards good judgment rather than perfect calls.

In the game world, each blob flips its own coin, and each flip lands heads or tails with no other information attached. We don't know whether any coin is fair, and we only get to see the results that the blobs produce. Our job is to read those results and decide which blobs to accuse. A correct accusation earns points, a missed cheater costs points, and a false accusation costs more, which is exactly where the interesting decisions begin.

A fair coin lands heads half the time, so one flip in two comes up heads over the long run. A cheating coin is weighted so that heads comes up more often, perhaps three times in five. Those two possibilities are the whole story, and the entire game is about telling them apart from a small amount of data. The difficulty is that a fair coin can still produce a run of heads by chance, so every decision we make carries some risk.

Before we start judging the blobs, I want you to pause and answer a quick question. How many heads in a row would you need to see before you accused a blob? Two heads, five heads, or ten? Take a moment to lock in a number, and then keep watching. Most players begin with a rule of thumb, and in a moment we'll test whether that rule holds up under the numbers.

Here's the first misconception to clear up, since one head proves nothing and two heads prove very little. If a fair blob flips twice, there's a one in four chance that both flips land heads, which is common enough to happen all the time across a large group. Accusing a blob after one or two heads means we will wrongly accuse many fair blobs. The evidence has to grow before it might mean anything, and that's a point worth remembering throughout the game.

The evidence grows in a predictable way, so we gave each blob 20 flips and then looked at the total number of heads. A fair blob probably lands near 10 heads, with some natural spread around that number. A cheating blob usually lands higher, closer to 12 heads, but the overlap between the two groups is large. A single run of 20 flips is never enough to be certain, and that overlap is exactly where the tradeoff lives.

Now comes the decision itself, and suppose we set the bar very low, so that any blob with more heads than tails gets accused. We'll catch almost every cheater, but we'll also accuse a large share of the fair blobs, and those false accusations pile up quickly. If we set the bar very high, we'll almost never accuse a fair blob, yet many cheaters will slip through. Every threshold trades one kind of mistake for the other, and there is no free lunch in this game.

I find that tradeoff the most interesting part of the game, because it has no perfect answer. The right threshold depends on how many blobs we're checking and how much each kind of mistake costs. If false accusations are expensive, we raise the bar, and if missed cheaters are expensive, we lower it. The point is that every choice is a decision under uncertainty, and the game asks us to make that choice explicitly rather than by instinct.

A practice round makes the tradeoff concrete. We set the threshold at 12 heads out of 20 flips and look at a group of 100 blobs. Roughly 1 in 4 fair blobs gets accused by chance, which is a painful number, and some cheaters still escape. How do we pick the right bar? Now we raise the threshold to 14 heads, and the false accusations drop sharply, while the cheaters who slip through become more common. Neither setting is wrong in every case, and that's the point of the exercise.

What does this teach us? We can see the game as a training ground for reasoning from limited data. Every accusation is a small bet about the coin, and every bet can be wrong. The best players don't expect to be right every time. They expect to be right more often than they are wrong, and they accept that some of their calls will look unlucky in hindsight. Why would anyone accept that?

Here's the caveat I want to stress most. Randomness never gives certainty, so no rule can catch every cheater without also accusing some fair blobs. A good strategy improves our odds, but it doesn't remove the uncertainty. If we ever find ourselves wanting a rule that is always right, that's a sign we need more flips, not a better guess. I find the honest answer is always a probability, not a verdict.

Scoring is built around that idea, since the game rewards careful judgment across many blobs, not a lucky streak on one. A player who accuses cautiously and gets most calls right will beat a player who accuses boldly and gets lucky once. Over many rounds, the averages do the work, and the scores reflect how well a threshold fits the mix of blobs we actually faced.

So here's the invitation I want to offer. Try the game for yourself, and test your own accusation rule against the blobs. Set your bar, watch the flips, and see how often your rule holds up over several rounds. You may find that your first instinct was too aggressive, or too cautious, and that a little patience with the numbers changes the outcome. That's the lesson the game is built to teach, and it's a lesson that carries well beyond coins.
