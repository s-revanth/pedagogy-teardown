## Narration

<<<NARRATION
A runaway trolley is heading toward five people. You can pull a lever. That sends it onto a side track, where it hits one person instead. Do you pull it? Most people say yes. I did too, the first time I heard it. But the question is not really about trains. It is about what we think counts as doing harm. Why does that matter so much? Stay with me, because it matters more than you think.

This is the trolley problem, and it has been around for more than half a century. The philosopher Philippa Foot described a version of it in 1967. Her target was not trolleys at all. She wanted to explain why causing harm and allowing harm can feel so different. Why do they feel different? That gap is the heart of the puzzle. I find it striking that the puzzle escaped the classroom long ago, and never really went back.

So let's leave the thought experiment and look at real life. Trolley-like choices show up in hospitals, in courtrooms, and in the design of machines. Did you expect that? I did not. We are no longer only imagining these choices. We are writing them into software, and that changes how we have to think about them. What happens when the lever is code? I have been asking that question for a while, and I still do not have a neat answer.

Here is the twist that philosophers love. Change the setup slightly, and most people's answer flips. Picture yourself standing on a footbridge above the tracks. The only way to stop the trolley is to push a large man off the bridge and into its path. Would you do it? Very few people say yes. Why? The outcome is the same. Five lives saved, one lost. I find that gap fascinating, and I cannot fully explain it, which is exactly why I keep coming back to it.

Why the difference? I started asking that question years ago, and the answer surprised me. Pulling a lever feels like changing a route. Pushing a person feels like using a body as a tool. Psychologists have a name for patterns like this, and I think the name matters less than the instinct behind it. Are we wired to respond to what we physically do, rather than what the numbers say? It looks that way, and I think that should make us humble.

Some philosophers have a tidy explanation for this. It is called the doctrine of double effect. The idea is that harm you intend is worse than harm you merely foresee. On the lever, the death of the one person is a side effect. On the footbridge, that death is the means of saving the others. You may find that distinction convincing, or you may find it strange. I find it useful, and I also find it slippery. Who decides what counts as intended?

In 2001, the psychologist Joshua Greene used brain scans to study how people react to these dilemmas. The footbridge version lit up emotional regions. The switch version drew more on areas linked to calculation. What does that tell us? Your brain, it seems, treats personal harm differently from impersonal harm. Does that make you irrational? Not necessarily. It may mean your emotions are doing their job. I wonder what that says about us, and whether we should trust the feeling or the math.

You might be wondering whether this matters outside a lab. I think it does. Think about the way you judge a friend who gets into trouble. Did they intend harm, or did harm happen along the way? Your answer depends on that difference more than on the total damage. That is the same instinct at work. It is quiet, fast, and surprisingly consistent across people.

Later work pushed this further. Some studies looked at people with damage to specific areas of the brain, where emotional responses are harder to produce. In 2007, researchers reported that these patients gave more purely numerical answers in footbridge-style cases. Think about what that means. The emotional alarm is not decoration. It is part of how we reach an answer at all. I find that both reassuring and a little unsettling, and I suspect you do too.

The footbridge version came from the philosopher Judith Jarvis Thomson. She added it to test the lever answer. If you think the lever is fine, the bridge should be fine too, right? Most people do not agree. Why not? That disagreement tells us something important about the rules we actually follow. Are those rules about counting outcomes? Clearly not, or the answers would match. I think we are following something older than arithmetic.

Picture a surgeon with five patients who each need a different organ. A healthy visitor walks in, and the surgeon could use that person's organs to save all five. Would you accept that? Almost nobody does. Why is that surgeon different from the lever? The numbers look the same. Yet the act feels different, and that feeling keeps showing up in every version of this puzzle. I know I feel it, and I think you do too.

Now let's go to the road. Self-driving cars face versions of this problem. Imagine a car that cannot brake in time. It can swerve into a wall, which risks the driver, or stay its course and hit pedestrians. Who should it protect? I have wondered about this for a long time, and I still do not have a clean answer. Do you? Would you buy a car that chose to sacrifice you? I would think twice, and many of us probably would too.

If you own a car, this is more than a thought experiment. Engineers try hard not to program a rule that picks a victim in advance. Instead, they aim to avoid the crash entirely. Still, somewhere in the edge cases, a choice remains. Would you want an engineer to decide that for you? Would you want to decide it yourself, every time you drive? Perhaps neither option feels right, and I would not want that job.

In 2018, researchers at MIT ran a huge online survey called the Moral Machine. People around the world were asked which outcome a car should choose across many crash scenes. The project collected more than 40 million decisions from people in more than 200 countries. The answers were not identical everywhere. Some groups leaned toward protecting the young, while others weighted age and status differently. Why does that matter? It means there is no single global answer. I think that should worry us, and it should also make us curious.

The same dilemma shows up in hospitals. During a crisis, a doctor may have one ventilator and two patients who need it. Triage rules are written in advance, and they are meant to be fair. Still, someone has to decide who gets help first. Have you ever thought about how a hospital makes that call? I did not, until I read about it. That decision can feel just like pulling the lever, and it can feel just as heavy. We rarely see that choice from the inside.

Many triage guides rely on medical need, the chance of survival, and sometimes how long a patient has already waited. None of them say one life is worth more than another. Yet the people making the call still carry the weight. Who decides the rules in the first place? You may never be in that room, but you may be asked to support rules like these. I think that is worth remembering.

Some real cases are even harder. In 1884, the small sailing ship Mignonette sank in the Atlantic. Four men survived in an open boat. After weeks at sea, two of them killed a cabin boy and ate him. Both men were later convicted of murder, and the case is still taught in law schools. Could necessity ever excuse that? The court said no. If you were in that boat, what would you say? I find that case chilling, and I think it still matters, because it asks what people do when the rules run out.

I want to be clear about the law here. The court did not accept necessity as an excuse for killing an innocent person. That ruling still shapes how we talk about emergencies. Could a rule like that ever be wrong? It is worth asking, and I think the question is fair. Most of us, if we are honest, would hope never to test it.

Philosophers often sort these answers into two camps. One camp says the right choice is whichever saves the most lives. That view is called utilitarianism. The other camp says some acts are wrong even when the math favors them. That view is often called deontology. You can feel both camps pulling on you right now. I know I do. Which camp are you in? Be honest, because most of us are in both, at different times.

We do this all the time, even without noticing. We decide which email to answer first. We choose which friend to call when we cannot call everyone. Each choice is a small trolley problem, and each one has a cost on the other track. Have you ever picked one friend over another? I have, and it still bothers me. We rarely name it that way, but we make these calls every day, and they add up.

If you have ever been asked to choose who goes first in line, you already know the feeling. The choice itself is small, but the weight of it is not. Your mind starts ranking people, and then it feels guilty for ranking them. That loop is real, and researchers who study decision making see it again and again. Why does it keep showing up? I think we should talk about it more, not less.

I should be careful here. These dilemmas are simplified on purpose. Real situations have uncertainty, missing information, and messy relationships. Is the lever ever really a lever? Are the people on the tracks ever just numbers? Maybe that is the real lesson. The puzzle is useful because it strips everything else away, but life rarely does that for us. I think that is why the puzzle keeps coming back, and why you should be suspicious of any answer that feels too neat.

Researchers now run these dilemmas in labs and online, and the results keep surprising us. People often want machines to follow rules, but they also want machines to protect them personally. Why do we want both? That tension is the core of the problem. We want fairness in theory, and self-protection in practice. If you are the one in the car, you want the car to protect you. I find that honest, if not comfortable, and I think we should admit it more often.

Here is a small test you can run on yourself. Picture that you are the driver, and you must choose between two outcomes that both hurt someone. Notice where your body reacts first. Notice whether you feel a pull toward action or toward doing nothing. You might be surprised by how much your answer depends on that feeling, not on the numbers. Why does the feeling win so often? I think the answer is simple, and it is not flattering to the math.

So try this at home. Pick a moral question from your own life. Picture two options, both bad. Ask yourself which one you could live with. Then ask why. Is the answer about the numbers, or about the act itself? I think the honest answer is usually both. Notice which one your gut picks first, because that choice tells you more than the argument does. Why would that be? Your gut has been practicing for a long time, and you should listen to it.

I used to think the trolley problem had a right answer. I no longer think that. Each time I return to it, I notice different things, and I learn something about my own reasoning. You may find the same thing. That is not a failure of the puzzle. That is the puzzle doing its job.

There is one more thing I want to be honest about. When I first thought about these dilemmas, I wanted a clean rule. Count the lives. Pick the bigger number. I no longer trust that feeling completely. Maybe that is the real growth a puzzle like this can give you: it teaches you how much your own intuitions are doing. Do you trust yours? I am still working out mine.

Here is where I land, for now. The trolley problem is not a riddle with a right answer. It is a mirror. It shows us how we weigh doing and allowing, and how much we care about being the one who acted. As machines and systems make more of these calls, the mirror matters more. Who do we want holding the lever? Who should be accountable when it moves? I believe we should look into the mirror before someone else decides what we see.
