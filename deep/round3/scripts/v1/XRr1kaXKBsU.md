<<<NARRATION
Look at the astronauts on the International Space Station. They float. They drift through the air, turn over, and push off the walls. So the question seems to answer itself. There is no gravity up there. That is the picture most of us carry around, and it is wrong in a way that is worth taking apart.

Before we go on, commit to an answer. The station is about four hundred kilometres above the ground. How strong is gravity at that height, compared with the surface? Is it almost zero, or is it most of what you feel every day? Write down a number, or at least a rough fraction. I will come back to it.

The first problem is the word weightless. It means the astronauts feel no push from a floor or a seat. It does not mean that gravity has switched off. The two ideas get mixed up all the time, and that mix-up is the source of most of the confusion.

So let's ask a few people on the street what they think is going on. One person said the Earth's pull stops once you are above the air. Another said gravity gets weaker with height but never quite goes to zero, and that felt like the answer that made sense to them. A third said the station is so far out that the Earth just cannot reach it. Those are the three most common explanations I hear, and each one points to a different idea about what gravity is.

Here is the plan. We will look at what it means to fall, then what the falling does to a moving object, then the picture Einstein gave us, and finally the caveats on the pictures we use. Each step builds on the one before.

Let's start with falling. Imagine dropping two objects of different weights from the same height. Your intuition may say the heavier one hits first. In air, that is roughly true, because the lighter one is slowed more by the air pushing on it. But without air, they fall together. That is the old result that Galileo worked out with careful reasoning about falling bodies, and it is a clean way to see what gravity does and does not depend on.

We can see it directly. In 1971, during the Apollo 15 mission, an astronaut on the Moon dropped a hammer and a feather from the same height. The Moon has almost no air, so nothing slows the feather. They hit the ground together. It is a simple demonstration, and it is the reason falling objects that are shaped differently still behave the same way, once air is taken out of the picture.

Now the equivalence principle, which I think is the single most useful idea here. Imagine a closed box, a lift with no windows, floating in empty space far from any star. Inside, everything is weightless. Now imagine the same box sitting on the surface of the Earth. Inside, you feel your weight pressing on the floor. The question is whether you could tell the difference from inside the box.

Think about what happens if the box is in free fall. Let it drop, with nothing holding it up. Everything inside falls with it, at the same rate. You float, because you and the floor are falling together. So a freely falling box feels exactly like a box with no gravity. That is the point. The station is in exactly that situation. It is not outside gravity. It is falling around the Earth, all the time, and the astronauts are falling with it.

Let's do the numbers, because it matters. At four hundred kilometres, the pull of gravity is about ninety percent of what it is on the surface. Most of it is still there. What makes the station float is not a lack of gravity. It is that the station is moving sideways so fast that, as it falls toward the Earth, the ground curves away beneath it at the same rate. It keeps falling and missing. That is an orbit. An orbit is a fall that never reaches the ground.

Here is a version of the same idea that makes it concrete. Imagine throwing a ball horizontally off a cliff. It falls and lands some distance away. Throw it faster, and it lands farther. Throw it fast enough, and the ground curves away faster than the ball falls. The ball goes around the Earth. The station is doing that, continuously.

Now let's go to Einstein. Newton gave us a rule that says every mass pulls on every other mass, and the pull weakens with the square of the distance. That rule is extremely accurate for almost everything we do. But Einstein's general theory of relativity gives a different picture. It says that mass and energy bend the geometry of space and time, and that objects moving freely follow the straightest paths available in that bent geometry. What we feel as gravity is the result of that bending.

The picture most people have seen for this is a rubber sheet. Put a heavy ball on a stretched sheet, and the sheet dips. Roll a smaller ball nearby, and it curves toward the heavy one. The sheet is a useful way to see that a dip can pull things in. And I want to be honest that it is a picture, not the thing itself.

The picture breaks down in a few places. The sheet is a surface with an up and down, and the ball rolls down because gravity pulls it down the slope. But in real space, there is no up. Gravity is not a force pulling things through a sheet. It is the shape of space and time, and objects follow that shape. If you take the rubber sheet too literally, you start imagining that gravity is something sitting in the middle of the sheet, pushing things, and that is the wrong image. The sheet is a good starting point, and it is also a trap.

So let's pause on that. Here is a picture that is genuinely useful, and that also hides something important about what it is describing. Good analogies do that. They give you a handle, and then you have to put the handle down.

Now the generalisation. The same geometry explains tides. The Moon pulls harder on the side of the Earth nearest to it than on the far side, and the difference stretches the oceans into two bulges. The Earth also spins through those bulges, which is why most coasts see two high tides a day. The orbits of planets, the bending of starlight near the Sun, and the timing of satellite clocks all follow the same logic. The falling and the curving are one story, told in different places.

Let's look at one more everyday confusion, because it comes up a lot. People sometimes say that the Moon falls around us, or that the Moon does not fall because it is far away. The Moon is falling around the Earth, in exactly the sense the station is. It is moving sideways so fast that it keeps missing. There is no separate thing that holds it up. Everything in orbit is in free fall, and that is why orbit is a state of motion, not a lack of gravity.

So let's go back to the hook. The astronauts float because they are falling together with their ship, not because gravity has left. At four hundred kilometres, the pull is still about nine-tenths of what you feel at home. The floating is what it feels like to fall freely, and the whole station is doing exactly that.

Next time, we take another everyday assumption and check whether the picture we carry around holds up.
