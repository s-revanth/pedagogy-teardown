<<<NARRATION
Here is a claim that sounds wrong. The most precise thermometer on Earth is not a machine. It is a column of old snow, drilled out of a sheet of ice, and it holds a record of temperature going back hundreds of thousands of years.

Before we go on, commit to an answer. How could snow record the temperature of a place, from long before anyone was measuring? Say what you think the mechanism might be. I will come back to it, and I want to see whether your guess matches what the ice does.

Most of us think of ice as passive. It is frozen water. It sits there, it melts, and that is the end of it. That picture is what I want to correct. Ice is not just a block. It is a layered archive, and each layer is a little document, written by the conditions at the time the snow fell.

So here is the plan. We will look at how snow turns into ice, then how scientists drill out a core, then how they read temperature and atmosphere from it, and finally what the caveats are. Each step is simple on its own. The result is a long record that is hard to get anywhere else.

Let's start with the snow. Every winter, snow falls on a polar ice sheet, and it does not all melt in the summer. The new snow sits on top of the old, and over time the weight of the layers above squeezes the snow below. The grains get packed tighter. Air between them is pushed out, and the snow slowly turns into firn, a dense, granular ice, and then into solid glacier ice. In a place where it snows every year and never fully melts, each year leaves a layer behind.

I want to show you that compaction step with a simple model. I have a jar of loose snow, and I am pressing it down with a weight. The snow starts out fluffy, with lots of air. As the weight goes on, the grains fuse at their contacts, and the block gets denser. The air does not disappear. It gets trapped in small bubbles, sealed off from the outside. That is the detail that matters later.

Now the drilling. Scientists go to places like the middle of Antarctica, where the ice is thick and the layers are undisturbed, and they drill straight down with a hollow tube. When they pull the tube up, they bring a long cylinder of ice with them. That cylinder is the core. The deeper the drill goes, the older the ice. Some of the deepest cores in Antarctica reach back roughly four hundred thousand years, and others go further. Each segment of core is a slice of history, and the segments are cut and labelled, layer by layer, the way a geologist would sample rock.

So how does ice tell you about temperature? This is the part I find most surprising. Water is made of hydrogen and oxygen, and both come in slightly different versions, called isotopes. The heavier versions are a little more likely to fall out of a cloud when it is cold. So snow that falls in a colder climate has a different ratio of heavy to light water than snow that falls in a warmer one. Measure that ratio down the core, and you have a temperature record. It is an indirect measurement, but it is consistent, and it runs back much further than any thermometer.

Now the bubbles. Remember those small sealed pockets of air from the compaction step. Each one is a tiny sample of the atmosphere at the time the snow was falling. Scientists can crack open the bubbles, or melt the ice and measure the gas, and find out how much carbon dioxide and methane the air contained. So a single core gives you two records at once. One is the temperature, from the isotopes. The other is the composition of the air, from the bubbles.

Put those together, and something striking appears. Over the long cycles that the core covers, the temperature and the carbon dioxide rise and fall together. In the colder periods, the air has less carbon dioxide. In the warmer periods, it has more. That does not prove which one causes the other, and I want to be careful about that. But the two records follow each other closely, through many cycles, and that is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that the climate system is tightly linked.

Now the caveats, because a record like this is only as good as the methods behind it. The isotope thermometer is indirect. It depends on assumptions about how the water got into the snow, and those assumptions can shift. The dating is also uncertain. Counting annual layers works near the top of the core, where they are visible. Deeper down, the layers thin and blur, and the age of each depth has to be estimated with models that carry their own error. And gas does not always stay put. In the upper part of the ice, air can migrate between the bubbles over time, which smears the record a little. So the core gives you a long and useful picture, but with error bars that matter.

These caveats are not a reason to throw the record out. They are a reason to read it carefully. Scientists have checked the isotope record against other archives, such as ocean sediments and cave deposits, and the broad pattern holds across them. The agreement across independent methods is part of what makes the record trustworthy, even though no single method is perfect.

That brings us to what this kind of archive is for. Ice cores are one of several natural archives. Tree rings record growing conditions year by year. Layers in lake and ocean sediment record what settled out over time. Cave deposits record water and temperature in another form. Each one has its own limits. Together, they let us check each other, and that is how a long record becomes something we can rely on.

So let's come back to the start. The most precise thermometer is a column of old snow. It works because snow falls in layers, because the layers trap air, and because the water in the snow carries the signature of the temperature when it fell. None of that is magic. It is slow accumulation, read with careful measurement.
