<<<NARRATION
Imagine you're told to climb out of a hole, walk across a field of mud toward a line of machine guns, and carry a shovel, a rifle and a whistle while you do it. Then, the next week, you're told to do it again. Congratulations, you have been drafted into the trenches of the First World War. This video is about what happens when a war stops moving and everybody keeps trying anyway.

Part one gave us the plans, the alliances and the first mad rush. This part is about what those plans did to the people carrying them. Here's the one-line chain. Trenches, attrition, new technology, new allies, and one final push that finally ended the thing.

By the end of 1914, the war on the Western Front had settled into a line of trenches stretching from the Channel coast down to the Swiss border. Both sides dug in. Each side had barbed wire, machine guns and artillery, which meant that any attack across open ground was a terrible bet. Picture a skit of daily life. The whistle blows. Men climb up, run forward a short way, and go down under fire. The next morning, the other side does exactly the same thing. Nobody gets far, and everyone counts the dead.

Life in the trenches was mud, cold, rats, lice and a lot of waiting. Most of the job was digging, carrying, and standing still in the wet while the artillery went over. Men were tired in a way that was hard to explain to anyone who had not been there. The front was not a dramatic line of heroic charges. For long stretches it was a very long, very boring, very dangerous queue.

In 1916 the Germans chose a place to attack that they expected the French to defend at any cost. It was Verdun, an old fortress town on the Meuse River, and the German plan was not to take the city outright. The idea was to make the French keep feeding men into the fight, so that the French army would bleed itself white defending a place it could not afford to lose. It's a cold logic. You don't need to win the fight, you need the other side to keep losing it.

The French held. They sent men in, and then sent more, and rotated units through the most dangerous ground. The fighting went on for months, through the spring, the summer and into the winter. The phrase they used to rally their men, roughly that they shall not pass, became a sort of motto, and it is one of the best-known lines from the war. But a motto does not stop artillery, and both sides took enormous losses.

Then came the Somme. In July 1916 the British and French launched a major offensive, and the first day is the one people remember. Men were ordered to climb out of the trenches, walk across open ground at a set pace, and head toward German lines that had survived the preliminary bombardment far better than expected. In a skit, you would show this as a line of soldiers walking slowly toward a wall. In reality, the machine guns were waiting, and on that first day the British alone suffered tens of thousands of casualties. It was among the bloodiest single days in the history of the British army.

The Somme also brought in a new gadget. Tanks made their first appearance there, lumbering and unreliable, and they were a sign of the arms race that the war had become. Every new weapon was meant to break the stalemate, and each one helped a little and then got countered. Gas had already been used earlier in the war, and now the same logic applied to tanks, to better artillery, and to tactics that changed each season. The loop is always the same. Attack, gain a little, get stuck, lose men, try a new thing, repeat.

I want to keep the timeline straight, because it changes the picture. In 1917 two big things happened. In Russia, the revolution brought the government down, and the new regime eventually moved toward peace. And in the United States, public opinion turned after Germany's escalation of submarine warfare and the Zimmermann telegram, a diplomatic message proposing a German-Mexican alliance. The United States declared war in April. The new American forces would take time to arrive, but they were a massive new source of men and money.

By early 1918, Russia was out of the war, and that freed up German troops for one last major push in the west. The German spring offensive made real gains at first, faster than the Allies expected. But the advance outran its supplies, the attackers were exhausted, and the lines stretched. Then the Allies counterattacked, with fresh American troops, tanks and better coordination. The offensive stalled, and the momentum went the other way.

Now a caveat. The casualty figures in this war are estimates, and they vary by source and by how you count the missing, the wounded and the dead. The Somme's first-day numbers are the most quoted, and they are shocking, but they are still best treated as close approximations. Use the overall scale, not the exact count, and be wary of anyone who gives you a single neat total.

The armistice came into force on 11 November 1918, at 11 in the morning. Think about that. For more than four years, men had been dying over small changes in the lines, and the war ended on a schedule that put the guns down at a specific time on a specific day. The men who had been in the trenches did not have a parade. Many of them simply stopped, and then tried to remember what the quiet sounded like.

So back to the scenario from the start. You climb out, you walk across the mud, you carry the shovel and the rifle and the whistle, and you do it again next week. That is what millions of men were asked to do, over and over, for four years. The lesson of the trenches is not that the generals were fools. It's that a war can become a machine that keeps running long after everyone can see it is not working.

Next time, we'll look at the peace that followed, and the way the settlement reshaped Europe, and why so many people were unhappy with it. It's a story with a lot of maps and a lot of arguments. See you then.
