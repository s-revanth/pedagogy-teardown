<<<NARRATION
Why does anything stick around? Look at a rock, a river, a song, or a living animal. Each one is a pattern that has held together for some length of time, and each one is, in a sense, lucky. The question here is not why anything exists at all. It's narrower: why do some patterns persist, and others vanish?

I'll answer it with a world. The world is a dish, and the dish holds shapes. A shape is any pattern you can describe and tell apart from another. Each shape is fragile. On every tick of the clock, each one has a small chance of falling apart, and when it does, it's gone for good. That's all the rules there are, for now.

Let's start with the baseline, because it's the most useful picture to have. Put a few shapes in the dish, and let them decay. Nothing copies. Every shape has the same small chance of falling apart each tick. Run it. The dish empties. Within a few hundred ticks there is nothing left. That's what you'd expect when nothing replaces what's lost. Persistence needs something that offsets the decay.

Here's the simplest offset: copying. Now let each shape, on each tick, make a copy of itself with some chance. The copy appears right beside the parent. The shapes still fall apart, but they are being replaced. Run it, and the dish stays populated.

Before you read on, make a prediction. Say the copy rate is ten percent faster than the decay rate. Does the shape survive? Now take the copy rate ten percent slower. Does it survive then? Pause and decide, for both cases.

Here's the run. When copying is ten percent faster than decay, the shapes persist. The dish fills, and it stays filled. When copying is ten percent slower, the shapes die out, slowly but reliably. The difference looks tiny on paper, and over time it is enormous. Persistence is a race, and the shape that copies faster than it decays wins the race.

That's a first answer to the question. Something exists, in this world, when it copies itself faster than it falls apart. It doesn't need to be strong, or well built, or special in any way. It only needs its copies to outpace its losses.

There's a tempting misreading here. People often assume that the things that last are the things that are sturdiest. But a sturdy shape that never copies disappears. A fragile shape that copies quickly fills the dish. Sturdiness matters only through its effect on copying, and copying is what the dish rewards.

Now the next step. Copies are never exact in the real world, so let's add variation. When a shape copies, the copy has a small chance of coming out different. Most changes make it slightly worse. Some make it slightly better. Run it. At first the dish looks the same. Then variants start to appear around the original. One detail to notice: nothing in the dish aims for a better copy. The faster copiers simply leave more copies behind.

Here's what matters. A variant that copies faster than the original will spread through the dish, even if it starts out rare. Run thirty copies of the same starting dish and look at each one. In most of the runs, a faster variant appears, and within a few hundred ticks it takes over. That isn't one lucky run. The pattern repeats, because the mechanism is simple: faster copying wins.

It's worth saying what doesn't happen. The faster variant doesn't wipe out the original in one step. It gains slowly at first and then quickly, because each copy it leaves is a little more likely to leave more copies. That slow start is why one run can look inconclusive, and why thirty runs are more convincing than one.

So there are three ingredients, and I want to name them after watching them work. Copying offsets decay. Variation creates new versions. And limited room means the versions compete for space. Remove any one of the three, and the story changes. No copying, and everything dies. No variation, and nothing improves. No limit on room, and every version fills the dish at once, so the race has no winner.

Here's the bookkeeping behind the race. Call the copy rate c and the decay rate d. Each tick, the population changes by roughly c minus d, as a fraction of its size. If c is bigger than d, the change is positive, and the shape grows. If c is smaller, it shrinks. The sign of c minus d is the whole story in this world, and that is why a ten percent difference was enough to decide the outcome.

I should be honest about what this model is. My shapes are a stand-in. They're not molecules, genes, or organisms. I picked the decay rate, the copy rate, and the size of the dish. Those choices shape the result. What I'm claiming is the logic: patterns that copy themselves, with variation, and compete for room, end up with the versions that copy best.

That logic shows up well beyond biology. A song that many people sing, a word that many speakers adopt, a company structure that others copy. Each one persists because it is copied, and each one changes a little when it is copied. The same three ingredients explain why some of them last and others fade.

So here's the recap. A pattern persists when it copies itself faster than it falls apart. Copies vary, and the best copiers take the room. That combination produces patterns that outlast their own decay, and the survivors are not chosen for being sturdy. They're chosen for copying.

If you remember one thing from this video, make it this. The useful question is never how sturdy a shape is. It's how well its copies keep up with its losses, and whether there is room for those copies to spread.

Next time, we'll give the copies traits, and the traits will decide who survives. Before then, try changing the copy rate in the dish. Find the point where the shapes stop persisting, and check whether your number matches the one we ran.
