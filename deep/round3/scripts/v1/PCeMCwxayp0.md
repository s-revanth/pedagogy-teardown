<<<NARRATION
Picture an old shed behind a building that nobody has used for years. The door is stiff, and the dust is thick. Inside, on a shelf, sits a metal box with a warning symbol stamped on the side. Somebody decides to open it. What happens next, and how far does it reach?

The headline version of this story says the whole world is at risk. That is a big claim, and big claims deserve a small test. So instead of asking whether something could end the world, ask a smaller question first. What is the harm, and how far does the harm travel?

Here is the model. It has four rules, three about the hazard and one about spread.

Rule one: the harm depends on how strong the source is. A weak source does little damage even up close, and a strong one can injure people in seconds.

Rule two: the harm depends on distance. Many hazards, including radiation, weaken fast as you move away from them. Being a metre from a source is very different from being a few centimetres away.

Rule three: the harm depends on time. A short exposure is far less dangerous than a long one, even from a strong source. Time spent close to the source adds up.

Rule four is about how far the hazard can spread. A sealed object stays where it is. A substance that gets onto skin, clothing, or objects, or into the air or water, can travel, and that is the difference between a local disaster and something much larger.

Now the real case. In 1987, in the city of Goiania in Brazil, a clinic had moved out of an old building and left behind a radiotherapy device. Inside the device was a sealed capsule of a radioactive material called caesium-137. The device was not removed. Eventually it was found by scavengers, who took it apart to sell the metal. The capsule was broken open, and the material inside was a powder that glowed a faint blue. People were fascinated by it. They took it home, shared it with friends and family, and handled it with their bare hands. Four people died, and hundreds more were contaminated. The city had to find, measure, and clean up many locations.

Walk through the rules. The source was strong, so rule one applies: the powder was dangerous at close range. Rule two: the worst damage was for the people who held it or carried it close. Rule three: people kept it around for days or weeks, so the time added up. And rule four: the powder was a loose substance, which meant it could be carried from one place to the next on skin, clothing, and objects. The harm was serious, and it was real, and it spread along the paths people took. It did not reach beyond the city the way the headline would suggest.

Now a toy version of the first three rules, to see how the numbers move. Take one sealed source. Stand one metre away from it for an hour, then stand ten metres away for an hour, then leave the room entirely. The dose falls steeply with distance, and falls to nearly nothing once you are out of the room. The same source is a serious hazard at one metre and a minor one at ten. The shed is not dangerous to the whole world. It is dangerous to the people who stand in it.

So here is the payoff. A forgotten shed can do real harm. But the harm usually stays inside a small radius, and it depends on what is inside, how long people stay near it, and whether it can move. A hazard that stays put is a local emergency. A hazard that spreads on its own is a different category entirely.

I need to add a caveat here, because a story like this can make people think they should go and open every old box they find. Please do not do that. Old containers with warning signs should be left alone and reported to the authorities, who have the equipment and training to deal with them. The point of the rules is to understand scale, not to encourage anyone to handle the material.

Now the generalisation. Which kinds of thing could ever be a world-scale problem? Not a sealed source in a shed. Something that spreads by itself would qualify, because it keeps moving from person to person or place to place without anyone carrying it. That is why people who study large risks spend much of their time on things that copy themselves or travel through air, water, or food, rather than on objects that sit still. The test is simple. Does the hazard travel on its own, and does it keep going once it leaves? If not, it is a local problem, however frightening it looks.

So which one would you guard? The box on the shelf, or the thing that could spread without anyone touching it? The rules say the box needs a locked door and a team with the right instruments. The spreading thing needs a much bigger system to keep it contained.

Back to the shed. The door is stiff, the dust is thick, and the metal box has a warning symbol on it. The headline says it could end the world. The rules say something different. It is dangerous to the people who open it, dangerous for a while, and dangerous within a radius that depends on the strength, the distance, and the time. The world is not at stake. The people around the box are.
