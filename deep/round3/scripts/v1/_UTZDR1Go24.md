## Narration

<<<NARRATION
This is a LEGO taco. Look at it for a second. Is it a real taco? Are you sure? A lot of images look like one thing and turn out to be something else, and the trick is working out what your eyes are really getting.

Here's the plan for this episode. We'll look at four images, and for each one ask what's actually there, what's been added by the camera, and what your brain filled in.

Start with the taco. Up close it's made of small plastic bricks. Each brick is a single coloured block, a bit like a pixel. A LEGO mosaic is a picture built on a grid of bricks, and if you step back, the blocks blur into a shape. That is the same thing a photo on your phone does: a grid of coloured squares, too small to see unless you get close. The bricks are just big pixels.

So let's count. The taco is roughly thirty bricks across. Once you know that, the picture stops being magic. It's a small grid, and the shape comes from the pattern of colours. Your brain's job is to read the pattern, and it's very good at that, which is why you saw a taco before you saw a grid.

The second image is a planet photographed by a spacecraft that, at first glance, looked like a painted ball, or a computer render. Here's a useful habit. Before you decide an image is real or fake, look for a scale cue. A tree, a person, a shadow, a known size. Once you find one, you can estimate how big things are. In this case the cue is the stripe pattern, and the stripes tell you how far away the camera was and how big the storms really are.

Now the misconception. A sharp, detailed image feels true. It feels like a window. But a window can be clean and still show a lie. Lenses have focus, cameras have exposure, and editing can add or remove things. Sharpness tells you about the lens, not about the scene. The question to ask is not "is it clear?" but "what made this picture, and from where?"

That same question works for every image on this list. The taco photo was taken from a few inches away, so the bricks look huge and nothing else in the frame gives scale. Your brain fills in the rest. The planet photo was taken from very far away, and its scale is hidden in plain sight. Both are tricks of distance, and both are solved by finding one familiar object.

Here's the reveal for the taco. The whole thing is about the size of your palm. It's a brick model, photographed close, against a plain background, with the lighting set so it looks like food. Nothing about it is a lie in the sense of being fake evidence. It's a model built to be photographed the way a meal would be. The lesson is that the same picture can be true and misleading at once.

And the planet has the same lesson. The colours had been stretched to show the detail in the clouds. The photo was accurate about the shapes and misleading about the colour. Both answers are right, depending on which question you ask.

So, back to the first question. Is it a real taco? The honest answer is no, not one you can eat. It's a real object, built from real bricks, photographed so well that your brain made a meal out of it. That's the trick of a good image. It doesn't just show you something. It lets you fill in the rest.

Next episode, we'll have more of these, including one that has been online for longer than you might expect. Keep looking closely.

Thanks for watching.
NARRATION>>>
