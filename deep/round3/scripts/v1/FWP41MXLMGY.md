<<<NARRATION
In the spring of 2020, London went quiet. The city's streets, which normally choke with buses, taxis, delivery vans, and commuters, were suddenly empty enough that you could ride a bike down the middle of a major road and hear your own wheels. The trains were almost empty too. I rode through the city in that strange stretch, and the first thing I noticed was not the quiet. It was the room. A city had quietly handed back its streets, and nobody had quite decided what to do with them. So what do you do with a city that suddenly has room?

Most people, looking at that, think the story is simple. The lockdown stopped the city. Fewer people moved, so fewer vehicles moved, and the streets were empty because the city was paused. When the pause ended, the city would return to what it had been. That belief is reasonable. It assumes traffic is a fixed amount of activity that the city produces, and that the empty road is only a temporary gap.

Here is a better model, with three rules.

Rule one: traffic is a set of choices, not a fixed quantity. Each person decides whether to drive, take the bus, walk, cycle, or stay home, based on what is cheapest and most pleasant for them.

Rule two: road space is finite. A street can hold only so many vehicles at once, and once it is full, every extra vehicle slows everyone down. That is why jams form.

Rule three: when road space frees up, something fills it. If driving becomes easier, some people switch to cars. If cycling becomes safer and more pleasant, some people switch to bikes. Empty space does not stay empty. It gets used by whatever is cheapest.

Now the context. Through that spring, the Tube lost most of its riders, and many of the people still travelling were the ones who could not work from home. Buses kept running, because many essential workers had no alternative. Shops were shut, schools were shut, and the daily rhythm of the city was broken open. Against that backdrop, the rules have something concrete to act on.

Now a toy street, so the rules can run. Picture one wide road near a big station, with room for four lanes. In normal times it has cars in every lane, buses slowing everything, and a line of cyclists squeezed against the kerb. Remove most of the cars, and suddenly there is room. What fills it depends on what has changed. If the space is simply left empty, the road stays empty. If someone paints a bike lane and makes it safe to ride, cyclists move in. If the buses get a clear lane, they run on time and carry more people. Same road, same space, but the rules decide who uses it.

Here is the payoff, and it is the reason the lockdown story is more interesting than the headline. In the months after the first emergency, the city's transport authority and councils began putting in temporary cycle lanes and reshaping some streets so that traffic was pushed to the main roads, while quieter streets were closed to through traffic. Some of these were called low traffic neighbourhoods. The point was not to close the city. The point was to move the empty space to where it did the most good, so that people who had no other way to travel could travel more safely.

Now let's ride it, because the best way to see the rules is from the seat. On a quiet morning, a junction that would normally be a shouting match of horns becomes a place where you can see every decision clearly. You can see the lights changing for no one. You can see a bus pull out on time. You can see a cyclist use the lane that was painted for them. And you can see, in a single short stretch, why the space got used the way it did. A road that had been taken over by cars was now a set of choices, and the choices were visibly different from before.

Let me step sideways for a moment, because there is a real question here about who uses the bike lanes. The people who depend on a bike are often the people least able to afford a car, and for them a safer street is not a hobby benefit. It is a daily change in whether the trip is possible at all. That is a point the simple model misses. The rules are about who gets to choose, not just about total traffic.

Now the rebound. Once restrictions eased, the roads began to fill again. Cars came back, and some of the new cyclists did not stay. That is rule three in action. The space was not permanent, and whatever was cheapest and most attractive took the first chance to come back. But the rebound was not a simple reset. Some of the changes stayed, and some habits did not return in the same proportion as before. The city did not return to exactly where it was, and it did not stay where it had been pushed.

I should be careful about what this proves. This is one city, during one extraordinary period, with many things changing at once. People were working from home, shops were closed, schools were shut, and public attitudes shifted in ways that are hard to separate. A lockdown is not a controlled experiment, and I cannot cleanly say how much of any change came from road design and how much came from the pandemic itself. The rules are a good way to organise what happened. They are not proof that any single policy would produce the same result everywhere.

Now the generalisation, and this is where the three rules start to earn their keep. The same logic applies to any city that changes the way its road space is allocated. A city that makes driving slower and cycling safer tends to see some car trips shift. A city that adds lanes for cars tends to see more cars arrive to fill them, which is a well-known effect in transport planning. The rules are not a promise that any particular policy works. They are a way to ask what will fill the space, and why. Every street that is reallocated answers that question in some way, whether anyone decides it or not.

So the question for you is simple. If you had a street for a single season, with no cars on it, what would you keep? Would you let it fill with whatever came first, or would you decide what should go there? The city had that choice in 2020, and the answers it found are still being argued over.

Back to the rules. Traffic is choices. Road space is finite. Empty space gets used by whatever is cheapest. The lockdown did not stop the city. It showed what the city does when its roads change. The empty streets were a test, not a pause, and the results are still worth reading.
