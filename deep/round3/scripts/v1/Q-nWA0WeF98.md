<<<NARRATION
How do you beat the greatest general in the world? The obvious answer is to find him and fight him. The Romans tried that, and it cost them a huge army at Cannae. So this time the answer is different. You stop chasing him, you cut off what feeds him, and then you fight on a field of your own choosing. This video is about how Rome learned that lesson, the hard way, and how long it took.

Here's the one-line chain. Stop chasing him, cut his money, cut his reinforcements, and fight where you can win. Every step of the Roman comeback is one of those four things.

After Cannae, Rome was in a bad state. A huge army had been destroyed. Many of its senior officers were dead. And Hannibal was sitting in the middle of Italy, winning towns and gathering allies. He even had a town, Capua, switch sides and join him. He spent the winter there in comfort, which is the kind of detail that makes a Roman senator sit up very straight.

The problem for Rome had two parts. Hannibal was in Italy, and nobody could make him leave. And Spain, across the sea, was where Carthage got much of its silver and many of its soldiers. As long as Spain kept sending money and men, Hannibal could keep his army fed and reinforced, even without any supply from home. So the question became simple. What feeds him, and can we take that away?

Think of it like a bucket with holes in it. If you keep pouring water into the top, you can't make it full. You have to plug the holes, and most of the holes are not where the water is splashing. Rome's holes were in Spain. The sensible plan was to take Spain, and Scipio, a young commander who had already learned a lot in the field, was given the job.

Here's the plan as a minimal definition. Take Spain. That's it. It doesn't win the war on its own. It stops Carthage from refilling its army, and that is enough to change everything else.

Spain itself is a map of rivers, silver mines and tribes who did not love either Rome or Carthage. Whoever controlled the mines and the coast controlled the war's budget. Scipio understood that. He did not try to fight the whole country at once. He went for the most important point, the city of New Carthage on the southern coast.

In 209 BC, Scipio took New Carthage in a fast assault. The story goes that the town's lagoon was drained at a convenient moment, which let the Romans wade in where the defenders had not expected them. It's a great story, and a little too neat for comfort. Keep it in mind for later, because the sources that tell it were written long after the event, and we'll come back to that.

Next came Ilipa, in 206 BC. Scipio faced a Carthaginian army that outnumbered him and sat in good positions. He tricked them with a feint, changed his formation during the approach, and the Carthaginian lines did not respond quickly enough. Picture it like a chess move that the other side sees too late. The Romans struck where the enemy had not finished rearranging itself, and the battle went their way.

With Spain falling apart for Carthage, Scipio went back to Rome with his victories. He was a hero, but he was also a young man with a lot of military success and a lot of enemies who did not like the idea of one commander becoming too powerful. Rome's politics were always watching the successful generals, and Scipio was about to be watched very closely.

Meanwhile, Hannibal's brother Hasdrubal was trying to do exactly what Hannibal had done. In 207 BC he marched an army over the Alps to join Hannibal in Italy. It was an attempt to reunite the Carthaginian forces and finish the war. But the Romans intercepted messages, and they knew where Hasdrubal was going. The Roman consuls moved to meet him before he could link up with his brother.

The battle took place at the Metaurus River. Hasdrubal's army was caught, and it was destroyed. Hasdrubal himself was killed. Now the story gets grim. Roman accounts say his head was cut off and thrown into Hannibal's camp, so that the brother who had come to help would be a message instead. It's a horrible detail, and it's one of those stories that historians treat carefully, because the writers who tell it had their own reasons for telling it that way.

And here is the real problem for Hannibal. He had lost the thing that made his strategy work, which was the hope of reinforcements. A commander who wins battles with the support of an army at home can still lose a war when the support never arrives. People often assume Hannibal was simply outmatched by a better general. That's not what happened. He was outlasted, cut off, and starved of new soldiers.

So Hannibal was stuck in the south of Italy, with no supply line and no new army coming. Rome did not need to chase him anymore. It only needed to keep him there, keep his allies nervous, and wait. This is the strategy that Fabius had tried earlier, and now it had something it had lacked before. A way to end the war.

This is a pattern you'll recognise from the last part. A general who cannot be reinforced is in the same position as a bear being shadowed through the forest. He can still hurt you, but he cannot easily get anywhere, and every day he stays in the same place is another day his support erodes.

With Hannibal boxed in, Scipio pushed for the final move. He wanted to take the war to Africa, to Carthage itself. The Senate was not thrilled, because it was a risky plan and some senators wanted to keep Scipio at home. But he was allowed to go, and in 204 BC he landed in Africa with an army. Carthage panicked. It recalled Hannibal from Italy, and he came home in 203 BC to face the Romans on their ground.

The battle was at Zama, in 202 BC. Hannibal had elephants, and the elephant charge was supposed to break the Roman line. Here is the skit. The elephants thunder toward the Roman ranks. The Romans, who had prepared for this, open lanes in their formation, the way you might open a gate in a fence. The elephants run through the lanes, and the handlers lose control of many of them. The Roman legions hold, the Carthaginian lines break, and Hannibal's army is defeated in a long, hard fight.

Now a caveat. The numbers for the armies at Zama, and for the casualties, are not precise. The story of the elephants is well attested in broad strokes, but the detail of exactly how each one was handled depends on which writer you read. Keep the outline and treat the decorations with care.

The peace that followed was harsh. Carthage gave up Spain, handed over most of its navy and most of its war elephants, agreed to pay a huge indemnity spread across decades, and could not start a war without Rome's consent. For Carthage, it was the end of its empire. For Rome, it was the start of a very long period as the dominant power in the western Mediterranean.

But there is a running gag in the Roman senate, and it is the last thing I want to plant for you. Cato the Elder, a senator famous for his stern views, had a habit of ending his speeches on almost any subject with the same demand. Carthage must be destroyed. It doesn't matter if he is talking about farming or taxes or the price of grain. The sentence comes at the end, every time. It's funny the first few times. By the tenth, the room is practically finishing it for him. That line is going to matter in the next war.

Hannibal did not die in Africa. He spent years in exile, first at the court of a Syrian king who was at war with Rome, and then at the court of a king in Bithynia. The Romans kept pressing for him to be handed over. The later accounts say that when it became clear the Romans were coming to take him, he drank poison that he had kept for exactly this kind of moment. You can see why the story appeals, and you can also see why historians hold it lightly.

So back to the question. How do you beat the greatest general in the world? You don't beat him by chasing him around Italy. You beat him by cutting off the silver and the soldiers that keep his army alive, and then waiting until he has nowhere left to go. The Romans learned that after a great deal of pain, and it became the template for every war they fought afterward.

And that Cato line? It's still waiting for its payoff. The next war is where it gets fulfilled, and it is not going to be a very cheerful ending for Carthage. See you there.
