## Narration

<<<NARRATION
If you stood on a mountain, how much of Earth could you see? Think about it, and keep your guess in mind, because most people guess too high. The reason is kind of beautiful.

Here's the answer in brief: much less than you'd think, and the horizon is much closer than it looks. But let's get there step by step.

Here's the plan. We'll measure the horizon from the ground, work out how it changes with height, turn that into a share of the planet, and then look at how far you can see from space.

Start with a ball and a person. Earth is a sphere about six thousand three hundred and seventy kilometres in radius. Picture yourself standing on a small ball, about the size of a basketball, with a tape measure. You're barely above the surface. The ground curves away from you, and at some distance it drops out of your view. That point is the horizon. Your eyes are a few centimetres above the sphere, and the horizon is only a few kilometres away.

Let's do it properly. Stand in a flat field with your eyes about one point seven metres above the ground. How far is the horizon? The answer is around four and a half kilometres. That's very close, not the hundred-kilometre view you might have pictured. Walk four and a half kilometres and the far ground has dropped behind the curve. Go up a hill and the horizon moves out.

Here's the rule that makes this work. The distance to the horizon grows with the square root of your height. Double your height and the horizon doesn't double. It grows by about forty percent. For a rough number, if you measure your height in metres, the horizon distance in kilometres is about three point five seven times the square root of that height. That's the whole trick. You don't need the full geometry to get a good answer.

Try a mountain. Stand at four thousand metres, on a high peak. The square root of that height is around sixty-three, so the horizon is roughly two hundred and twenty-six kilometres away. That's a big jump from four and a half kilometres. The mountain buys you a lot of horizon, but it's still a small part of the planet.

Now the bigger question: what share of the planet's surface can you see at once? Here the picture changes. The visible part of a sphere, from a given height, is a cap, a round patch cut off by the horizon. The cap's area grows with your height, but it's a share of the whole sphere. The formula for the share is simple in words. Take the planet's radius, divide it by the planet's radius plus your height, subtract that from one, and halve the result. That gives the fraction of the surface you can see.

Let's plug in numbers. At ten kilometres, the height of a commercial airliner, the share is about point zero eight percent. That's less than one tenth of one percent of the planet. Even from a plane, you're looking at a tiny patch.

Now the space station. At about four hundred kilometres, the share is roughly three percent. A few percent of Earth in one view is still a lot of land, cloud and ocean at once. Astronauts often describe it as a huge curved landscape, and it is, but it's still a small share of the total.

Here's a surprise. From geostationary orbit, about thirty-six thousand kilometres up, where some weather satellites sit, the share is about forty-two percent. That's a big jump. A satellite that stays over one spot can see a large part of the planet's disk, enough to watch whole storm systems form and drift.

And from the Moon? Roughly three hundred and eighty-four thousand kilometres away, the visible share is just under half. You see almost the whole hemisphere that faces you. You never see the full sphere at once, because you only see the side facing you, and the far side stays hidden. Half is the most you could ever see from far away.

So the answer changes a lot with height. On a mountain, you see a circle hundreds of kilometres across. In a plane, you see a patch smaller than a single city's worth of land. From orbit, a few percent of the planet. From the Moon, about half the surface facing you. From infinitely far away, you would see exactly half, and never the whole.

Here's a common misconception. People think that from space, you can always see the whole Earth. You can't. From the station, the planet fills much of your view, and it looks like a complete globe in photos, because cameras are wide and the disk is big. But the disk is only the visible part, and the far side is behind it. The curved edge shows you where the planet ends, not what is on the back.

That leads to an honest caveat. The horizon numbers assume a smooth sphere and light that travels in straight lines. Earth's atmosphere bends light a little, so the real horizon sits slightly farther away than the geometry says. Temperature and air density change that effect too. The formulas are good estimates, and they're enough for most purposes. But if you're measuring something precise, you have to account for the bending.

Now a tangent. Why do mountains look the same from far away? Because of the same geometry. The horizon depends on height in a way that flattens out. A tall mountain and a taller one can have horizons that differ by much less than their heights would suggest, so from a distance their profiles can look alike. You can't always tell how high a peak is just by looking at its outline from far away.

Let's generalise. The same horizon idea explains why radio and cell towers need to be tall. Their signal reaches the horizon, and the horizon grows with the square root of the tower's height. Doubling a tower doesn't double its range. Engineers use this to plan networks, and it's why coverage can feel patchy in a valley. The valley's walls form a horizon of their own.

Ships are another example. Historically, a sailing ship disappears hull first, then the sails, as it moves away over the curve of the sea. That's the horizon in action. The ship is still there, only its lower part is hidden. The top of the mast stays visible for longer than the hull, because the top is higher.

Here's the recap in a few lines. The horizon grows with the square root of your height. The share of the planet you can see grows quickly at first, then more slowly as you go up. In orbit it's a few percent, and at the Moon it's about half. The far side is always hidden. And the real horizon is always a little farther than the geometry says, because air bends light.

So back to the mountain. From the peak you see far, but not as far as you might think. Your horizon is a couple of hundred kilometres away, and the rest of the planet curves away behind it. The distance is real and generous, and it's still a small patch of a very big ball.

Next time, we'll look at what lies over that horizon, and what it would take to see the far side at all. Could anything ever see the whole globe at once?

Thanks for watching. Look for the horizon the next time you're up high.
