<<<NARRATION
For most of human history, the two halves of the world barely knew the other one existed. Then, in 1492, they met. What followed was one of the strangest lopsided exchanges in history. Diseases crossed the Atlantic in huge numbers, and very little, or at least very little anyone agrees on, crossed back. Why would germs only travel in one direction?

Let's start with the expected answer, because most people carry one. The belief goes like this. A germ is a germ. It has no nationality. Put two populations on one ocean, let the ships run back and forth, and disease should wander both ways, roughly evenly, with the bigger or better-armed side coming out ahead on whichever disease happened to hit harder. So the lopsided result must be bad luck, or the destruction of one side by the other, or just something about the ships.

That belief is not stupid, but it leaves out a rule. Diseases do not just happen to people. They need conditions. So here is a three-part model that does most of the work.

Rule one: a disease needs enough hosts to keep going. Once an infection has run through a population, the people who recovered are immune, and the disease has nobody left to infect. If new people do not show up fast enough, it burns out. Infections that need a lot of people to stay alive need a big, dense population to stay alive at all.

Rule two: a disease needs a steady supply of hosts. For some diseases that means a population that keeps growing, with new children every year. For others it means a reservoir, an animal species that keeps carrying the infection even when the humans are gone.

Rule three: a disease needs a route, a way from one host to the next. Ships are a route. Trade is a route. So is a river, a road, or a market full of animals.

Now a toy version, so you can see the rules work. Take a village of fifty people. Put one measles case in it. The first few people get sick. They recover and are immune. The rest of the village is still susceptible, but the people who could have passed it on are gone or protected. Within a few months the outbreak simply runs out of people. Now take a city of half a million. The same measles case has an enormous number of susceptible hosts, and the outbreak does not run out. The disease keeps circulating, year after year, because the city keeps producing new children who have never met it.

That is rule one, and it is not subtle. A disease that needs a large population cannot persist in a small one. Estimates for how large a population measles needs to stay alive run into the hundreds of thousands, though the exact figure depends on the assumptions you make.

So where did the big Old World diseases come from, the ones that did so much damage on the other side of the ocean? Mostly from animals. This is the step that makes the whole story work.

Smallpox, measles, influenza, and several others that spread among humans probably began in animals, and then made the jump. Measles is thought to have come from a disease of cattle. Influenza is an old bird virus that often passed through pigs on its way into people. Many of these jumps happened after humans began living in large numbers next to domesticated animals, sharing buildings, water, and air with them.

Here the continents differ. Eurasia had a large set of animals that could be domesticated, and people domesticated many of them. Cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, horses, chickens. Each one carried its own set of microbes, and every time people lived close to them, some of those microbes found a way into the people. Over thousands of years, Eurasian populations were exposed to a steadily growing pool of pathogens, and the survivors of each outbreak passed on some resistance.

The Americas had far fewer. The llama and alpaca in the Andes, the guinea pig, the turkey, and the dog were domesticated, and there were important crops and some dense settlements. But the large livestock that carry so many human diseases were missing. There were no cattle, no pigs, and no horses in the sense that mattered. So the Americas had a much smaller reservoir of animal diseases waiting to make the jump.

Run the rules again. Eurasia, with a big population and a big set of animals, produces a big set of diseases that can persist in human populations. The Americas, with fewer large animals and a smaller settled population, produces a smaller set. When the two sides meet, the side with the bigger disease pool hands over its diseases, and the other side does not have the same things to hand back.

Here is the same logic in another place. Africa had its own dense populations and long-standing trade routes with Eurasia, so its disease history ran through the same three conditions. Australia was largely cut off for tens of thousands of years. When the British arrived, they brought diseases like smallpox that the local population had no experience of, and the damage was severe. Again, the pattern is the same. The side with more people, more animals, and more long-distance contact has the bigger pool, and that side wins the lottery when the two meet.

Now comes the part where I have to slow down, because the story has a weak spot. The usual candidate for a disease that went the other way is syphilis. There is a real argument that it came from the Americas to Europe after Columbus. There is also a real argument that it was already in Europe, under other names, and that Europeans simply saw it spread as a new and terrifying epidemic in the late fifteenth century. Both arguments have supporters, and the genetic and historical evidence has been argued over for decades without a clean verdict.

So the honest answer is that we do not know how much traffic went back, and the case that matters most is disputed. The model says the flow should have been lopsided, and the evidence for the lopsidedness is strong. But the exact reverse trickle is a question the records cannot fully settle.

Let me also say what this does not mean. It does not mean any one population was better or worse, or that disease was a moral verdict on anyone. It is a matter of population, animals, and routes. Put the same people with the same animals in the same place, and the lottery looks different.

Now go back to the three rules and check them against the lopsided result one more time. A big host population, a reservoir of animals, and routes that keep the traffic moving. The Old World had all three in abundance. The Americas had some of them, and the crucial reservoir was largely missing. The one-way flow is not a mystery once you know what each disease needs to survive.

Last step: replay the same question on a counterfactual. Ask what would have happened if the population of the Americas had grown to match Eurasia, with the same animals. You get the same rules and a different outcome. The disease does not care about the continent. It cares about the three conditions.

So let's return to the question we started with. Why would germs only travel in one direction? Because germs travel only as far as the conditions let them, and for most of the diseases that mattered in 1492, the conditions were on one side of the ocean. The traffic was never really symmetrical. It was never going to be.
