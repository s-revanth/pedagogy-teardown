Picture an army crossing the Alps with elephants. Now picture it is autumn, it is snowing, and the local tribes have decided that the newcomers are a very good source of loot. That's the image, and it is the reason anyone still talks about this war. The man leading the army is Hannibal, a Carthaginian general, and he is about to walk into Italy and try to take the Roman Republic apart from the inside of the peninsula.

Here's the one-line chain. A siege, a declaration, a mountain crossing, a delayer, and a trap. We'll get to the trap in a while, but there is a lot of mountain to climb first.

The war starts in Spain, not Italy. A Spanish town called Saguntum was allied to Rome, and Hannibal besieged it. The siege lasted most of a year, and it was brutal. The town held out, then fell. It's important to be clear about the sequence here. Rome had a friend under attack, and it had a treaty that made Saguntum its responsibility. Hannibal knew that, and he attacked anyway.

Rome sent envoys to Carthage and asked for Hannibal to be handed over. Carthage refused, and that refusal made the war official. So here is the skit. A Roman envoy stands in a Carthaginian council chamber and says, hand him over. The councillors say, we are not doing that. The envoy says, then this is war. The councillors say, this was going to be war anyway. It's the cleanest declaration of war you'll see in a history video, and it is also a very rude way to end a conversation.

Hannibal had a plan, and it was not complicated. Go to Italy by land. Everything else in the plan is a means of doing that. The Romans controlled the sea, so a sea invasion was out. The land route through Spain and Gaul, and over the Alps, was the only way to surprise them. It was also the route that almost destroyed his army.

Think of the Alps as an obstacle course designed by a very unhelpful landlord. There is ice. There are rockfalls, steep paths, river crossings that can drown you, and mountain tribes who see an army marching through their valleys as an opportunity. An army of tens of thousands, plus horses and pack animals, plus elephants, is not a fast-moving thing on a mountain path. Every day it is slower, colder, and hungrier.

So here's the crossing as a skit. Act one. The columns climb into the snow. The soldiers are cold, the horses are nervous, and the elephants are, for once, the calmest members of the expedition. Act two. The path narrows, and the ice gets worse. One elephant slips and falls, and the column has to find a way around the mess. Act three. Tribesmen attack from the high ground, rolling stones and firing from the rocks, and the army has to push through under fire. Act four. The army comes down into Italy, thinner than it started, and the survivors are exhausted.

You may be wondering how many elephants made it. The sources do not agree on the exact numbers, and the numbers for the army are not precise either. What is consistent is that the crossing was very costly, and that by the time Hannibal reached the plains of northern Italy, only a few elephants were left. The animals were a huge advantage on the plains in theory and a huge liability on the mountains in practice.

Rome was not expecting this. It had been thinking about the war in Spain and in the sea. Its two consuls, Publius Scipio and Sempronius Longus, were sent to meet Hannibal in the north. Scipio was hurt in an early cavalry clash, and the main Roman force was beaten in the field, by a combination of Hannibal's cavalry and the terrain.

The next big fight came in the winter of the following year, at the Trebia River. Here, the Romans were in the wrong place at the wrong time, and the weather was on Hannibal's side. The Romans had to cross a freezing river to attack, and Hannibal's soldiers had been waiting on the other bank, warm enough to fight and well fed. The Roman troops were soaked, cold, and exhausted by the time they reached the line. Hannibal had planned the ambush well, with cavalry and hidden infantry, and the Romans were defeated.

Now a misconception. People often picture Hannibal as a wild aggressor who simply charged into Italy and hoped for the best. That's not really what happened. He planned around local support, timing, and the weather. He knew when the rivers would be cold and when his enemy would be tired. He was not blind. He was just very willing to take a risk that other generals would not take.

Rome panicked, which is what empires do when they lose two armies in a row. The Senate appointed new leaders, and the pressure to win a battle quickly got stronger. Then came the next disaster. In the spring, a Roman army marched along the shore of Lake Trasimene, following Hannibal's trail. It was early in the morning, and there was a heavy fog over the lake. Picture the Roman column strung out along a narrow road between the water and the hills. Hannibal's soldiers were already in the hills, waiting, and when the fog lifted, the trap was closed. The Roman army was destroyed, and its commander was killed.

Rome had had enough. It appointed a dictator, a man named Fabius Maximus, and he took a very different approach. Fabius did not want a battle. He wanted to shadow Hannibal, keep the Roman army alive, and wear the Carthaginians down through raids and delay. His nickname, roughly the delayer, was not a compliment at the time.

Imagine shadowing a bear. You follow it around the forest, you keep your distance, and you never let it corner you. You don't fight it, because that's how people get hurt. The strategy is sound, but it is deeply unpopular, because everyone watching thinks you are a coward who is letting the bear eat the village.

And that's exactly what happened. Hannibal's army raided the countryside, took food and supplies, and made Fabius look weak. Critics in Rome said he was too cautious. The public wanted a victory, and they wanted it now. Fabius was pushed aside, and two new consuls were sent out to find a battle.

Rome kept choosing the fight, and Hannibal kept choosing the ground. That's the pattern of the war so far, and it's the pattern that matters for what came next. A general who wins by choosing the terrain can make a larger army look foolish, and the other side can't simply out-number its way out of the trap.

The next battle was at Cannae, in the summer of 216 BC. The Romans fielded an enormous army, far larger than Hannibal's, and they gathered on open, flat ground by a river in southern Italy. The Carthaginians were smaller, and they were arranged in an unusual shape. It looked, at first glance, like a weak centre and two strong wings.

Here's the skit. The Roman centre pushes forward, expecting to break the line in the middle. The Carthaginian centre gives way, slowly, in a controlled retreat. The Romans push deeper and deeper into the gap. And then the wings close in behind them, from both sides, and the Roman legions are caught in a space too small to use their numbers. It is a trap built from the inside out, and the Romans walked into it with their full weight.

The sources give large numbers for the dead. The figures are not reliable enough for exact claims, and some are almost certainly exaggerated, but the scale of the defeat is not in doubt. A huge Roman army was destroyed in a single afternoon, and many senior officers were killed. The defeat was among the worst in Roman history.

So that's where we leave Part 1. Hannibal has crossed the Alps, beaten Rome in the field, and then trapped the largest army Rome had ever assembled at Cannae. He is in Italy, Rome is reeling, and the big question is whether Rome can recover, or whether this is the end of the Republic.

Next time, we follow the Roman fightback, and we meet a young general in Spain who is about to become a serious problem for Carthage. Bring your patience, because Rome's answer to Hannibal is not what you would expect. See you then.
