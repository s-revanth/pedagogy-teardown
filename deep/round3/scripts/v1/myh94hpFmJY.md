<<<NARRATION
Think about the animals that could seriously hurt you. The snake under a rock, the spider in a woodpile, the jellyfish that drifts through a warm coastal bay. Now think about where most of them live. You might guess that the dangerous ones are in the deserts or the far north, where things are harsh. But if you look at the map, many venomous species cluster near the equator, in warm rainforests, reefs and wetlands.

So here is my question. Why would that be? Before I go on, commit to an answer. Do you think venomous animals are more common near the equator, and if so, why? Pick one reason and say it out loud, or write it down. I will come back to it.

The easy answer is that venom is a defence. Animals that cannot run away need a way to deter predators, and a bite or a sting does that. If that were the whole story, you would expect venom to be spread fairly evenly, or to be strongest where predators are most dangerous. But defence does not explain the pattern on its own. Venom is also a hunting tool, and hunting pressure depends on what is around, not only on what might eat you.

So let's do this in order. First we map the pattern, then we test three ideas about it, and then we look at where the evidence is weaker than it sounds.

Here is the pattern. If you count venomous species across the world and plot them by latitude, the numbers rise as you approach the tropics. Near the poles, there are few venomous species. Near the equator, there are many. This is one of the most widespread patterns in biology. Many groups of animals are more diverse in warm places, not only the venomous ones. Birds, insects, plants, all of them tend to show more species in the tropics. So the question is whether venom follows that general pattern, or whether something specific about venom is driving it.

Now the first idea. Warm places simply have more species. More species means more predator and prey pairings, and more pairings means more chances for an arms race. Each side evolves a defence, the other side evolves a way around it, and the arms race can push both toward more specialised and more potent tools. If that is right, venom is more common because there are more competitors, and each one is under more pressure to develop something unusual.

The second idea is about specialisation. Some venomous animals do not just use venom to hurt. They use it to subdue a particular kind of prey. A cone snail, for example, fires a tiny harpoon loaded with a cocktail of toxins, and different species have different cocktails for different prey. Let me show you the idea with a simple mechanism. The snail extends a tooth, which works like a dart, and the venom is delivered through it. Each cocktail is tuned to a different target, and the more prey types there are in a place, the more specialised the cocktails may need to be. Warm ecosystems have more prey types, so the pressure to specialise may be higher.

The third idea is about cost. Venom is expensive to make. It takes energy to produce, store and deliver it. Cold-blooded animals in the tropics do not need to spend energy keeping warm, so they may have more energy to spare for making venom. Animals in colder places have to spend more of their energy on staying warm, and that may leave less room for an expensive toxin. This is the idea I find most interesting, and also the one I trust least. It sounds neat, but I have not seen a result that settles it.

Now let's be honest about the evidence. Each of these ideas makes a prediction. The arms-race idea predicts that venom should track species diversity. The specialisation idea predicts that venom should track the range of prey. The energy idea predicts that venom should track how cheap it is for an animal to make it. The problem is that these things all rise and fall together in the tropics. Warm places are rich in species, prey and energy at the same time. So a correlation does not tell you which one is doing the work.

There is also a sampling problem. Scientists have studied some venomous animals in great detail, and others barely at all. Well-known snakes and famous spiders get attention. A small frog, or a little-known marine worm, may have never been properly catalogued. If the places that get studied the most are also the places with the most species, the map itself is biased. So when you see a clean pattern, ask how the data was collected before you trust it.

Here is the part that I think is the most striking. Venom has evolved independently many times across the animal kingdom. Snakes, spiders, scorpions, cone snails, fish, lizards, mammals and jellyfish have all developed venom on their own, in different lineages, often in unrelated groups. When something evolves that many times, it usually means the pressure behind it is widespread, and not a quirk of one species. That is consistent with the arms-race idea, and it is also consistent with the other two, which is part of why the question is hard.

The same logic shows up for other traits. Some features of animals appear more often in warm climates, such as bright colours in certain groups, or larger numbers of nocturnal species. Each of those patterns has its own story. But the general lesson holds. A trait that shows up across many unrelated groups in one kind of environment is telling you something about that environment.

So let's be clear about what is settled and what is not. The pattern is real. Venomous species are more common in warm places. The mechanisms are plausible, and each of the three ideas has some support. But no single explanation has been shown to be the main driver, and the research is still developing. If you read a confident one-line answer to this question, be suspicious of it.

Let me go back to the opening animal. A snake or a spider that could hurt you, living in a warm, crowded place. It is not there because the world is designed to be dangerous. It is there because a warm, crowded ecosystem is a place where many species compete, many prey types exist, and some of those species have been pushed to build a very specialised tool. Venom is what happens when that pressure goes on for a long time.

So here is the question I asked at the start, with a better answer than the one most of us would give. Venomous animals live in warm climates for several reasons that may all be true at once, and the research is working out which of them matter most.
