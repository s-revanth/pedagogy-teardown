<<<NARRATION
Imagine the very early universe, a hot, dense soup of energy. Physics says that energy can turn into particles and their antiparticles in pairs. So if the universe started with equal amounts of matter and antimatter, then when they met, they would annihilate each other. Two particles would vanish, and their energy would come out as light. If that happened perfectly, the end result would be light and almost nothing else.

But that is not what we see. There is matter everywhere. Stars, planets, you. So something went wrong with the perfect symmetry, and the question this video asks is what.

Before we go on, commit to an answer. For every matter particle left over from the early universe, how many antimatter particles would you guess there were? One for one? A million to one? Just a few? Pick a number, and write it down. I will come back to it, and I want to see how close you get.

Let's start by making sure we are not dismissing antimatter as science fiction. A lot of people assume it is an idea from films, an exotic thing that would need a spaceship to find. That is not right. Antimatter is real, it is made in laboratories every day, and it shows up in hospitals.

So here is the plan. We will look at where antimatter came from, what it actually is, why we are told the universe kept some matter, and what remains unexplained. The last part is the most interesting, and it is still open.

Start with the history. In 1928, the physicist Paul Dirac wrote an equation that combined quantum mechanics with the rules of special relativity. It described the electron, and it did so beautifully. But it also had a strange extra solution, one that seemed to describe a particle with positive energy and the opposite charge. Dirac at first thought that solution might be the proton. That was wrong. But the solution was real, and it pointed to an antiparticle.

The antiparticle was found in 1932, by Carl Anderson, who was studying cosmic rays. Cosmic rays are high-energy particles that arrive from space and hit the upper atmosphere. Anderson used a cloud chamber, a box filled with supercooled vapour, where a charged particle leaves a visible track. In one photograph, a track curved in the direction that showed it had the electron's mass but the opposite charge. It was the positron, the electron's antiparticle, and it had been predicted years earlier by the mathematics.

So what is an antiparticle? The simplest definition is this. It is a partner particle with the same mass as the original and the opposite charge. An electron has negative charge; a positron has positive charge. When the two meet, they annihilate, and the energy of their mass is released as light. That is the minimal definition, and it is enough to proceed. The details get more interesting, and we will refine them as we go.

Antimatter is not just a curiosity. Positrons are used in a medical scan called PET. A patient is given a tracer that emits positrons, which quickly meet electrons in the body and annihilate, producing pairs of gamma rays that fly in opposite directions. A detector ring picks up those pairs and reconstructs where they came from. The result is a map of activity in the body, used to look at metabolism and to detect certain cancers. So a substance the mathematics predicted in 1928 is now part of routine care in some hospitals.

Now let's test the puzzle with a toy model, because the logic is easier to see with a simple system. Imagine a game with coins. Each round, you flip a coin to create either a matter token or an antimatter token. Every time a matter token and an antimatter token meet, both disappear. If every round produces one of each, and every pair meets and annihilates, then at the end of the game the table is empty. Run the game a thousand times, a million times, and the table is always empty. That is what a perfectly symmetric universe would look like.

So the toy model makes the problem sharp. If the universe really did start symmetric, we should not be here. The symmetry has to break, and the question is how.

In 1955, the antiproton was made and detected at the Bevatron in Berkeley. That was an important step, because the proton is far heavier than the electron, and making its antiparticle required a lot of energy. The antiproton showed that the pattern of matter and antimatter held for more than just the lightest particles.

Then, in 1995, researchers at CERN produced the first atoms of antihydrogen, a positron orbiting an antiproton. These atoms only lasted a fraction of a second before touching ordinary matter and annihilating. But the experiment showed that antimatter atoms can be made. In 2010, a different team managed to hold antihydrogen atoms in a magnetic trap for a while, long enough to study them.

And so far, the comparisons come out the same. In the experiments we can do, matter and antimatter behave in essentially identical ways. Their masses match, their energies match, and the rules that apply to one seem to apply to the other, with the charges reversed. Everything we can measure says the two are mirror images of each other, which makes the imbalance even more puzzling.

So one obvious idea is that antimatter is somewhere else. Maybe there are whole regions of the universe made of it, and we just have not seen them. That is a reasonable thought to test. If there were large regions of antimatter next to regions of matter, the boundary between them would be a place where the two meet and annihilate, producing a distinctive glow of gamma rays. Astronomers have looked for that glow, and they have not found the large-scale signal it would produce. That does not rule out every version of the idea, but it makes the simple version unlikely.

So we are left with the harder version. The universe really did end up with more matter than antimatter, and it did so for a reason. In 1967, the Soviet physicist Andrei Sakharov wrote down three conditions that any process would have to meet to produce that imbalance. Let me take them one at a time.

First, the process must violate baryon number. Baryons are the heavy particles like protons and neutrons, and the process must be able to create more of them than their antiparticles. Second, the process must violate what physicists call C and CP symmetry. C swaps particles and antiparticles. P flips things left to right, like a mirror. If the laws treated a process and its mirror-swapped version identically, there would be no preference for matter. Third, the process must happen out of thermal equilibrium. In a hot system that stays in balance, the tendency to create and destroy pairs cancels out. The imbalance needs a moment when the system is not in balance, when the cooling universe lets the preference stick.

Let's look at the second condition, because it turned out to be real. In 1964, researchers studying a particle called the kaon found that its decays were not perfectly symmetric under the combined operation of swapping matter and antimatter and mirroring the process. That was a surprise. Physics had assumed that combined symmetry held. The result was called CP violation, and it was confirmed in later experiments. It won a Nobel Prize, and it told us the laws of physics do have a small built-in preference.

Now the caveat, and this is the uncomfortable part. The CP violation we have seen is real, but it is far too small. When physicists add up the known sources of CP violation, the effect is many orders of magnitude too weak to produce the imbalance we see in the universe. The Standard Model, which is the best description of particle physics we have, includes CP violation in the mixing of quarks. But the numbers do not match. So something else must be going on, either in particles we have not found yet, or in the way the early universe behaved.

Let's make the size of the leftover concrete. The imbalance is about one extra matter particle for every billion or so pairs of matter and antimatter that were created and then annihilated. That is a tiny leftover. Think of a billion coin flips in which the coin comes up matter a little more often than antimatter, by just one flip. The one that survives is everything we are made of, every star, every planet, every person. The rest of the pairs turned into light, which filled the universe and is still around as the faint background glow of the cosmos.

To feel what annihilation would release, think about energy. Einstein's equation says that mass and energy are the same thing, with a conversion factor of the speed of light squared. If you could bring a kilogram of matter and a kilogram of antimatter together, you would release roughly two kilograms' worth of energy in full, which is about the same as a very large explosion. That is why antimatter is so hard to store, and it is why a leftover of one part in a billion is enough to build a universe.

So what is still open? Several things. We do not yet know what process produced the imbalance, whether it was a particle we have not seen, or a change in the early universe that happened in a specific way. And we do not know whether the answer will come from collider experiments, from precise measurements of particles that are rare, or from observations of the universe itself. Each route is being pursued, and none has delivered the answer yet.

Let's summarise. Antimatter is real, and we can make and hold it. The universe kept a tiny leftover of matter, and Sakharov's three conditions say what would be needed. The one condition we have seen directly is real, but too small. The rest is still open.

Now go back to the number you wrote at the start. For every matter particle, how many antimatter particles did you guess? The answer, in the early universe, was about one extra matter particle for every billion or so pairs. That means the leftover is tiny, and everything we see is built from it. So the hook has a clear answer. If the universe had been perfectly symmetric, nothing would be here to ask the question. The leftover is everything we are made of.

Next time, we look at another part of the universe that is hard to see directly.
