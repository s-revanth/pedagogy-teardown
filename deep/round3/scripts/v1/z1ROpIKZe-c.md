<<<NARRATION
Pick a city. Any city. Go back about seven centuries and ask one question. Who got a say in how it was run? For most cities, over the centuries since, the answer has moved in one direction. More people got a vote, more people could hold office, and more people could be heard by the people in charge. But there is a famous exception, and it is a city that did the opposite. Its governing council closed its doors. Why would a city do that, and why did it stay closed for so long?

The usual story is that democracy is a one-way escalator. Once a city starts widening its circle, it keeps going, and the only question is how fast. That story is tidy, and it is half right. The part it leaves out is that the circle can also be closed, and that closing is often easier than opening, once the people inside the circle have something to lose.

Here is a three-part model.

Rule one: a city's government is run by a list. The list says who is eligible to sit on the council and who can vote for it. Change the list, and you change the government.

Rule two: someone writes the list. In most cities the list is written by the people who are already on it, or by the people who benefit from it. The current council has a say in who joins the next council.

Rule three: a list can be closed. Once the people on it decide that no new names will be added, the list stops growing, and the government stays in the same hands. A closed list is very hard to reopen, because the people who would have to reopen it are the people who benefit from keeping it shut.

Now a toy council, so the rules run. Take a city with a council of twelve families. Each family has one seat, and the seats pass from father to son. A new family moves in and grows rich, and it wants a seat. The council votes. The existing twelve families are not eager to share, so they add a rule. Only families that already have a seat can nominate new members. The newcomers are shut out. Now the list is closed, and it will stay closed as long as the twelve families agree. The list decides who gets power, and the people on the list decide the list.

Now the real case. In the late thirteenth century, the city of Venice faced exactly that problem. Its great council had been growing, as more families gained a seat, and some of the families already in power were worried about newcomers. In 1297 the council took a decisive step. It restricted membership to families who had sat on the council in recent years, and it recorded those eligible families in a register. The register later became known as the Golden Book. Families not on the list were shut out of the council, and the list stayed closed for centuries.

Look at what happened. The council wrote the rule that decided who could write the rule. Rule two in action. Once the list was closed, the people on it had every reason to keep it that way. Rule three in action. The result was a city whose government stayed narrow for about five hundred years, until the republic ended in 1797 when Napoleon's forces took the city.

Now let's be careful, because this is the part where the historical record is shakier than the neat version suggests. The exact procedures, the sizes of the bodies involved, and the details of how the registers were used changed over time, and different historians emphasise different parts of the story. Some argue the closure was a response to a real crisis of competition among families, and others emphasise a more gradual process that the 1297 decision formalised. The broad outline is well supported, but the precise mechanics are argued over.

So why does this matter for other cities? Because the same three rules show up in cities that did open up. In many places, power was held by a narrow group of families or guilds, and the broadening of government came when the list was reopened. Voting rights expanded in stages, property qualifications were dropped, and elected offices were added. Each of those changes was a rewrite of the list, and each one met resistance from the people who were already on it. The cities that got more democratic were the ones where the list was eventually written by someone other than its current members. The cities that did not, like Venice for a long time, kept the list in the same hands.

That is the generalisation. The question is never just whether a city is more democratic than it was. It is who writes the list, and whether the list can still change. A city can become more democratic and still have a closed list in a particular office, and a city can look stable from outside while its list is quietly shrinking. The three rules let you see that difference.

So go back to the question from the start. Who got a say in your city seven hundred years ago? Probably a small set of families or guilds, with a narrow idea of who belonged. Now ask the same question today. The answer is wider, but it is not finished. There are still lists that decide who gets a say, and there are still people who write those lists.

Think about that the next time you hear that a city is more democratic than it used to be. Ask who writes the list, who is allowed onto it, and whether the people on it want new names. If you can answer those three questions, you know more about that city's government than the headline could tell you. And if you can answer them for your own city, you may find that the list is shorter, or longer, than you expected.

Who gets a say in your city now?
