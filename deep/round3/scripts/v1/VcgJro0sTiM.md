## Narration

<<<NARRATION
If you've stuck with this far, you probably want more math, so here's a short tour of places I'd send you next. One honest note at the start. These are my tastes, shaped by what I've watched and what I find useful to teach from. This is not a ranking of quality. Plenty of excellent channels aren't on this list, and you should try things and see what clicks for you.

Here's how I picked. I want channels that do a few specific things well. They should show you a picture before a formula. They should let a question sit with you for a moment before answering it. And they should be honest about what a method can and can't do. Those three qualities are rarer than they sound.

First, a kind of channel that works through proofs slowly, one line at a time, pausing to say why each step is the natural one. If you want to see the reasoning rather than just the result, start there. The pace is slow on purpose, and the slowness is the value. Don't skip the lines you think you already know.

Second, a kind of channel built around puzzles. Each video poses a problem, gives you a few minutes to try it, and then works through a solution that often reveals a trick you could have found yourself. The best of these leave you with a habit: asking whether a smaller version of the problem is easier. Try the small version first, every time.

Third, a kind of channel focused on the history of ideas. Some of the most interesting mathematics is the story of people who got it wrong first, argued with each other, and eventually found a cleaner way. These videos are less about technique and more about how a field came to believe what it believes. Watch one of these after a technical video, and the technical one will feel less arbitrary.

Fourth, a kind of channel that uses code. A short simulation, a plot that updates as you change a parameter, a demo that shows a theorem failing in a small case. For many people, watching a process run makes an abstract point stick faster than a derivation does. These videos are excellent for building intuition before the algebra arrives.

Fifth, and this one is a bit of a wildcard, a kind of channel that covers applied math. Statistics, signal processing, optimization. These fields borrow ideas from pure mathematics and then put them to work on messy data. Seeing the same tools used under pressure changes how they feel, and it shows you which parts of the theory people actually lean on.

A quick aside before the advice. A list like this goes stale. Channels change how they publish, some stop, and new ones appear every year. Treat this as a snapshot of one person's taste on one day, and check for yourself in a year.

Now, how to use the list. Don't binge. Pick one channel and watch one video. Then stop and try to reproduce the main example on paper. If you can explain the key step to a friend, you've learned more than you would from three more videos. Passive watching feels productive, and often isn't.

Also, a note on pace. Many of these channels run slower than you might expect. Pause when you need to. Rewind a step. A video that takes twice as long and sticks is better than one you finish and forget.

Finally, one small thing to keep. If you go looking for more, watch for the moment when a channel makes you pause, because that's usually the moment the explanation is doing its job. And if a video leaves you completely lost in the first minute, that's often fine. Give it another ten minutes before you decide.
NARRATION>>>
