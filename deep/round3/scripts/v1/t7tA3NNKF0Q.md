## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Look at the corner of any video feed and you'll see a number. A view count, ticking up as the clip plays. It looks like a simple measure of how many people watched something. But every view is a little piece of attention, and somebody, somewhere, is counting. Across the big platforms, the totals run into the billions every day. So the question is worth asking: where do all those views actually come from, and who really gets credit for them?

Let's start with what a view is. A view is a number that a platform decides to count. It is not a person choosing to watch a video from start to finish. Each platform sets its own rule. Some count a view once a clip has played for a few seconds, even if the viewer scrolled past it a moment later. Others count autoplay, where the next video starts on its own. So the number on screen is a definition, not a headcount of attentive people.

Now think about the feed as a conveyor belt. Every clip that comes along is a candidate for your attention. The system watches what you do. If you pause on a clip, it slows the belt down and shows you more like it. If you swipe away, it speeds up. The machine is not trying to make you happy. It is trying to keep you looking, because looking is what the platform sells. That's why the most effective clips are often the ones that stop you in the first second.

This is where the reposting problem starts. Imagine a creator who spends weeks making a video. They upload it, and it does well. Then a copy appears. Someone downloads it, cuts off the original name, maybe adds a caption or a watermark, and uploads it again. The copy is served to a new audience, and it gets its own views, its own likes, and its own momentum. The original creator might see a fraction of that attention, if any. The clip has been moved, and the counting follows the clip, not the person who made it.

Here's the misconception to clear up. When you see a huge view count, it's natural to assume that many separate people chose to watch that video. Sometimes that's true. But autoplay, short thresholds, and repeated uploads all inflate the number. A single clip can be counted many times across many copies, and a single person can add to the total just by letting a video play while they do something else. So a big number does not always mean a big audience. It means a big amount of counted attention, and the counting rules matter.

Here we need to be honest about what outsiders can see. Platforms publish some numbers, but not all of them, and the detailed data about how clips move between accounts is usually private. Researchers and creators can measure parts of the picture, but the full scale of reposting is hard to pin down from the outside. So the claim that billions of views are being taken from original creators is a strong claim, and it depends on data that most of us can't check directly. What we can say is that the incentives push in this direction.

Those incentives are not unique to one platform. Any system that pays for attention, whether it's a broadcast network, a search engine, or a social feed, will reward whatever holds people longest. Reposting is the cheapest way to get that attention. Original work costs time and money. A copy costs almost nothing. When the reward goes to the count rather than the source, the cheapest route tends to win.

So who loses? First, the original creator, who put in the work and gets less of the reward. Second, the audience, because the feed starts to show what is copied and repeated rather than what is new. Third, the platform itself, because when people notice that the numbers are inflated or shared across copies, trust in the counts drops. A view counter is only useful if people believe it measures something real.

That brings us to what you can do. You don't need to be a researcher. Check where a clip came from when you see it. If a video looks like a copy, look for the original. If you enjoy a creator's work, watch the original version, because that's the one that supports the person who made it. And when you see a huge count, treat it as a claim about the platform's counting rules as much as a claim about people.

So go back to the counter in the corner of the screen. The number is still ticking up. But now you know what it is. It's not a simple tally of people who chose to watch. It's a record of attention, measured by rules, and shared across copies that may have nothing to do with the original maker. Every view is a little piece of someone's time, and it's worth knowing where it went.
