## Narration

<<<NARRATION
You have never touched anything. That sounds wrong, so let's check it. Put your hand on a table. It feels solid. It pushes back. So you touched it, right? Well, it depends what you mean by touch, and by table, and by the word real. The answer keeps getting stranger the closer you look.

Here's the plan. We'll zoom in on the table and find out what solid actually means. Then we'll ask what colour is, why seeing is not the same as knowing, and how anyone can tell what's real at all.

Start with the table. Zoom in. Keep zooming. Past the wood grain, past the cells, past the molecules, until you reach atoms. An atom is mostly empty space. Its nucleus, the dense centre, is a speck, and the electrons around it sit so far away, compared with that speck, that the atom is like a stadium with a pea in the middle. Most of what your table is made of is nothing at all.

Even that picture is too tidy. Electrons aren't little planets on fixed orbits. A quantum description treats them as spread-out probabilities, clouds of where you're likely to find them, and the sharp edges of an atom are a statistical result rather than a hard surface. So the closer you look, the less the word "thing" seems to apply.

So why doesn't your hand fall through? The answer is not that atoms are hard little balls. It's that the electrons in your hand and the electrons in the table push on each other. That push is electromagnetism. When your hand meets the table, the two sets of electrons repel, and that repulsion is what you feel. Solid is not a substance. It's a pushing relationship between very distant bits of nothing.

That gives us a first definition. A thing is real, in one useful sense, if it keeps pushing back when you interact with it. The table pushes. Your hand pushes. The push is consistent, it doesn't depend on whether you're looking, and it can be measured. That's a good start, but we'll need more.

Now colour. A table looks brown, and most people would say the brown is in the wood. But it isn't really in the wood. The wood absorbs some wavelengths of light and reflects others. The reflected light is what enters your eye. Your eye and your brain turn that mix of wavelengths into the sensation of brown. The object has a surface that sends back certain light. The colour you experience is made in you.

Here's a test. Two lines on a page can be exactly the same length, but one can look longer, depending on the arrows at the ends. Your visual system is making decisions for you, and you don't see the decisions, only the results. So seeing is not the same as having direct access to what's out there. It's more like reading a report written by a very fast, very biased assistant.

Plato had a version of this long before anyone had a microscope. He pictured people chained in a cave, facing a wall. Behind them, a fire casts shadows of objects carried past. The prisoners see the shadows and name them. For them, the shadows are reality. If one of them is freed and walks outside, the sun is painfully bright, and the world is far richer than the wall ever showed.

A shadow puppet is a good model of the cave. You see a hand against a light, and the hand has a shape, a bird or a dog. But the shape is a projection of the hand, not the hand itself. What you see is a flat outline. The real thing is three-dimensional, moving, and lit from a source you can't see in the picture. The shadow is useful, and it isn't the whole story.

Descartes took the doubt further. He asked what he could be certain of. His senses could be fooled. His memory could be wrong. Maybe some deceiver was tricking him. But even in that case, he noticed, something must be doing the doubting. That's the one thing he couldn't doubt without doubting it. "I think, therefore I am" is a compact way of saying that some kind of experience must be happening, even if the rest of the world is up for debate.

Now the question of how we know anything at all. Your brain doesn't receive the world like a photograph. It builds a model of it from noisy signals, guesses what's out there, and then checks the guess against new data. That process works. It's why you can catch a ball, cross a street and recognise a friend's face. But it's a model, and models can be wrong.

Science is one of the most reliable ways we have to test models. A good scientific claim makes a prediction that could fail, and then you check it. If the table isn't mostly empty space, a precise measurement should show it. If colour is stored in the object, a change in the light should not change the colour you see. We run those checks again and again, and the models that survive are the ones that predict well. That's not the same as certainty, but it's a very useful kind of reality.

Here's the caveat, and it's an honest one. Philosophers have argued about these questions for thousands of years, and they still don't agree. Some say the external world is real and our senses are imperfect windows. Some say the mind builds most of what we call reality. Some say the question is badly posed. We don't have a final answer. What we have are better and worse models, and a method for telling them apart.

That brings up a famous tangent. Some thinkers have asked whether we might be living in a simulation, a computer program running a world that looks real to the people inside. It's a fun thought, and it has one interesting feature. If the simulation exists, it still needs rules. Something has to decide how the table pushes, how light travels, how atoms behave. Even a fake world would have to be consistent to be worth living in. So the question shifts from "is it real?" to "what follows the rules?"

Let's go back to the table. The table is real in the way that matters to your hand, because it keeps pushing back in predictable ways, and the pushing can be measured and predicted. The colour is real in the way that matters to you, because your visual system reliably builds it from light. The mind is real, because it's the thing doing the doubting and the modelling. None of these is a single hidden essence. They're patterns that hold together.

So here's a working definition. A thing is real if it persists, interacts with other things, and makes predictions that hold up when you test them. Not everything you see meets that test, and not everything you imagine does. But the table does. Your sense of colour does. Your memories do, as long as you check them against other evidence.

Now it's your turn. Pick one thing you would call real without thinking. A friend, a country, the number seven, a memory of the house you grew up in. Ask what it would look like if it were not real. Then test it. What would you expect to see? What would surprise you? If you can make a prediction and check it, you've done the same thing a scientist does, with the same limits.

That's the key lesson. You can't step outside your model to see the world directly. But you can build models that are more reliable than the ones you started with, and you can check them against the world, which pushes back.

Next time, we'll look at a question that feels even more slippery. If the table is mostly empty space, then what about time? Is it real, or is it just a story our brains tell about change?

Thanks for watching. Push back when something pushes back.
NARRATION>>>
