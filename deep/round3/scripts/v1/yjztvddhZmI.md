<<<NARRATION
Here is a number that most of us have stopped hearing. Every year, road crashes kill more than a million people around the world. That is more people than live in many large cities, lost on the road, in a typical year. Most of us know it in the abstract, and then we get in the car and forget it.

Before I go on, I want you to commit to an answer. Estimate that toll, worldwide. Then estimate it for a single country that you know well. Write both numbers down. We will come back to them, and I want to see how close your estimates are to the data.

Now let's look at the question this video is actually about. Should we want driverless cars on the roads now, even if they are not perfect yet? Many people instinctively say no. They say, if a machine kills someone, that is a scandal, and so the machine has to be flawless first. I understand the feeling. But I want to test whether that standard makes sense, because it has a hidden assumption in it.

The hidden assumption is that the right comparison is the machine against perfection. It is not. The right comparison is the machine against the people it would replace. If the current human system kills a great many people every year, then a machine that is safer than that system is saving lives, even if it still makes mistakes. Waiting for perfection is not a neutral choice. It keeps the existing toll in place.

I want to test that with the people around us. I asked a few strangers on the street to guess the annual toll. One person guessed a few thousand. Another guessed a few hundred thousand. A third person said they did not know, but it felt like it was going down. When I told them the real number, most people were surprised by how high it was. That surprise is worth noticing, because it means we are not paying attention to a risk that is very large.

So where does the danger come from? When investigators try to explain why crashes happen, they usually end up pointing at the driver. Official studies in the United States have often attributed the large majority of crashes to a critical error by the driver, such as distraction, fatigue, misjudging a gap, or failing to notice something in time. That sounds like a sweeping claim, and I want to be careful with it. The statistic depends on how investigators sort crashes into categories, and different studies draw those lines differently. So the broad point is well supported, but the exact percentage should be treated with care.

Still, the broad point is worth sitting with. Most crashes are not caused by a single dramatic decision. They are caused by ordinary lapses. A glance at a phone for a second too long. A late brake on a wet road. A tired driver who drifts over a line. None of those are rare. They are the background noise of driving, and they happen to everyone, including careful people. A machine does not get tired, does not read a message, and does not look away for a second. That does not make it perfect, but it removes one of the largest sources of failure.

Now let's look at what the data says, and what it does not. Several companies running robotaxi services in cities have published safety records. Their numbers often show fewer serious crashes per mile than human drivers in comparable areas. That is encouraging. But there are a lot of ways to get those numbers wrong. Robotaxis tend to operate in specific, mapped parts of a city, often at lower speeds, and in good weather. Human drivers cover a wider range of roads, including highways at high speed and rural roads at night. A fair comparison has to match those conditions, and that is hard to do.

There is also the problem of small samples. A few million miles is a lot of driving, but serious crashes are rare, so a handful of incidents can move the numbers a lot. Two years of data from one city does not tell you what a whole country would look like. So the honest summary is that the early results look promising, and that they still need careful comparison before anyone can say how much safer a machine is in general.

Let me deal with the famous dilemma too, because it comes up every time. If a car has to choose between hitting one person or another, which should it pick? It is a real question in ethics, and people have spent a lot of time on it. But in practice, those situations are rare. The everyday risk is not a machine choosing between two bad options. It is a machine, or a person, failing to notice the situation in the first place. So if you care about lives, the bigger lever is the boring one: the steady reduction of ordinary errors.

Now the human side of this. Picture someone who is walking across a crosswalk on a sunny afternoon. A driver, looking at a phone, does not see them until it is too late. That is a single event, and it is one of thousands like it every week. Now picture the same crosswalk, with a machine that never looks at a phone, never gets drowsy, and always watches the road. Not every collision would be avoided. But fewer of those afternoons would end the way they did.

Let's look at the other side of the argument too, because it is serious. People worry about new kinds of failure. A machine can make a mistake that no human would make, and that mistake can be strange and hard to predict. There are questions about who is responsible when something goes wrong, about how the software is tested, about privacy, and about what happens to jobs. Those questions are real, and they deserve answers. They are not reasons to ignore the question of how many people die on the roads now.

The same reasoning shows up in other fields. Aviation did not wait for perfect autopilots before using them. It used them where they were better than the average pilot in specific conditions, and it collected data, learned from every incident, and tightened the rules over time. Medicine works the same way. We approve treatments that are better than the existing option, not treatments that are flawless. The question is always whether the new thing is better than the thing it replaces, and by how much.

So what would a sensible rollout look like? I think it would look like the aviation approach. Deploy the systems where the data supports them. Require companies to report every serious incident in a standard way, so the comparisons can be checked by people who are not employed by the company. Make the performance data public, and update the rules as the evidence comes in. That is slower than a press release, and it is also how you earn trust that lasts.

Now let's go back to the numbers you wrote down at the start. How close were your estimates? For many people, the worldwide toll is higher than they expected, and that gap is the point. We have been living with a large, steady risk, and we have accepted it because it is familiar. A better machine is worth wanting, not because it is flawless, but because it could reduce a toll that is already far too high.

So let's come back to the hook. A million lives a year on the roads is the baseline. The question is not whether the machine is perfect. The question is whether it can lower a number we have stopped noticing. If it can, waiting for perfection is not caution. It is accepting the current toll for a bit longer.

Next time, we look at another case where the familiar choice is not the safe one.
