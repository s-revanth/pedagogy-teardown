<<<NARRATION
Vienna, 1907. A young man from Austria walks up to the Academy of Fine Arts with a portfolio of drawings under his arm, and he is turned away. It is not the last time he will be turned away, and it is not the last time he will blame somebody else. Keep that in mind, because history has a habit of following people who have bad days and decide the bad days were somebody else's fault.

This is a short video, and it covers a lot of ground. Here's the one-line chain: failed painter, war messenger, party speaker, failed putsch. We'll go in order, and we'll be careful about what we claim, because a lot of the most familiar stories about this man were told by the man himself.

He was born in 1889 in Braunau am Inn, a small town on the border between Austria and Bavaria. His father was a customs official, a strict man who expected a career in the civil service. The son had other ideas. At school in Linz he did badly, he did not get along with teachers who wanted him to behave, and he spent much of his time sketching buildings and daydreaming about a life as an artist.

Then came Vienna. He arrived with a little money and a lot of confidence, and the confidence did not survive contact with the city. He lived in a men's hostel, sold drawings and postcards to get by, and applied to the Academy of Fine Arts. The Academy said no. He tried again the next year, and the answer was no again. The letters were brief. There was no ceremony to the rejection, just a sheet of paper that told him the thing he wanted was not going to happen. Imagine the mood of a man who had told everyone at home he would be a great painter.

By 1913 he had moved to Munich, in Germany. A year later, the First World War began, and he volunteered for the Bavarian army. This is where the story of a painter becomes the story of a soldier. He served as a dispatch runner, carrying messages between units under fire. It is dangerous work, and it is not the work of a commander, and that matters. He was decorated for it, and he was wounded, and he carried the experience with him for the rest of his life.

Here's a misconception worth clearing up. He is often pictured as a war hero who led men into battle. That is not what he did. He carried messages, which is a real job with real risks, but it doesn't put you in charge of anyone. The leadership story came much later, and it was built on the medals and the wounds rather than on any command he actually held.

When the war ended in 1918, Germany was a country in shock. The army had lost. The government that signed the armistice was unpopular. And a story began to spread that the army had not really been beaten on the battlefield, but had been betrayed from behind by politicians and by others. This is what historians call the stab-in-the-back myth. It is a very clean story. It explains defeat without anyone having to be the one who made mistakes. It's also a template, and we're going to see it again.

After the war, the army gave him a job. It needed people to watch the small political groups that had popped up in Munich, and he was assigned to attend their meetings and report back. One of these small groups was the German Workers' Party. He went to a meeting, expecting to take notes, and he ended up joining. Then he started speaking, and it turned out he was very good at it. He could hold a room. He could repeat a simple idea until it felt like common sense. The party grew, and it changed its name to the National Socialist German Workers' Party, which is the name you'll recognise.

The party's strongest weapon was that stab-in-the-back template. Find a clear villain, offer a simple explanation, and promise a dramatic fix. Each rally repeated the same script, and each new member heard it in a slightly different form. You can see how that works, and you can also see why it was so hard to argue with inside a crowded hall.

By November 1923, Germany was in a severe economic crisis, with hyperinflation wiping out savings and everyday life falling apart. Hitler decided the moment had come. He and his supporters planned a takeover of the Bavarian government in Munich, and on the night of November the eighth they burst into a beer hall where state leaders were speaking. The next day, they marched through the centre of the city.

Picture the march. A column of armed men moves down a broad street toward the city's heart, expecting the crowd and the army to fall in behind them. Instead, police open fire. Several people are killed, the march falls apart, and the leaders scatter. Within a few days, the attempt is over. What was meant to be a revolution lasted less than a day in the street.

Hitler was arrested and put on trial. The courtroom gave him a platform, and he used it well, speaking at length and making himself the centre of the story. He was convicted, but the sentence was short, and that is part of the history too. We will come back to what he did in prison, because that is where the next stage of the story begins.

A quick caveat before we go. This is a short video, and I'm skipping a lot. Many of the stories he told about himself, about his war and his wounds and his early life, were polished and some were simply false. Historians have worked to separate what happened from what he later said happened, and in some areas the record is still contested. Treat the outline as the reliable part, and treat the personal anecdotes with suspicion.

In part two, we follow him into prison, through the book he wrote there, and into the long road back to power. It is a story where the early chapters look almost ordinary, and that is exactly why it is worth looking at carefully. See you there.
