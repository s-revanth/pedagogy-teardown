## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Imagine an ordinary afternoon about sixty-six million years ago. The planet is warm. There are no ice caps. Forests stretch across continents, the seas are full of life, and the largest land animals on Earth are the dinosaurs, which have been running the place for well over a hundred million years. Nothing about this afternoon looks special. The sun is up. Life is going on. And then, within a single day, almost everything about this world changes for good.

We usually tell this story as a sudden event, a meteor and a flash. But the real story is a sequence, and if you slow it down to the scale of minutes, it becomes much clearer and much stranger. So let's do it that way. We'll walk through the day minute by minute, then ask what happened in the weeks and years that followed, and then ask why the survivors survived.

First, a quick definition, so we know what we are talking about. When scientists say the dinosaurs died out, they mean the non-avian dinosaurs. The big reptiles we picture, like Tyrannosaurus and Triceratops, along with their relatives, are gone. Birds are not gone. Birds are dinosaurs too, a branch that made it through. So the word "died" has to be handled with care. Some lineages ended, and one lineage kept flying.

Rock layers record the end of this world. In rock from about sixty-six million years ago, there is a thin band that marks a boundary. Below it, fossils of dinosaurs and many other creatures are common. Above it, most of those fossils are missing. Geologists call this the boundary between two geological periods. It's a clear line in the rock, and it tells a story of loss.

Now picture a clock. Each second on this clock represents a few seconds of the real event. Our clock starts with the asteroid. It is roughly ten kilometres across, travelling at tens of thousands of kilometres per hour, and it hits what is now the Yucatán peninsula in Mexico. That is minute zero. The impact is so violent it is hard to picture, so we'll just follow what happens next.

At the moment of impact, the energy released is enormous, far more than any weapon ever built. The rock hits the ground and vaporises. A crater tens of kilometres wide forms in seconds. The shock wave travels outward through the crust, and the ground around the impact site is lifted, cracked, and flattened. Anything within a few hundred kilometres is destroyed almost at once: forests, animals, rivers. This is the first wave of the event, and it is the one people imagine. But it is only the beginning.

Within the first few minutes, a heat pulse spreads outward from the impact. It is a flash of thermal radiation, and anything exposed to it that is not sheltered can burn. Think of an animal in the open on the wrong side of a hillside. Shelter matters. Burrows matter. Water matters. A burrowing mammal, a small reptile under a log, or a creature under water may survive the first minutes that would kill an exposed one.

Meanwhile, debris is being thrown into the sky. Huge amounts of rock, dust, and melted material are blasted up through the atmosphere. Some of it escapes into space. Much of it falls back down, and as it re-enters the atmosphere, it heats up. Here is the part that surprises people. The falling ejecta can heat the entire atmosphere, and the sky itself can glow. Within hours, the surface of the planet is being warmed by this rain of hot material, across the whole globe, not just near the crater.

Now picture the hours that follow. Wildfires start, fuelled by the heat and by the burning debris falling on forests and grasslands. Tsunamis race across the oceans, carved out by the impact and the shaking of the seafloor. Seismic waves ripple through the crust and rattle continents. Creatures in the region that have survived the first blast are now trapped in fire, water, and collapsing landscapes. The hours of this day have a brutal sequence, and each step makes the next one worse.

Here we need an honest caveat. The timings we're describing come from computer models, from the shapes of the crater, and from the chemistry and layering of sediments. These are powerful tools, but they are not a time machine. The exact sizes of the waves, the speed of the fires, and the spread of the debris are estimates, and they are refined as new evidence arrives. There is also a real debate about a second factor. Around the same time, enormous volcanic eruptions were building up in what is now India, the Deccan Traps. Scientists disagree about how much those eruptions contributed. Most agree the impact was the decisive blow, but the picture is more complicated than one rock from space.

Then comes a misconception that's worth clearing up. People often imagine that the dinosaurs died in one instant, the way a light switches off. That isn't what the evidence shows. The impact was instant. The collapse of the world that depended on sunlight took much longer. Non-avian dinosaurs were already in decline in some regions before the impact, and the final loss unfolded over the following days, weeks, and years. And the birds, the living branch of the dinosaurs, didn't vanish at all. They were on the same planet on the same day, and some of them kept going.

Now the long part. The dust and soot thrown into the sky spread around the globe. Sunlight is blocked for months, and perhaps for years. Without sunlight, plants can't photosynthesise. Without plants, the herbivores that depend on them starve. Without herbivores, the predators that feed on them starve too. The food web collapses from the bottom up. Sulphur from the rock also forms aerosols that reflect sunlight and cool the planet further. The result is a world that goes dark and cold, with the usual rhythm of seasons suddenly breaking apart.

It's worth noticing the pattern here, because it applies far beyond this day. A disruption that hits the base of the food web is slow to show its full effect, but it spreads upward. Large animals need a lot of food. Animals that can eat very little, or can eat dead material, do better. Organisms that can live in soil, in water, or underground often pass through the worst years. So when the dust settles, the planet is no longer ruled by the largest creatures. It's shaped by the smallest and most flexible.

That brings us to the payoff. Who survived? Small mammals survived, often in burrows and hiding places. Many birds survived, especially those that could eat seeds and that were not limited to a narrow diet. Some reptiles, amphibians, and aquatic creatures survived too. Plenty of plant life came back from seeds and spores that could wait out the darkness. The survivors weren't necessarily the strongest. They were the ones that could tolerate a lot of change, eat a wide range of food, or hide until the sun returned.

And there's a quiet irony in this. Those survivors would go on to become the ancestors of much of what lives on Earth now, including us. The mammals that came through the darkness spread out into a world without dinosaurs, and over tens of millions of years, they filled the niches the giants had left. Birds, meanwhile, kept their dinosaur line alive and changed in ways we can still see whenever a sparrow lands on a branch.

So go back to the calm afternoon we started with. The forests, the sea, the big animals in the sun. From the point of view of that afternoon, nothing was ending. The same sun shone, the same continents sat in their places. But from the point of view of the rock, that afternoon was the last ordinary hour of a world that had lasted for a very long time. The clock on the screen only ran for a few minutes. The consequences ran for millions of years.

If you want to remember this day, remember the sequence. A single impact, a spreading wave of heat and fire, a sky that goes dark, a food web that unravels from the bottom, and a planet that slowly rebuilds itself out of the few creatures small and flexible enough to live through it. That is what the day the dinosaurs died actually looked like. It was not a switch being flipped. It was a world that ran out of light.
