<<<NARRATION
Australia, 1932. A major, two machine guns, a truck, and an enemy that is nearly two metres tall, has feathers, and can run at about fifty kilometres an hour. Yes, the enemy is a bird. So how does a country lose a war to a bird? To find out, we have to go back, because the birds were not the first problem, and the army was not the first to make a bad decision.

After the First World War, the Australian government had a plan for its returning soldiers. Here is some land. Go and farm it. It was a generous thank you, and it had a useful side effect, because it filled a big, mostly empty country with people who knew how to handle a rifle and a shovel. Many of those soldiers were given blocks of land in Western Australia. The land was dry, scrubby, and full of stumps. Before anything could grow, somebody had to clear it, fence it, and find water.

Picture the life of a soldier settler. You arrive with a loan, a little money, and a promise. The field needs years of hard work before it pays anything. You borrow for fencing, for seed, for equipment, and you are betting that the wheat will pay the debts before the bank comes asking. The fence is the first thing to go up and, as it turns out, the first thing to fail.

Picture a fence line at dawn. The settler walks out to check his crop and finds a neat row of large birds standing exactly where his wheat should be. They look back at him with the calm of animals who have no intention of going home. He has a loan to pay, a family to feed, and a field full of birds that have, in fairness, a better sense of what this land is for.

Then the Great Depression arrived, and the bet went bad. Wheat prices collapsed. A farmer who had planted a crop found that the price barely covered the cost of the seed. The loans did not care about any of that. The settlers were now poor, stressed, and short of cash, and that is the state of mind in which you least want an unexpected problem landing on your doorstep.

Which is where the emus come in. Emus are large flightless birds. They are tall, fast, and very good at ignoring your fence. In the early 1930s, a large number of them had been drifting into the wheatbelt in search of food and water. Here's the thing I want you to notice. Every actor in this story has one want. The emu wants food and water, and it wants them consistently, every season. The farmer wants a crop. The army wants to look like it is doing something. Once you know each actor's single want, the rest of the story starts to predict itself.

Politically, the government had a problem of its own. It did not want to be the one that abandoned the settlers, and it did not want to pay for a long-term rescue plan during a depression. A campaign against birds is cheaper than a rescue, and it looks like action. Keep that in mind, because it explains a lot.

The settlers did what people do. They wrote letters. They complained to local officials. They pressed the government to act. At first the government responded in the careful language of a body that does not want to appear to do nothing. Then the pressure built, and the answer came back in a different language. Send soldiers.

Here is the logic, and it's worth noticing how sensible it sounds at the time. Machine guns kill things efficiently. Birds are things. The army has machine guns, and it has men trained to set them up. So the government sent Major Meredith out with a pair of Lewis guns, a crew, and a truck. It is a plan that makes perfect sense if you don't think about birds at all.

Let's run the first campaign as a skit, because it has the shape of a very short play. Act one. The men set up a position at the edge of a paddock, the guns are ready, and they wait for the flock. Act two. The birds come into view. The men open fire. A few birds go down. The men cheer. Act three. The rest of the flock turns, spreads out, and keeps walking, in the manner of animals who have been shot at before and are not impressed.

Act four is the truck. The men drive after the birds, and the gun is firing from the back of a moving vehicle. This is a fine way to fill the air with lead and a poor way to hit anything. The birds keep pace with the truck over the rough ground, and they change direction faster than the driver can turn the wheel. Then the Lewis gun jams. You saw this coming, because I showed it to you in the cold open. The gun stops, somebody swears, and the emus stroll off across the paddock as if they have somewhere better to be.

You might think a machine gun should beat a bird. And the honest answer is that a machine gun is brilliant when the target stands still in a line. Emus do not stand still in a line. They zig, they zag, and each burst of fire is a stream of bullets sent into the space where a bird was a moment ago. The army spent a great deal of ammunition, and the tally of dead birds was far smaller than the tally of rounds fired. If you want a rule for the whole campaign, here it is: the bullets ran out faster than the birds ran.

Then the army tried again. The second campaign has the same structure as the first. Set up. Wait. Birds arrive. Fire. Birds scatter. The gun jams or runs dry. Pull back. Repeat. Each time, the soldiers add a detail. They widen the line, they try to herd the birds toward a particular spot, and they bring more firepower. Each time, the birds respond by doing exactly what they were already doing. The soldiers change their tactics, and the birds keep winning the same argument in different clothes.

Imagine the briefing room. Someone says the birds will head for the water, so set up by the water. They head for the water, and they were already there, because they got there first. Someone else says the birds follow the wheat, so set up in the wheat. The wheat is everywhere. Every plan is correct on paper, and every plan puts the soldiers exactly where the birds are not, or exactly where the birds already are, which is a different kind of wrong.

Now, I have to be straight with you about the numbers. Accounts of how many birds died vary. Some give a figure in the hundreds. Others are vaguer. Part of the record comes from official reports, part comes from newspaper stories, and both had reasons to make the campaign look a particular way. So treat any exact count as a rough estimate, and treat the overall picture, a campaign that did not work, as the more reliable part.

The newspapers had a field day. The campaign was mocked, the soldiers were teased, and the name it got has stuck ever since. Being the punchline of the press is a bit like being a famous footnote, except that the footnote has feathers and no intention of leaving.

It is worth saying that the emus were never in danger. The complaint was about crops and fences, not about the species, and the birds did not become rarer because of the campaign. Years later, people still bring the story up at parties, which is the one outcome this campaign reliably achieved.

So here's the chain. The government gave returning soldiers land that was hard to farm. The Depression crushed wheat prices. A large population of emus moved into the wheatbelt, looking for food and water. The farmers needed help. The government answered an agricultural problem with machine guns. Each step looks sensible if you squint. Put them together and you get a military campaign against a flock of birds, complete with a jammed gun, a truck chase and a quiet retreat.

The army packed up. The emus stayed. The farmers kept fencing and hoping, and the birds did not read the reports. Nobody in this story found a clean solution, and the birds kept their territory. If you're keeping score, and I know some of you are, the emus won.

Remember that jammed gun from the cold open? That tiny moment turned out to be the whole story. That's what I love about these Mini-Wars. The silly detail is often the beam holding up the entire disaster, and the disaster is funnier when you realise it was never really about the birds. It was about a plan that never asked the birds what they wanted, which was food and water, which was the entire problem.

Next time, we're going somewhere that also made an awful strategic decision. It doesn't have feathers, but it does have a lot of confidence. See you then.
