## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Here's an integral that looks like it should be a mess. Take the sine of x, divide it by x, and integrate that from zero out to infinity. The answer is pi over two. Fine, that's a famous result. Now multiply the integrand by another factor, the sine of x over three, divided by x over three. The integral comes out to pi over two again. Multiply by a third factor, with x over five in place of x, and the answer is still pi over two.

Keep going. Add a factor, then another, and another. Every time, the integral returns pi over two, to more digits than you would ever check by hand. Then, at some point, one more factor arrives, and the answer shifts. Only slightly, but it shifts.

So here's the question for this video. When does the pattern break, and why does it break so late?

The plan goes like this. First we'll look at a single curve and what its integral means. Then we'll try the brute-force approach, which tells us the numbers agree but not why. Then we'll find a picture that explains the surprise, turn that picture into a fact about boxes, and find the exact point where the pattern gives way. At the end I'll be honest about which part of the explanation is a heuristic and which part a rigorous proof would need.

Start with one curve. The function sine of x over x is a wave that wiggles with decreasing height as you move away from zero. The integral is the signed area under it, so the question is how the positive humps and negative dips cancel as you go out to infinity. For this curve, they cancel to give pi over two.

Now the brute-force route. You could compute the integral numerically, chopping the range into tiny pieces and adding up the areas. Do that for the first few products and the numbers agree with pi over two to a remarkable number of decimal places. A natural reaction is that someone must have a bug in the code. Surely it can't be that clean.

But it isn't a bug, and the numerical check is actually the point. The agreement is real, and what we need is a reason for it. Brute force tells you it's true, but not why. For that, we need a different language.

Here's the language. The sine of x over x is the Fourier transform of a simple shape, a flat box. Let's name that box: a flat-topped shape of height one, centered at zero, with a width that depends on the factor. Each factor in our product corresponds to a box of its own. The box for sine of x over x has half-width one. The factor with x over three has a box of half-width one third, and so on down the odd numbers.

Why does this help? A standard fact says the integral of a product of these curves can be rewritten as the value at zero of the convolution of their boxes. A convolution is what you get when you slide one shape over another and add up the overlaps. So the question becomes: what does the convolution of all these boxes look like at zero?

Picture it. Each box is a flat plateau, and convolving plateaus gives a smooth bump. Convolving two boxes gives a trapezoid. Convolving a third box rounds it further. Each extra box smooths the shape more. As long as the total width is small enough, the bump is flat-topped near zero, and its height there is exactly what we want. That's why the answer stays at pi over two. The top is still flat where it matters.

Now the surprise becomes visible. As the boxes pile up, the top stays flat for a while, so nothing changes. But the widths keep accumulating. Eventually the bump's top begins to sag at zero. At that moment the integral stops giving pi over two, and it shifts by just a sliver.

Let's test this on the slider. Drag the widths and watch the bump. With a few boxes, the top is flat and the value holds. Add the next box, and the top is still flat enough that you can't tell. Add one more, and the peak dips, very slightly. That's the moment the pattern breaks.

Now the exact threshold. The first width is one, and the rest are one third, one fifth, one seventh, and so on, the reciprocals of odd numbers. Set the first one aside. The running total of the others grows slowly, but it does keep growing, and it first passes one at the eighth factor. That's the factor with x over fifteen. Before it, the total is just under one. Add it, and the total is just over. You can check this by adding the reciprocals yourself, and it lands exactly where the numerical evidence says the break happens.

This has a broader lesson. The sine-over-x identity isn't a special fluke. It's an example of an almost identity: an expression that equals a simple value for a long stretch, because the smoothing has not yet had enough room to disturb the peak. Near-misses like this appear whenever you multiply together functions with simple Fourier pictures. The habit to take away is to look for the picture first, and let the picture tell you where the pattern gives way.

Now the caveat. The box-and-convolution picture is a heuristic. It captures why the answer holds and why it breaks, and it predicts the threshold. A rigorous proof has to handle the tails and the fact that these integrals are not absolutely convergent in the usual sense, which requires more careful analysis. I'll leave the full argument to the references, because the picture is the thing I want you to keep.

Let's recap. Each factor is a box. Multiplying the curves becomes convolving the boxes. The value at zero stays fixed while the top stays flat, and it breaks when the total width crosses a threshold. That's the whole mechanism.

So, back to the opening. The first several integrals weren't a bug, and they weren't a coincidence. They were true for a reason you can now see: the peak hadn't yet been disturbed. The surprise was never that the answer changed. The surprise was how much room there was before it did.
