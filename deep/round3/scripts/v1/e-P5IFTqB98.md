## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Somewhere in the sky, there is an object that nothing can escape from. Not gas, not dust, not starlight. It doesn't glow on its own. It just sits there, pulling everything nearby, with a bright ring of gas spinning around it. That's a black hole, and it may be the strangest thing in the universe. So let's follow one through its whole life, from birth to death, and see what happens along the way.

Let's start with a simple definition. A black hole is a region where mass has been packed so tightly that the escape speed at its edge is faster than the speed of light. Nothing can go faster than light, so once you cross that edge, you can't come back out. We call that edge the event horizon. It's not a surface you could stand on. It's a boundary of no return.

Now, how does a black hole get made? Most of them start as massive stars. A star is a balance. Gravity tries to squeeze it inward, and the heat and pressure from nuclear fusion push back outward. For most of its life, the two forces hold each other in place. But when a massive star runs out of fuel, that balance breaks. The core collapses under its own weight. The outer layers may blow away in a supernova, a huge explosion that scatters the star's material across space. If the core left behind is heavy enough, it keeps collapsing until it becomes a black hole.

It helps to picture escape speed. Throw a ball up from the ground, and it comes back down, because Earth's gravity pulls it. Throw it harder, and it goes higher, but it still falls back. Escape speed is the speed you need to leave a planet's pull for good. Now imagine squeezing Earth, or a star, into a smaller and smaller space while keeping its mass the same. The surface gets closer to the centre, the pull gets stronger, and the escape speed goes up. At some point, the escape speed would be faster than light. That's the point where the black hole forms.

Now think of the event horizon as a one-way line on a map. You can walk toward it and turn back, as long as you stay outside. But once you cross it, every path leads inward. It's not a wall you hit. It's a line where the rules of your future become fixed. From the outside, a black hole looks like a dark patch with a bright halo of hot gas spiralling in before it disappears.

What would it feel like to fall in? Here the picture gets weird. Gravity is stronger at your feet than at your head, if you fall feet first. That difference pulls you apart. The effect is sometimes called spaghettification, and it's a bit like stretching a rope by pulling harder at one end than the other. For a small black hole, the stretching would start well before the horizon, and it would be fatal long before you got close to the centre. For a huge black hole, the stretching is gentler at the horizon, but it still gets lethal further in.

Here we should be honest. The picture of spacetime as a stretched sheet, with a heavy ball sitting in the middle, is a useful way to imagine gravity bending space. But it breaks down near the centre of a black hole, where the equations of general relativity stop giving clean answers. We cannot look inside a black hole. Light can't come out, so we can't see what's happening past the horizon. Everything we know about the interior comes from theory, and theory has limits.

Once formed, a black hole can keep growing. It can swallow gas, dust, and stars that come too close. Some black holes sit quietly, while others shine brightly as they feed. Supermassive black holes, millions or billions of times the mass of the Sun, sit at the centres of most large galaxies, including ours. They are not formed in the same way as the ones from dead stars, and how they got so big is still an active area of research.

But black holes do not last forever. In the 1970s, the physicist Stephen Hawking showed that quantum effects near the horizon should make black holes slowly lose mass. Pairs of particles can appear near the horizon, and one of them can escape while the other falls in, leaving the black hole a little lighter. This is called Hawking radiation. It's a slow process, and for a stellar black hole it would take far longer than the current age of the universe.

Let's put that number in perspective. The universe is about fourteen billion years old. A black hole with the mass of a star would take something like ten to the power of sixty-seven years to evaporate through this process. That's a one followed by sixty-seven zeros. It's an absurd timescale, and it means that the first black holes formed in the universe are still around, almost certainly. In this sense, the death of a black hole is the slowest event in the cosmos.

Here's the caveat you should hold onto. Hawking radiation has never been detected. It's a prediction from the combination of quantum theory and gravity, and it's one of the most important open questions in physics. There's also a deep puzzle called the information problem: if a black hole evaporates completely, what happens to the information about the matter that fell in? Physicists have many ideas, and none is settled.

So go back to the dark shape with the bright ring. It has a birth, a life of feeding, and a death so slow that it barely fits into thinking. Nothing gets out of the event horizon, and yet the boundary itself is a question we still don't know how to answer. That's what makes black holes so compelling. They are both the simplest and the most mysterious objects we know.
