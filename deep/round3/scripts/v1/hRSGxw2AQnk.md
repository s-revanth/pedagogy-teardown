<<<NARRATION
Rome had never fought a sea battle when it decided to fight Carthage on the sea. Carthage had been running the Mediterranean for centuries. So naturally, Rome built a fleet, and then it built a bridge, and then it lost a lot of ships to the weather. Rome wins this war in the end, and it does it with a boarding bridge, some storms, and a level of stubbornness that would be impressive in any other context.

Last time, we were stuck on islands and in sieges. This time, it's about ships, and why a land power had to learn to swim. Part one left Sicily as the prize. Part two is the story of how Rome tried to take it from the people who had owned the seas for generations.

Quick recap. Carthage and Rome are fighting over Sicily. Rome is very good at armies and very bad at boats. Carthage has centuries of sea power, a network of harbours, and a lot of experienced sailors. On paper, this is a one-sided fight, and that is exactly the assumption that makes it interesting.

Most people assume Rome had no chance at sea. Here's the twist. Rome did not try to out-sail Carthage. It tried to turn a sea battle into a land battle. That's the whole idea of the strategy, and it's a neat piece of thinking, because a Roman soldier knows exactly what to do on land, and the sea is only a problem if you insist on sailing.

So Rome built a fleet. Quickly. The story says the Romans had no ships worth copying until a Carthaginian warship ran aground on the coast, and they used it as a model. This may be a neat bit of legend, but it does the job of showing how quickly Rome was willing to learn. In about two months, a fleet was launched, and it was crewed by men who had never really been at sea. You can picture the training. Rowers practise on dry sand, on wooden benches, in rows, shouting the timing until somebody cries. It's not a navy, but it's a navy's first day.

Now to the crucial invention. The corvus. It's a boarding bridge, essentially a long plank with a spike on the end, dropped onto an enemy ship so that the Roman soldiers can run across it and fight like it's a field. The Carthaginians had the better sailors, but a sea battle usually ended with ships ramming and manoeuvring around each other. The corvus cut the manoeuvring out. Once the bridge was down, the Roman infantry could simply walk on board, and the sea fight became a land fight.

Here's the first big test, at Mylae. The Romans sail out, the Carthaginians come on expecting another round of clever sailing, and the Roman ships drop their bridges. The corvus bites into the Carthaginian decks, and the Roman soldiers pour across like they are marching into a town square. It works. The Carthaginians lose a lot of ships, and Rome gets its first big naval victory. Nobody was more surprised than the Romans.

A few years later came Ecnomus, the largest naval battle anybody had ever fought up to that point. Hundreds of warships were involved, and more men than most cities of the period could put on a field. Rome won again, which gave the Romans a new idea. If they could beat Carthage at sea, why not go to Carthage itself?

So they invaded Africa. The consul Regulus landed near Carthage with an army, and he issued terms the Carthaginians found impossible to accept. Carthage counter-offered, and the negotiations went nowhere. Then the fighting began, and it went badly for Rome. At Adys, Regulus was defeated by a Spartan mercenary commander whom the Carthaginians had hired to lead their army. Regulus was captured, and the invasion was over.

Now a serious note. The most dramatic story about Regulus says that he was sent back to Rome to negotiate an exchange of prisoners, urged his fellow citizens to refuse, and then returned to Carthage to be tortured to death. It's a great story. It is also told by writers who lived long after the events, and the details are not something you can rely on. What is solid is that he was captured, and that his campaign failed. Be careful with the speeches.

On the way home from Africa, the Roman fleet ran into a storm. The ships were heavy, the sea was rough, and the fleet was caught in the open. A large number of ships went down in a single night, and many thousands of men were lost with them. The sea had done what the Carthaginians could not. And the Romans, instead of giving up, started building again.

Think of it like moving a whole house across a lake in a gale. You can be very good at carrying furniture, and the lake will still have an opinion about it. Rome's fleets kept getting caught in that lake, and each time the question was the same. Do we stop building boats, or do we build more?

They built more. The state did not have enough money for a third fleet, so wealthy citizens lent it the funds, and their money paid for another round of warships. This is the kind of commitment that sounds noble until you realise the ships have to float. The new fleet sailed to Sicily, and it had a new commander, Publius Claudius Pulcher, who was not known for patience.

Now, the famous Drepana episode. Before the battle, Claudius had some sacred chickens with him, which were supposed to predict the outcome of a fight by how they fed. The chickens refused to eat. Claudius, who had an answer for everything, said something like, then they can drink. He threw them into the sea. The story is wonderful, and it's also very likely a later embellishment made to make a loss look like a bad joke. But the defeat at Drepana was real. The Carthaginian admiral won, and the Roman fleet was destroyed.

And the storms came back. A second fleet, sent out in a different direction, was lost to bad weather near Sicily. So Rome had now lost multiple fleets to the sea, and a lot of people were starting to wonder whether this war was worth the price. Notice the pattern. Build, lose at sea, rebuild. The sea keeps asking the same question, and Rome keeps giving the same answer.

Meanwhile, on land, Carthage had a very stubborn general of its own. Hamilcar Barca took command in Sicily and seized a mountain fortress called Mount Eryx. From up there he could raid the Italian coast and hold a strong position that was hard to dislodge. He held it for years. Rome kept trying to take him out, and Hamilcar kept not being taken out. Stubbornness works both ways in this war, and it's often the reason the war takes so long.

That brings us to the final battle, at the Aegates Islands. The new Roman fleet, funded by all those loans, faced the Carthaginian fleet off the western coast of Sicily. Rome won. The Carthaginians had been cut off from supplies, the fleet had been weakened, and now the Romans had the sea. It is the same lesson, reversed. The corvus logic had been abandoned, and the Roman fleet won anyway, with plenty of ships and better planning.

Carthage asked for peace. The treaty was simple, if not gentle. Carthage would leave Sicily, pay a large indemnity over ten years, and accept Roman control of the island. Rome had its first province outside Italy, and the first war with Carthage was over.

So back to the hook. Rome fought Carthage at sea, using a boarding bridge, a long string of storms, and an unusual level of stubbornness. It's a hard thing to say about a state that kept losing its fleets, but every time it lost one, it built another. That, more than the bridge, is the real lesson.

Next time, the story moves to a sequel war, and a general who never stopped marching. It will be a very different kind of stubbornness. See you then.
