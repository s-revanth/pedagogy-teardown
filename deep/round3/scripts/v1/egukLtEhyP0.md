<<<NARRATION
I made my first game when I was a kid, and it was terrible. It had a grey box for a character, a floor that was slightly the wrong height, and a score that never meant anything. So what went wrong? I want to tell you how I went from that to making games for a living, and what went wrong on the way. It is not a neat story, which is the point.

The plan is three parts. The first game. The long middle, where most of the learning happened. And the job, which turned out to be less glamorous than I expected.

So, the first game. I made it in a basic tool that let you drag things into a scene and write a few lines of script. The character was a grey box. It could move left and right, and when it touched something, the score went up. That was it. I was very proud of it, and I showed it to everyone who would sit still for thirty seconds.

Looking back, the physics was the problem, although I did not know that at the time. The box did not quite stop when I wanted it to. It slid a bit, or it sank into the floor, and I did not understand why. I tried changing numbers until it felt right. Some of the numbers felt right for a day, and then they did not. I did not know what the numbers meant, so I could not reason about them.

Then came the first real failure. I tried to make a second game with more features, and it went badly. The new version was bigger, and every bug from the first one came with it. The grey box still sank, and now there was also a menu that did not close. I looked at the result and it looked like nothing. I was embarrassed by it, and I quit. I did not touch the project for weeks.

I want to say something about that, because it was the most useful part of the whole decade. I thought the reason I was struggling was that I did not have talent. Other people seemed to just know how to make things, and I did not. That belief was wrong, and it is worth saying clearly. What was actually missing was volume. I had made two projects and stopped, and I had never made the same small thing enough times to get good at it. The people I looked up to had made dozens of bad ones before the good ones.

I did not know that at the time, and I think it matters. When you only see the finished work of people you admire, you assume their early work must have been better than yours. It was not. It was just as bad, and they kept going past the point where it was embarrassing. The difference was not who they were. It was how many times they were willing to look at something unfinished and make it a little less unfinished.

So I restarted. This time I did something dull on purpose. I took a working example, a small game someone else had made, and copied it line by line. Then I read what each line did. Then I changed one thing, and checked what changed. It felt slow, and it was. But I learned more about movement and collisions from that one copied project than from the first two I had made from scratch.

Here is the comparison. On the left is one of the early projects, from that restart period. On the right is a project from a few years later. Both are the same kind of game, with a character, a floor and a goal. The difference is not the art, which is still rough. It is that the later one has fewer surprises. The camera stays where it should. The objects stop where they should. I can tell you why each one behaves the way it does, and I could not do that for the first game.

Then I got a job at a studio. This is where I expected the magic to happen, and it mostly did not. A lot of the work was boring. Fixing the same kind of bug in different places. Reading other people's code. Writing down what a feature was supposed to do before anyone touched it. The skills I was missing were not the flashy ones. They were patience with someone else's messy project, and a habit of checking my own work before I asked anyone to look at it.

The other thing I underestimated was how much of the job is communication. A feature is not finished when the code runs. It is finished when the person who needs it can tell what it does, and when someone else can change it without breaking it. I used to write code that only I could follow, and the first time a colleague asked me a simple question about it, I realised I could not answer without rereading my own work. Writing a short note about what a piece of code is for turned out to be one of the most useful habits I picked up.

There was also a lot of waiting. Builds took a long time, tests ran slowly, and sometimes an entire afternoon went into a bug that turned out to be a single wrong setting. Those afternoons were frustrating, but they taught me to find the smallest version of a bug first, and to reproduce it before trying to fix it. That habit is what made the rest of the work possible.

So here is the advice I would give, and I want to be clear that it comes from one person's experience. Finish small things. A small game that you finish teaches you more than a huge one you abandon. Copy a working example, then change it, rather than starting from a blank page every time. And keep the bad versions. I still have them, and they are the most honest record of what I learned.

Now the caveat. My memory of these years is imperfect. I have tidied up some of the timeline, and some of the details are probably wrong. This is my path, and it is not a template. Other people got there faster, or by a completely different route. I am telling you what happened to me, and that is all I can vouch for.

So the three lessons, again. Finish the small things. Copy, then change, and read what you copied. And do not throw away the bad versions, because you need them to see how far you have come.

Which brings me back to the grey box. It was ugly, and the floor was the wrong height, and the score did not mean anything. But it was the first thing I ever made that moved when I pressed a key, and that was the moment I understood I could make something at all. Everything after it was just more of the same, done slightly better each time.

Thanks for watching.
