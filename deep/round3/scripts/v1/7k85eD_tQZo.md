## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Why is a rainbow always on the opposite side of the sun from you? Think about that for a second. The answer isn't a bridge of colours sitting in the sky, and there's no rainbow you can walk to. And the same kind of puzzle is hiding in your pocket, in a tangled set of earbuds.

Here's the plan. We'll explain the rainbow with one glass of water, then take the same idea to a problem that looks unrelated: why cords knot.

Start with the sun. Its light looks white, but it's really a mix of colours, and in air every colour travels at the same speed. When light enters a drop of water, it slows down, and different colours slow down by slightly different amounts, so they bend by slightly different amounts. The bending is called refraction, and the splitting of colours is called dispersion.

Here's a toy you can build at home. Put a glass of water in a sunny window, and shine a thin beam through it onto a white card. You'll see a spread of colours. Red bends the least, violet bends the most. The glass isn't adding colour. It's sorting the light that was already there.

Now a raindrop is a tiny sphere of water. Sunlight enters it, bounces off the back inside surface, and comes out again. That single bounce sends most of the light out in one direction. For the main rainbow, that direction is about forty-two degrees from the line running from the sun through your head. Every drop at that angle sends red to you, and every drop at a slightly different angle sends a different colour. That's why the rainbow is a band, and why it always sits at the same angle from the sun.

This gives us a misconception worth breaking. People picture a rainbow as a physical arc out there, and they think if they drive far enough they'll reach its end. But a rainbow is a pattern in your eyes. The drops that send you red are not the same drops that send your friend red, a few feet away. The arc moves when you move. Every viewer has their own.

Keep in mind that this is the simplified picture. It ignores the second bounce that gives you a fainter, reversed outer rainbow, and it ignores the fact that drops come in different sizes. Those details matter for the full picture, but the main idea holds: the angle is set by the physics of a single bounce.

Now the headphones. You put your earbuds in your pocket in a neat loop, and you take them out a few minutes later as a knot. Nobody tied it. It's a physical process, and it has a clear mechanism.

Think of a cord as a long rope with some stiffness and some friction against itself. When you pull it out and shake it, the bends move around at random. Any time a loop slips over another loop, it can tighten into a knot, and once a knot forms it's hard to undo, because the cord's own friction holds it in place.

Here's an analogy. Imagine tossing a long spaghetti strand into a bowl and shaking the bowl. Some strands land straight, others land in loops. The loops are more likely when the strand is longer and more flexible. Each shake is a random step, and each step can add a twist or a loop. Over many shakes, the chance of a knot grows.

The interesting part is what makes a cord knot less. A stiffer cord has fewer tight bends, so it has fewer chances to loop. A cord kept in a loose coil, with the loops laid flat, avoids the random twists that make knots. That's why the standard advice of wrapping a cord loosely around your hand, instead of stuffing it, actually works. It's not magic. You're limiting the random motion.

The same physics shows up in a rope on a boat, a garden hose, a cable on a truck, and even the long molecules inside living cells. Long, flexible things that move at random tend to tangle, and the knot is an accident of motion, not a design.

Here's the caveat. Tangling research uses simplified cords and controlled shakes, and real pockets are messy. The physics explains the tendency; it doesn't predict the exact knot. So don't expect one rule to fix every tangle.

So let's go back to the start. Why is a rainbow opposite the sun? Because drops send light back at a fixed angle, and that angle is set by how water bends light. Why do cords knot? Because random motion and friction turn loops into knots. Two different puzzles, and both come from the same shape: a bend that repeats until the physics settles into a pattern.

Thanks for watching. Go untangle something.
