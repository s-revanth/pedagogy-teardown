<<<NARRATION
You are driving across a country you have never seen before, and a green sign flashes past. It says I-84. You have never driven this road. You have never looked at a map of this place. And yet, from that one number, you can tell roughly which way the road runs and roughly where it sits in relation to the rest of the country. How?

Most people assume the number is just a name, like a person's name. Somebody decided, long ago, that this road would be called eighty-four, and the number is an arbitrary label, a bit like a phone number or a serial number. That is a reasonable first guess. It is also wrong, and the way it is wrong is useful.

Here is the model. It has three rules, and every one of them is a rule about position.

Rule one: odd numbers run north and south. Even numbers run east and west. So if the number is odd, the road is roughly vertical on a map, and if it is even, it is roughly horizontal.

Rule two: the numbers increase as you move across the grid. On an east-west road, the numbers rise as you go north. On a north-south road, the numbers rise as you go east. So low numbers sit in the south and the west, and high numbers in the north and the east.

Rule three: three-digit numbers are a different kind of road. Their last two digits point to the main route they connect to. If the first digit is odd, the road is a spur that runs into a city from a main route. If the first digit is even, the road is a loop that goes around a city, a bypass or a beltway.

Now a toy grid, so the rules can run. Draw a box with four roads: a low even number at the bottom, a high even number at the top, a low odd number on the left, and a high odd number on the right. Put a name in each corner. Now any new road with a number lands somewhere on that box. An even number between the two horizontal roads sits somewhere between the south and the north. An odd number near the left edge sits somewhere in the west. You have not seen the road, but you already know where to look for it.

This is the step where the number stops being a name and starts being a coordinate. The numbering is a grid, and each route is a point on it, with its position encoded in its number. The sign is not telling you what the road is called. It is telling you where it sits in a system.

Let's check the rules against the real map. The long north-south roads run along the coasts. The road on the west coast carries a low number, and the one on the east coast carries a high number, which is the rule two pattern exactly. The long east-west roads go across the country. The one at the southern edge carries a low even number, and the ones further north carry higher even numbers. Numbers climb as you go up the map. The grid matches the country, not by accident but by design.

So the number on the sign tells you two things at once. It tells you the orientation of the road, which is the odd and even part. It tells you its position relative to the rest of the grid, which is the size of the number. Read them together and the sign becomes a rough map reference.

Now the three-digit rule, which is where people get tripped up. Take a road with a three-digit number and look at the last two digits first. Those are the main route. If the road ends the same way as a main route, you know this road is tied to that one. Then look at the first digit. An odd first digit means a spur into a city, a route that leaves the main line and runs toward a particular destination. An even first digit means a loop, a road that goes around a city so traffic can avoid it. One rule, applied to a family of roads, tells you what kind of road each one is without a map.

Now a story about how it got that way. The national network of high-speed roads was planned and built in the middle of the twentieth century. Planners needed a numbering scheme that a driver could read at speed, without a map, from a passing sign. The odd and even split and the increasing sequence were the answer. The planners were making a practical choice, and the practical choice turned out to make the numbers carry geography.

I should say what the rules do not do. The scheme applies to the interstate highway system, the big limited-access roads, and not to every road in the country. Other numbered highways and state routes follow their own conventions, some similar in spirit and some not. Also, the rules are a guide, not a law of nature. Some routes break the pattern because of how the network grew over time, or because a route was rerouted or extended after the numbers were already assigned. When you hit one of those, the rules do not fail. They just tell you that someone made an exception, and you can look it up.

So the pattern is strong, but it has edges. Keep that in mind, because it is the same kind of caveat every good model needs. The model predicts most cases well, and it tells you where the exceptions are likely to be.

The same idea shows up in other grids. City street systems, where avenues run one way and streets run the other, let a visitor work out where they are without a map. A grid system makes location cheap to read, and a number system that encodes location is just a grid written in digits.

Now try it yourself. The next time you pass a sign with a number on it, take a second and read it as a map reference. Odd or even tells you the direction. The size of the number tells you where it sits. A three-digit number tells you whether it is a loop or a spur, and what it connects to.

Back to the sign you saw at the start. I-84. You still have not seen that road. But you now know it runs east and west, that it sits somewhere between the low numbers in the south and the high numbers in the north, and that it is a main route rather than a spur or a loop. The number was never just a name. It was the coordinates, and you had been reading them the whole time.
