<<<NARRATION
Two countries go to war over a group of islands that has more sheep than people. That's the premise, and it is an honest one. This is the Falklands, a cold, windy patch of the South Atlantic, and it started a war in 1982 that lasted about ten weeks. It began with a flag, and we'll come back to that flag, because it is the best piece of set dressing in the whole conflict.

Here's the one-line chain. A claim, a flag, an invasion, a long trip home, and a surrender. Keep it in mind, because the whole video is a series of small decisions that turned into a big one.

The islands are cold and windy. There is peat, there are penguins, there are a lot of sheep, and there are roughly two thousand residents, most of them British in background and proud of it. Think of it as a small farming community at the end of the world. Nobody there is planning an empire. The most dangerous thing on the islands is probably the weather.

Argentina has claimed the islands since the nineteenth century, and it calls them the Malvinas. Britain has run them as a colony for a long time, and it has no intention of giving them up. The two countries argued about it for generations, in diplomatic notes, in speeches, and in the occasional stern letter. For most of that time, the dispute was a long, low sore. Nobody was bleeding from it, but nobody could stop scratching it either.

In March 1982, a group of scrap-metal workers landed on South Georgia, a remote island to the east, without the proper paperwork. They took down a British flag on one site and raised an Argentine one. It sounds like a comedy sketch, and to some degree it was, except that a comedy sketch with navy ships involved has a way of getting serious. The British sent a ship to remove them, and the crisis began to escalate.

The junta in Buenos Aires was in serious trouble. The economy was in a mess, inflation was high, and the military government had a human rights record that many people at home and abroad were uneasy about. Here's the misconception most people carry around. They assume the invasion was simply about the islands. It was, but it was also about the government. A rally around the flag is a very old trick. If the people are angry at you, find a foreign enemy and remind them that they are on the same side.

On 2 April 1982, Argentine forces landed near Port Stanley, the capital. Picture this as a skit. A small detachment of Royal Marines is guarding a few buildings. The governor, in a government house with a view of the harbour, has very few options. There are more Argentine soldiers in the streets than there are defenders, and the defenders are told to lay down their arms. The Argentine flag goes up over the town. For the junta, this is a moment of triumph. For the islanders, it is the start of a very bad spring.

Britain's response was the part nobody in Buenos Aires had really planned for. A task force was assembled, with aircraft carriers, troop ships, and a large number of warships, and it sailed about eight thousand miles to reach the islands. That is a long way to send a fleet, and it meant the British had weeks of sailing to think about it. The United Nations called for an Argentine withdrawal. Diplomacy tried to do its work, and for a while it seemed like it might.

Diplomacy did not win. In April, British forces recaptured South Georgia, which is a clean and important win, and then the fighting moved to the main islands. The war was fought in cold conditions, at sea and on land, with aircraft, missiles and ships all in play. And that is where the costs started to climb.

The sinkings are the wall. In early May, a British submarine sank the Argentine cruiser General Belgrano, with a very heavy loss of life, and the decision is still debated by historians. Days later, an Argentine aircraft fired a missile that hit HMS Sheffield, and the Royal Navy lost a destroyer that sank a few days later. The war was no longer a quick diplomatic argument. It was a real war with real ships going down, and both governments had to explain that to their people.

Now a caveat, because this is a short video and some figures will be quoted in many different ways. Casualty totals are approximate, and they vary by source. The number of dead on each side is well documented in broad strokes, but the exact breakdown for each incident and each battle depends on the record you read. The Belgrano decision is particularly contested, and I'd treat any single explanation of it with care.

The land battles that followed were hard, cold, and muddy. British forces came ashore, pushed across the islands in stages, and fought through a series of engagements. The Argentine garrison was large, but it was poorly supplied, badly positioned, and not well prepared for a winter fight. In June, the Argentine commander surrendered at Port Stanley. The war was over, and the islands stayed British.

Now, the flag. Remember the scrap-metal workers at South Georgia, the flag they raised, and the crisis they set off? That small, almost absurd moment turned into a war that cost lives, careers and governments. The flag is the bookend of the whole story. The junta had hoped the flag would rally the country. Instead, the defeat finished it. Galtieri resigned, the regime collapsed, and the democratic government that followed would spend years dealing with what the junta had done.

And the sheep? They are fine. The islanders went back to farming and fishing, and the islands went on being the same cold, windy place they had always been, with a lot of new memories and a few new graves. That is the odd thing about small places in big wars. The territory barely changes, and the people who live there carry the weight.

Next time, we'll look at another mini-war, and this time the leader in charge genuinely believed nobody would notice. Spoiler: a lot of people noticed. See you then.
