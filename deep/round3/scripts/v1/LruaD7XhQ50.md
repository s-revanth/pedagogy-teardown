<<<NARRATION
Alaska is enormous. Its shape fills a corner of every world map, and its size is one of the first things people learn about the United States. So here is a claim that sounds wrong on first hearing. The federal government owns more land than Alaska does. It is not a joke, and it is not a trick about the word own. It is a fact about the country's land, and the rules that produce it are simpler than you would expect.

Most people assume federal land is a rounding error. They picture a capital district, some military bases, a few national parks, and then private property filling everything else. That picture feels right because most of the country you have seen is private, or city, or state. The belief is not stupid. It just leaves out where most of the land actually is, and how it got there.

So here is the model. It has three rules, and each one is about history, not geography.

Rule one: the federal government got much of its land by buying it, by treaty, or by taking over claims from the states. Land acquired this way did not start as a set of private farms. It started as a large, unsettled public block.

Rule two: much of that land was never sold off. The government could have sold it to private owners, and sometimes did, but in large parts of the West the land was dry, mountainous, or otherwise hard to farm, so buyers were few and the government kept it.

Rule three: once the government owns the land, it has to manage it, and it manages it for many uses. Forests for timber and recreation, rangeland for grazing, parks for preservation, and military training grounds. A federal acre is not one thing. It is an inventory the government administers.

Now a toy version, to see how the rules play out. Imagine a country with five states. Four are old and fully settled, with almost all the land sold to private owners, and one is huge and mostly unsettled. The federal government gets the big one as a block, keeps most of it, and manages it. The four old states have very little federal land, because their land was sold off long ago. Now add more states and more purchases. The total federal share grows, but it grows almost entirely in the places that were settled late, and fastest where the land was least attractive to buyers.

Now the history, because this is where the rules come from. The land that became the federal estate was added in big chunks. In 1803, the Louisiana Purchase brought in a vast middle of the continent. In 1848, the Mexican Cession added a huge stretch of the Southwest and California. In 1853, the Gadsden Purchase filled out a strip of the southern border. In 1867, the United States bought Alaska from Russia. Each purchase came with land the government then had to decide what to do with. Some of it went to settlers. Some was sold. A lot of it stayed in federal hands.

The Homestead Act of 1862 is the important turn in this story. It offered settlers a quarter section, roughly 160 acres, for a small fee and a commitment to live on and farm the land. In the wetter eastern parts of the country that worked reasonably well. In the arid West, it ran into a problem. A geographer named John Wesley Powell argued that 160 acres was far too small to farm in dry country without irrigation, and that a family could not make a living from it without much more land or water. He was right about the arithmetic. The consequence was that much of the West stayed federal, because nobody bought it at the price of eastern farmland. Yellowstone became the first national park in 1872, which was a deliberate choice to keep a place out of private hands rather than a leftover of land nobody wanted.

Now the number. Roughly 640 million acres are federal land. That is around 28 percent of the country. Alaska's land area is about 365 million acres. So the federal estate is larger, by a good margin, than the whole of Alaska's land. The number is large, and it is not spread out evenly. In some states, the federal share is a small fraction of the land. In others, it is most of the state.

Here is the payoff: the uneven distribution is the rule-two result. Nevada is about four-fifths federal. Utah is close to two-thirds federal. Alaska itself is roughly sixty percent federal. The eastern states have a small fraction. The West holds almost all the land the government kept, and that is why the national number looks so large. The government did not go out and buy the whole country. It ended up holding the parts nobody else wanted to buy, and then deliberately reserved some of its own.

I want to flag a caveat about the number itself. Figures like this depend on what you count. Some sources count only land the government owns outright, and others include land held in trust for tribes, land the military uses under leases, or land managed on behalf of the public in other ways. The totals move a little depending on the method. The broad picture does not change: the federal share is large, concentrated in the West, and bigger than Alaska. But if you see a precise figure, check what it includes before you quote it.

Now the same question for other countries. Plenty of nations hold large amounts of land under state or central control, and the reasons vary. Some inherited land from empires. Some kept land that nobody settled because it was too remote or too harsh. Some set aside land for conservation. The three rules are a rough guide for thinking about any of them. Ask how the land came into state hands, why it was never sold, and what the state does with it now. The answers differ, but the structure of the question is the same.

So ask yourself the closing question. If you live in the eastern part of the country, which federal land have you visited? Most people can name a national park, a national forest, or a military site. Count them up. You may be surprised how much of your own country is held in a form you did not expect, because the rules put it there one purchase and one choice at a time.

Back to Alaska. It is still enormous, and it still fills the corner of the map. But the federal government is still, in a very literal sense, the largest landlord in the country, and it holds more land than Alaska does. The rules are not a secret. The purchases, the settlement choices, and the management decisions are all on the record. Read them in order, and the surprising number stops being surprising.
