<<<NARRATION
Quick word from our sponsor first. It's a product that promises to make your week more organised, and we were paid to say so. Now, back to a country that could really have used an organised week.

Petrograd, February 1917. It is bitterly cold, the bread queues are long, and a woman standing in one of them has had enough. Not of the cold. Of waiting. Over the next few weeks, the queue becomes a protest, the protest becomes a strike, and the strike becomes something the Tsar never saw coming, because the soldiers sent to stop it start joining in. Who is in charge when the crowd and the army are on the same side?

Last time, Russia had a tsar with a very big throne and a very big problem. This time the throne is empty, and the question is who gets to sit in it. Spoiler: several people try, and not all of them get to keep the chair.

In late February, the strikes spread. Workers walked out, the soldiers were ordered to shoot them, and some of the soldiers refused. Units mutinied, some joined the crowds, and the capital slipped out of the government's control within a few days. The Tsar, Nicholas the Second, was away at army headquarters. When his advisers begged him to make concessions, he took too long. By early March he had abdicated. A monarchy that had lasted centuries ended with a signature and a train ride.

Now we have two bosses in the same building. One is the Provisional Government, a mix of liberal politicians who want a parliament, elections, and to keep fighting the war alongside Britain and France. The other is the Petrograd Soviet, a council of workers and soldiers elected in the factories and the barracks. Picture two people trying to run one office. The government has the official stamps. The Soviet has the crowds, the troops, and the bread lines. Each one can say no to the other, and nobody can quite say yes.

Here's the myth most people carry around. February was the revolution, and October was the coup. That is too tidy. Nothing about this story was one big day. It was a tug of war that lasted most of the year, and the rope kept changing hands. If you think the whole thing happened in one night in a palace, you've missed the months in between, and the months in between are where most of the real decisions were made.

A soviet, in the simplest sense, is a council. Workers elect delegates, soldiers elect delegates, and the delegates meet and tell everyone what they want. That's the whole definition for now. The rest of the year is about what happens when a council has no army of its own and a government has no crowd of its own, and each side needs the other to be useful, and neither one wants to admit it.

Now to Lenin. He had been living in exile in Switzerland, and when the Tsar fell, the German government, which was at war with Russia, let him travel home across its territory in a sealed train. Take a moment with that image. A revolutionary being carried back to a revolution through the country he was trying to defeat. When he reached Petrograd in April, he laid out a set of ideas and summed them up with a slogan that is easy to remember: peace, land, and bread. Every one of those three things was something the Provisional Government was not delivering. Soldiers were sick of the war, peasants wanted the land, and everybody in the queue wanted bread.

Then the Provisional Government tried a summer offensive against Germany. It failed. Soldiers deserted, the front came apart, and in July the frustrated crowds in Petrograd rose up. The Bolsheviks were blamed, some of their leaders were arrested or forced into hiding, and Lenin escaped by going underground. The government was still in charge, but it had just lost its most useful argument, which was that it was the only thing standing between Russia and chaos.

Then came the Kornilov affair. General Kornilov, the army's commander-in-chief, decided that the government was too weak, and he moved troops toward Petrograd to restore order. The Provisional Government panicked. It needed help, and the fastest help available came from the very people it had spent months trying to contain. The Bolsheviks armed workers, and railway workers slowed the troop trains. The army sent to crush the capital melted away, and the coup fizzled. The Bolsheviks came out of the affair looking like the people who had saved the city.

Here is a skit of how that works. A government asks the revolutionaries for help. The revolutionaries say yes, and hand out rifles. The government thanks them, and then wonders who has the rifles now. That question is the whole of September.

By September, the Bolsheviks had won majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow soviets. Their slogans had worked, and their argument had been tested in public and had held up. Lenin, still hiding, started pushing hard for an uprising. Other Bolsheviks were nervous about it. Lenin was not.

In late October, the Bolsheviks moved. The plan was to seize the key sites: telegraph offices, bridges, railway stations, and the Winter Palace, where the government was meeting. The skit version has an armed crowd storming a grand palace, a famous shot from a cruiser, and a dramatic last stand. The actual version is quieter. The cruiser Aurora fired a blank as a signal, and the palace was taken with far less fighting than the legend suggests. The ministers were arrested in a building that had mostly emptied out around them.

So that famous storming of the Winter Palace? Not a battle in the sense you'd picture. It was the takeover of a building that was barely defended. The image of a giant assault came later, and it got repeated because it looks like a revolution should look. That's the danger of a good picture. It can teach you the wrong thing and feel completely true while doing it.

The Bolsheviks then promised elections for a Constituent Assembly, a national parliament. The elections happened in November, and the Bolsheviks lost. The Socialist Revolutionaries took the largest share of the seats. Lenin's response is a little like losing a club vote and then locking the door. In January the Assembly was dissolved by force, and the Bolsheviks kept the power they had taken.

If it helps, think of a school council vote where you lose, and then the new rule is that the meeting is on a Tuesday at a time you're always busy. It isn't exactly the same, but it has the same flavour. Power that started as a promise of democracy ended up as one party holding the keys.

Then came the peace and the war. In March 1918 the Bolsheviks signed a treaty with Germany at Brest-Litovsk, giving up huge chunks of territory to end the fighting on the eastern front. The price was steep, and their critics called it a betrayal. The real fight came next. A civil war broke out between the Reds, the Bolsheviks and their allies, and a patchwork of White forces, some of them backed by foreign powers that had their own reasons for wanting Russia to fail. The fighting went on for years, and it brought famine and terror to large parts of the country.

In July 1918, the former Tsar and his family were killed in Yekaterinburg. The Bolsheviks announced it in carefully worded statements, and the exact circumstances are still argued over. I want to be honest with you here. Many details of this period come from memoirs, reports, and propaganda. Historians disagree on casualty figures and on who made which decisions. Treat any single number in this video as a rough estimate.

Let's go back to that bread queue. The problem that started the revolution was not solved by the revolution. Bread stayed scarce for years, and the civil war made it worse. The woman in the line in February probably did not imagine that her queue would end with a one-party state. That's the thing about slogans. Peace, land, and bread is a very good slogan. It is a much harder thing to deliver.

Next time, we'll look at the civil war and what came out of it, including the economic policies that were supposed to fix everything, and the people they did not fix. There's a lot more in that story, and most of it is not very cheerful. See you there.
