Here is an equation that looks almost too simple. x cubed equals 15x plus 4. Plug in 4, and you get 64 on the left and 60 plus 4 on the right. It works. The answer is 4. So the question is not whether there is an answer. The question is whether a formula can find it, and what that formula has to do along the way.

Before I show you, I want you to commit to a guess. Can a formula that passes through a square root of a negative number ever give you a real answer like 4? Say yes or no, and say why. I am going to hold off on the answer.

The first thing to notice is that the name is a problem. The numbers we are about to meet are called imaginary, and that sounds like a verdict, as if somebody decided they were fake. But the word is just a label that a mathematician gave them, and the label was meant to be insulting. We will get to that. For now, keep in mind that the name is not the thing.

Let's go back to sixteenth-century Italy. There were no journals then in the modern sense, and mathematicians made their reputations by challenge. You posed a set of problems to a rival, and the rival had to solve yours or lose face, sometimes money, sometimes a teaching post. Solving cubic equations was a prized skill, and the methods were kept secret, because a secret method won duels.

One of these contests happened around 1535, when a mathematician named Tartaglia beat a rival by solving a batch of cubic problems that rival had assumed no one could crack. Tartaglia had a method. He shared it, in confidence, with a physician named Cardano, who had promised not to publish it. Cardano broke that promise in 1545 in a book called Ars Magna, and he did it in his own name, although he gave credit where he felt he had to. Cardano's book also included a method for quartic equations that his student Ferrari had found. That is the background. Now the mathematics.

Here is the cubic formula in its basic form. A cubic with a plain x cubed, a multiple of x, and a constant can be solved by taking the cube root of one expression and adding it to the cube root of a related expression. Each piece has a square root inside, and the two pieces are tied together. I will write it out for our equation, so you can follow along.

Take x cubed equals 15x plus 4, and rearrange it. The formula asks for two numbers that are cube roots of expressions involving 2 and the square root of something. When you put our numbers in, the expression under the square root comes out to minus 121. And the square root of minus 121 is not an ordinary number.

Now let's test it. Take the formula, plug in the numbers, and follow every step. The square root of a negative number has to be dealt with, and the formula seems to be saying the answer involves something impossible. But we already know the answer is 4. The formula has to get to 4. Something has to give.

This is the wall. The answer is clearly a real number, and yet the method seems to demand the square root of a negative, which everyone at the time thought was nonsense. Many mathematicians stopped here. They said the formula gave a useless result, and the cubic was just a problem with no sensible method. Cardano himself was uneasy about the square roots of negatives, and he set the idea aside.

Bombelli, an engineer working in Italy in the 1560s and early 1570s, took a different path. He was willing to treat the strange quantities as if they obeyed the ordinary rules of arithmetic. He wrote them as a plus or minus times the square root of minus one, and he multiplied them the way you would multiply any binomial. His book was published in 1572, and it is where the strange quantities start to look like tools.

Let's check Bombelli's move. Take 2 plus i, where i is the square root of minus one. Cube it. Square it first, and you get 3 plus 4i, because i squared is minus one. Then multiply by 2 plus i once more. You get 6, plus 3i, plus 8i, plus 4 times i squared. The 4i squared is minus 4, which cancels down to 2. The imaginary parts combine to 11i. So the cube of 2 plus i is 2 plus 11i.

So the cube root of 2 plus 11i is 2 plus i. And the cube root of 2 minus 11i, which is the other piece, is 2 minus i. Add them. The i parts cancel, and you are left with 4. The formula, followed honestly, lands on the real answer. The detour through imaginary numbers was not a mistake. It was the road.

Pause for a second on that, because it is strange. The imaginary parts cancel out completely. The only thing left is the real answer. A calculation that goes through a world nobody thinks exists delivers something you can check with your hand.

So what is i? The smallest definition is the one you are already holding. i is a number whose square is minus one. That definition is enough to let you do arithmetic with it. It does not tell you what i is, and for a long time people did not know what to make of it. That is why the name stuck. Descartes called them imaginary, in the sense of not real, and he meant it as a dismissal. Euler used the symbol i in the way we still do, and his identity linking the exponential to a rotation made the numbers feel less like a trick. Later, in the early 1800s, Gauss helped show how to picture them as points on a plane, with the real numbers along one axis and the imaginary numbers along the other.

Here is what that picture buys you. A complex number is a point on the plane, or equivalently an arrow from the origin to that point. Multiplying two of them does two things at once. It multiplies their lengths, and it adds their angles. So multiplying by i is a quarter turn. Multiplying by i twice is a half turn, which takes you to minus one. That is the whole mystery, and it is a simple one: i is the quarter turn.

Once you see the rotation, the numbers stop looking like a trick, and they start appearing everywhere a rotation or a wave appears. In electrical engineering, alternating current is a wave that has a size and a phase. Engineers write the phase as a complex number, and they can add and multiply those numbers to combine circuits, without drawing a sine wave every time. In quantum mechanics, the wavefunction that describes a particle has complex values, and the equations that govern it use i directly.

But I want to be careful. Here is a caveat about the history. The story I have told is a clean one, with a clear line from Tartaglia to Cardano to Bombelli. The actual record is messier. Who first saw what, and who deserves credit for which step, is disputed among historians. Some of the methods had earlier roots, and the attribution is reconstructed from letters and books, not from a single agreed source. So treat the sequence as the broad outline, not a verified timeline down to the year.

With that caveat in mind, look at what the story gives us. The imaginary numbers were not invented to be clever. They were pulled out of a method that worked, when the people using it were willing to ignore the discomfort. The name stuck as an insult, and the tool outlived the insult.

So let's go back to where we started. x cubed equals 15x plus 4. The answer is 4. The only road to it went through a number that does not exist on the number line, and that road gets you to a real answer, which you can check. Imaginary numbers were the detour, and the detour was the only road.

Next time, we follow the same kind of problem into a place where the discomfort is harder to ignore.
