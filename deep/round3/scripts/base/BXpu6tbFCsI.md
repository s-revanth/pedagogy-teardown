<<<NARRATION
Let's start with a question nobody in 1932 expected to hear. What do you do when your enemy is a bird?

Not a clever bird. Not a bird with a plan. Just a big, flightless, very confused emu.

Here's the answer, and I'm telling you now so you can relax. The birds won. Nobody was happy about it, but the birds won.

So what is an emu? Picture an ostrich, but Australian, a little smaller, and with feathers that look like they lost a fight with a hairbrush. Emus can't fly. They can run really fast, though, around thirty miles an hour, and they're native to Australia, which means they were there long before the people who would end up arguing with them.

To understand why anybody would fight them, we have to go back to World War One.

After the war, Australia had a lot of soldiers who came home and needed something to do. The government had a plan. It gave land to returned soldiers, especially out in Western Australia, in places where farming was hard. Think of it as a very optimistic moving-day gift, delivered on land with terrible soil. You got a plot, some tools, and a loan, and the expectation that you would grow wheat and become a success story.

Some of these settlers did well. Many did not. Then came the Great Depression, and wheat prices crashed. A farmer who was barely making it was suddenly making nothing. The margins were thin, the debts were heavy, and everybody was tired.

And then, in 1932, the emus showed up.

Here's the thing about emus. They roam. They move in big groups, following the seasons and the food. After a stretch that made life hard for birds in the interior, a huge number of them, estimates usually land around twenty thousand, started drifting toward the farmland of the Campion district. Not because they had a grudge. Because the crops looked fantastic.

Imagine you've been counting every bushel and every dollar, and a flock of enormous birds wanders through your fields, eating your wheat, leaning on your fences, and the next morning there are more of them. That is not a good morning.

The farmers asked for help. They wanted the birds gone. And the government said, sure, let's send in the army.

I want to pause on that, because it is one of my favourite sentences in all of history. The government sent in the army.

The army sent Major G.P.W. Meredith, a few soldiers, a truck, and two Lewis machine guns. Lewis guns were light machine guns from the First World War, designed to hold back wave after wave of infantry. Now they had a new target: a flock of birds about as tall as a person, moving faster than a sprinting dog, and completely uninterested in the army's schedule.

So the first attack happens. The gunners set up. They open fire. The emus, being smart enough to see a machine gun coming, scatter in every direction. Some of them drop. Most of them, honestly, just keep running. A handful of birds dead, a truckload of ammunition used, and the emus are a few hundred yards away, possibly laughing.

The soldiers regroup. They try again. And this time the emus do something clever. They split up. Instead of one big target, you've got little groups scattering across the field, and each group is harder to hit than a single one. The gunners end up chasing birds across open ground, firing, reloading, and firing again. It is not the stuff of legend. It is a lot of expensive bullets chasing very fast birds.

There's a detail I want you to keep in your head. The gunners were not bad shots. They were using a weapon built for a completely different kind of battlefield, and their target was not doing what targets are supposed to do. Emus don't stand still and wait to be shot. They move, they regroup, and they come back.

Now, the numbers in this story are a bit fuzzy. Different reports give different counts, and nobody was keeping perfect notes while the bullets flew. The figure you'll usually see is just under a thousand birds killed over the whole campaign. That sounds like a victory until you ask how many rounds were fired, how many emus were still out there, and whether the crops got any better. Historians disagree on how precise the story can be, and it's worth being suspicious of anyone who gives you an exact body count with a straight face.

Meanwhile, the people who had asked for help were not thrilled with how it had gone. Every bullet that missed was effectively money that came out of a farmer's pocket, in a year when the farmer had none to spare. Local people were already joking about the campaign, and newspapers reading the reports from the scene were not kind. The army had been brought in to fix a farming problem, and it had turned into a public embarrassment that nobody could quite explain.

So here's where things end up. The campaign was called off, and the army packed up and went home before the year was out. The emus kept doing emu things. The farmers were still dealing with the damage. And the government had learned, in the most expensive way possible, that a machine gun is not an agricultural policy.

And the people making these decisions were not stupid. They were under pressure from every direction. The farmers wanted action, the public wanted results, and the government wanted the whole thing to go away quietly. When you're in that position, a dramatic military response looks like a decision, even when it's really just a way of looking busy.

What's left is a story that gets repeated because it sounds ridiculous, and it is ridiculous. But it's also a story about a government under pressure, farmers in real trouble, and an army asked to solve a problem it was never built for. The birds were the only ones who came out of it looking good.

So the next time someone tells you a war is simple, remember the emus. They didn't have a plan. They didn't have a general. They had numbers, speed, and the good sense to run.

And, spoiler, they're still out there.
