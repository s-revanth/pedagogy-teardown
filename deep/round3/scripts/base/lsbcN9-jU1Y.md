<<<NARRATION
This is a story about one of the most famous underdogs in history. A general from Carthage, a commander who takes an army over the Alps with elephants, and beats the Romans in battle after battle, and still loses the war. Part one covers the origins, the Alps, and the biggest disaster in Roman history, Cannae.

Here's the answer up front. Hannibal wins brilliant battles in Italy, and Rome still refuses to surrender. That's the paradox we're going to unpack. Battles are one thing. Wars are something else.

Quick roadmap. We'll cover the background, the decision to go to Italy, the crossing of the Alps, the big early battles, and then Cannae, which is the moment that should have ended everything.

First, the recap. The First Punic War ended with Carthage losing Sicily, paying a massive indemnity, and facing its own unpaid mercenaries. Carthage is humiliated, broke, and angry. And Rome, in the meantime, is getting bigger, taking more territory, and making more enemies. So you have two powers that do not like each other, and neither one has forgotten the last round.

Carthage's answer to its problems is the Barca family. Hamilcar Barca, a commander from the First Punic War, realises that Carthage needs a new base of power. So he goes to Spain. Spain is rich in silver and resources, and it gives Carthage a way to rebuild its finances and its army. Hamilcar builds a power base there, with his family taking over more and more of the command. He's the engine, and his relatives are the gears.

When Hamilcar dies, his son-in-law takes over, and then Hannibal, Hamilcar's son, takes command in his twenties. Hannibal is young, aggressive, and very focused on one target. Rome.

Now a caveat. There's a famous story that Hannibal, as a boy, swore an oath to hate Rome for the rest of his life, and that his father made him take it. It's a great story, and it's probably a later embellishment, written by people who wanted to explain why Hannibal was so determined. So treat it as a story about how Hannibal was seen, rather than a fact about a childhood moment.

The trigger is Saguntum. Saguntum is a city in Spain that has an alliance with Rome. Hannibal lays siege to it. And after a long and bloody siege, the city falls. The Romans complain, the Carthaginians argue back, and the diplomacy goes nowhere. By 218 BC, Rome declares war. So now you've got a second great war, and Hannibal has a decision to make.

He decides to take the war to Italy. That's the bold move. Carthage has the sea, and Rome has the land. But Hannibal says, why wait for them to come to us? Instead, he marches his army to Italy, through Spain and southern France, and then over the Alps.

Let's pause on the Alps, because this is the moment that makes Hannibal a legend. He takes a huge army, tens of thousands of soldiers, cavalry, and dozens of war elephants. The route is through mountains, snow, and cold, and the local tribes are not friendly. The army loses many men along the way, and many animals. The elephants suffer badly in the cold. And when they finally come down into northern Italy, the army is battered and exhausted, but it's still there.

That's a huge achievement. And it's also a huge gamble. Hannibal is betting that the Gallic tribes in northern Italy will join him, and that Rome will not be able to stop him quickly. That bet mostly pays off. Gallic tribes, who hate Rome, come over to his side. And his army starts winning fights.

The first big battle is at the Trebia river, in the winter of 218 BC. It's cold, the ground is rough, and Hannibal sets up an ambush. He lets the Roman army cross the river in the freezing conditions, and then hits them from the flank and the rear. The Romans are routed, and Hannibal wins a big victory with a relatively small loss.

Now here's a point I want you to keep in your head. Rome isn't just failing in these battles. It's learning. Each defeat is a lesson in how Hannibal fights, and that matters later. The problem is that each lesson costs thousands of lives.

The next big test is Lake Trasimene, in 217 BC. The Roman consul, Gaius Flaminius, is marching his army along a narrow path beside a lake. Hannibal hides his troops in the hills around it. In the early morning, a thick fog rolls over the lake. The Romans march straight into the ambush without realising how bad it is. When the fog lifts, the Romans are trapped. Flaminius is killed. A huge number of Roman soldiers die, and the rest are scattered.

So Rome has lost two major battles in a row. That's a national emergency. And Rome does something unusual. It appoints a dictator, a single leader with emergency powers, and that dictator is Quintus Fabius Maximus.

Fabius takes a completely different approach. He refuses to fight Hannibal in open battle. He shadows the Carthaginian army, cuts off its supplies, harasses its foragers, and waits. He's nicknamed the Delayer, which sounds a bit like an insult, but it's actually a clever strategy. The idea is simple. Hannibal is brilliant in battle, but he's in a foreign country. Keep him moving, keep him hungry, and eventually he'll run out of options.

Think about it like a cat watching a bird that's stuck in a room. You don't rush in. You wait for the bird to get tired, and then it stops being a threat. Fabius is betting that Hannibal's army can't last forever in enemy territory.

But Fabius is not popular. Roman politicians think he's too cautious, and they want a real fight. And the public is angry. So when the next pair of consuls take office, they're determined to crush Hannibal in one decisive battle. That is the setup for the disaster at Cannae.

In 216 BC, the Roman army marches to Cannae, a small town in southern Italy near a river. It's a huge army, with more than eighty thousand soldiers by some estimates, though the figures vary. The Romans are confident. They think they can overwhelm Hannibal with sheer numbers.

Hannibal does something nasty. He arranges his army in a curved line. The center is weak on purpose. The Romans push into the center, and it bends back, and the Romans think they're winning. But the wings of Hannibal's army are strong. Then Hannibal's cavalry sweeps around the flanks and closes the trap. The Romans are surrounded from all sides. There's no escape, and the slaughter goes on for hours.

The result is one of the worst defeats in Roman history. Casualty numbers are huge and disputed, but the common figure is tens of thousands of Romans dead in a single afternoon, and many more captured. It is the kind of loss that should end a war.

And here's a caveat that matters. After Cannae, there's a famous story that Hannibal's cavalry commander urged him to march straight on Rome, and that Hannibal refused, and the delay gave Rome time to recover. Historians argue hard about this. Some think it's a dramatic story made up after the fact. Others think Hannibal simply didn't have the means to take the city. So don't take the marching advice at face value. It's a debate, not a settled fact.

That's where we're going to leave it for now. Hannibal has won the biggest battle in a generation, Rome is staggering, and the question is no longer whether Hannibal can win battles. It's whether he can turn those wins into a victory. And Rome has not given up yet.

Next time, we see what Hannibal does with his victory, and what happens when the Romans start fighting back in a completely different way.

Thanks for watching.
