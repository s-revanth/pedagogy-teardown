## Narration

<<<NARRATION
What was the first photo ever put on the internet? It sounds like a question with a clean answer, the kind you could look up and be done with. But the moment you start digging, the word first starts to wobble. First on what network? First in what format? And first shared by whom? The answer depends on the definition, and that's the interesting part.

Start with the early web. In the early 1990s, the web was mostly text. It was built at CERN, the large physics laboratory near Geneva, where Tim Berners-Lee developed the basic ideas. In 1991 he put up a first website, and it was almost entirely words with links between pages. There were no pictures to speak of. The web was designed to share documents between researchers, not to show off photographs.

That's the setting for the famous image. In 1992, a group of women at CERN, sometimes called the Cernettes, had a photograph taken. It was scanned and put online as a small image file, and it became one of the earliest pictures people point to when they talk about photos on the web. It's small and grainy, but it's one of the few early examples people can still look at today.

Now the complication. Pictures had been shared over computer networks long before 1992. Before the web existed, people were trading images on bulletin board systems, university servers, and early file-sharing setups. Some of those systems were connected to networks that later merged into the internet. So if you define the internet broadly, the first photo may be older than the web, and it may be a picture nobody remembers. If you define it narrowly as the web, you get a different answer. The photo from CERN is first only inside one particular slice of the network.

That's why the question isn't really about pictures. It's about definitions. The word internet covers many systems, and the word photo covers many formats. A scanned print, a digital drawing, a bitmap, a compressed file, a thumbnail embedded in a document. Each of those choices changes the answer. You can't count the first without deciding what you're counting.

Let's look at some other firsts to see the same pattern. Take the first email. In 1971, a programmer sent a message between two computers on an early network, and that message is often credited as the first networked email. But people had been leaving notes for each other on shared machines before that, so the claim depends on the word networked. Or take the first tweet. In 2006, a message went out that said something plain about setting up an account, and it's remembered as the first tweet. It's a lovely piece of history, and it's also a decision about what counts as the beginning of a platform.

Each of these stories has the same structure. There's a clear event, a clear date, and a clear label. But the label quietly carries a definition. First email means the first message between machines, not the first note between people. First tweet means the first post on a particular service, not the first short message anywhere. Once you see that structure, it's hard to unsee it in every history book you read.

There's also a practical problem. Early records are patchy, and nobody logged every image transferred. So when we say something is first, we often mean the earliest one we can document.

So what's the best answer to the question? It depends on what you mean. If you mean the first photo on the web, the 1992 image from CERN is a strong candidate, and it has a great story behind it. If you mean the first picture sent between computers, the answer goes back much earlier, into a messier history where the records are thin. Either way, the honest response is to name the definition before you name the photo.

So which definition of first feels right to you? The web, the network, the format, or the person who pressed send? Pick one, and you'll have an answer. Pick another, and you'll get a different photo, and a different story.
