<<<NARRATION
Last time, we left the First World War in a deadly stalemate. Both sides dug into trenches, nobody could break through, and everybody kept sending more men to try anyway. Now we're in the back half of the war, and this is where it finally ends. Spoiler: the ending is not as clean as anyone wanted.

Here's the answer up front. The war ends in November 1918 with an armistice. But the way it ends sets up the next war, so the ending is really just the beginning of a different problem.

Quick recap of the logic. On the Western Front, the big strategy for most of the war is attrition. You don't win by taking a lot of ground. You win by killing so many of the other side's soldiers that they can't keep going. It's a horrifying plan, but it's the logic that both sides keep returning to.

Now enter 1916. The Germans launch a massive attack at Verdun, a French fortress city. Their idea is to pull the French into a fight they can't afford to lose, and bleed them dry. It works in the sense that the French suffer horribly. But it's a meat grinder for both sides. The fighting goes on for months, the front barely moves, and both armies lose hundreds of thousands of men for almost nothing.

Think about what Verdun meant for the people living through it. Soldiers were told the fortress had to hold at any cost, and so the ground around it became a churned-up landscape where villages disappeared. Men on both sides spent months in shell holes, eating whatever they could carry and sleeping whenever they could. The strategy was supposed to bleed the other side. Instead, it bled both armies, and the front barely moved.

Then comes the Somme. In July 1916, the British start an offensive there, with a huge artillery barrage and an assault on the first morning. The first day alone brings tens of thousands of British casualties, and a very large share of them are dead. The attack gains a tiny amount of ground. The fighting drags on into the autumn, with heavy losses and very little change. If you want a picture of what the war is doing to people, this is it.

Meanwhile, the war at sea is a different kind of problem. Germany's submarines are attacking shipping, including ships carrying American passengers. The United States is trying to stay out of the war. But in early 1917, a telegram from the German foreign minister, known as the Zimmermann telegram, gets intercepted by British intelligence. It proposes that Mexico join the war against the United States, with the promise of land in return. When that becomes public, American opinion turns hard against Germany.

So in April 1917, the United States declares war. It's a big deal, because it brings fresh manpower and money to the Allied side. But it takes time for American troops to reach the front, and in the meantime, the Allies are still stuck. Shipping has to be organised, troops have to be trained, and the new arrivals need to learn a kind of warfare that has already chewed through a generation. So the American entry is a promise of future strength more than an immediate fix, and everyone on the Western Front knows it.

Meanwhile, the home fronts were suffering too. Food was short in Germany, and the British blockade made it worse. Civilians queued for scraps, and the strain on morale was real. War is not only fought in the trenches. It's also fought in kitchens and factories, and by 1917 those were starting to crack.

Then the war changes again, because Russia collapses. In 1917, the Russian Revolution pulls the country out of the war, and the new Bolshevik government makes a separate peace with Germany. The treaty of Brest-Litovsk, signed in early 1918, gives Germany huge territories in the east. For Germany, this means the eastern front is no longer a problem. And for the first time since 1914, Germany can move its troops to the west in large numbers.

So Germany makes a decision. It launches a big spring offensive in 1918, trying to break the Allied lines before American troops arrive in full strength. It works at first. The German army pushes deeper than it has in years. People in Germany start to think victory might be possible.

But there's a catch. Those offensives run out of steam. The soldiers are tired, the supply lines are stretched, and the food situation at home is terrible. The Germans have made gains, but they can't turn them into a decisive breakthrough. And the Allies, now with more American troops and better coordination, start pushing back.

Here's a quick caveat, because the numbers in this war are slippery. Casualty totals depend on how you count the dead, the wounded, the missing, and the prisoners, and different sources add those up differently. So when you see a single figure, ask what it includes. That's true for almost every battle in this part of the story.

From the summer of 1918, the Allies go on the offensive. The so-called Hundred Days is a series of counterattacks that push the Germans back, step by step. German units are worn down. Many soldiers are sick, hungry, or demoralised. The army is still fighting, but it is no longer able to win.

And then the end. On 11 November 1918, at eleven in the morning, the armistice takes effect. The guns stop. The war is over. For many people, it's an enormous relief, and for many others, it's a strange silence after years of noise.

But the war being over does not mean the damage is fixed. The dead aren't coming back. The empires that fought the war are falling apart. Entire regions are in chaos, and the people who signed the armistice are going to face a very hard fight about what comes next.

So that's the end of the war. Next, we'll look at the peace treaty, and why, for a lot of people, it did not settle anything. The bill for this war doesn't stop when the shooting does.

Thanks for watching.
