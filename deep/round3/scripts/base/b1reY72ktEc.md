## Content skeleton
- Recap: the Tsar's fall in February 1917 and the uneasy split between the Provisional Government and the Petrograd Soviet.
- Lenin returns to Russia in April 1917 by sealed train through Germany and pushes the "peace, land and bread" line.
- The July Days and the Kornilov affair weaken the Provisional Government and arm the Bolsheviks' supporters.
- The October seizure of power in Petrograd, mostly quick and lightly fought, followed by the Constituent Assembly being shut down.

- War Communism, grain requisitioning, famine, the Kronstadt revolt, and the retreat of the New Economic Policy.
- The Soviet Union formed in 1922, and the irony that the revolution for the people produced a one-party state.

<<<NARRATION
Last time, the Tsar got out of the way, and nobody was really sure who was in charge. Let's get into the part where that question gets answered, violently.

Here's the big answer up front, so you can see where we're going. In late 1917, a small party called the Bolsheviks takes power in Petrograd. Then they spend the next few years fighting a civil war to keep it. And by the early 1920s, they've built a state that's going to last for decades. But it was not inevitable. At almost every step, it looked like they would lose.

Quick recap. In February 1917, the Tsar abdicates. A Provisional Government takes over the official job. But at the same time, there's the Petrograd Soviet, a council of workers and soldiers with a lot of street power and a lot of opinions. So you have two governments, both sort of claiming to be in charge, and neither one able to solve the big problems. Russia is still in a massive war. People are hungry. Nobody trusts anybody.

Now enter Vladimir Lenin.

Lenin has been living in exile in Switzerland. And in April 1917, the Germans, who would very much like Russia to fall apart, let him cross their territory in a sealed train. Think about that for a second. The enemy government gives a revolutionary a ride home, because they think his chaos will hurt Russia. Lenin gets off in Petrograd and starts talking.

What's he saying? Three promises, which you can keep in your head as peace, land, and bread. Peace, meaning get out of the war. Land, meaning give the peasants the estates. Bread, meaning feed people who are starving. It's a great slogan, because every single person in the country wants all three. And the Bolsheviks become the party that says it loudest.

Let's define one term, because it matters. A soviet is a council. It's a group of elected representatives from factories and army units. The Bolsheviks want power to go to the soviets, not to the Provisional Government. That sounds nice and democratic. The question is what happens when the soviets are controlled by whoever is shouting the loudest.

Lenin's big document from that spring is called the April Theses. It basically says the Provisional Government is not worth supporting, and the war should end, and all power should go to the soviets. A lot of people thought he had lost his mind. The party was tiny at that point, and most socialists thought Lenin was getting ahead of himself.

But the war stays grinding, and the summer offensive goes badly. In July, there's an uprising in Petrograd called the July Days. Soldiers and workers take to the streets, and some Bolsheviks join in. It fails. The government cracks down, and many Bolshevik leaders have to run or hide. Lenin himself goes underground for a while. For a moment, it looks like the whole thing is finished.

And then something weird happens. In August, a general named Kornilov decides to march on Petrograd, supposedly to restore order and to crush the soldiers' councils. The Provisional Government, the very people who had been fighting the Bolsheviks, freaks out. And they need help. So they turn to the Bolsheviks and the Red Guards, the armed workers' militias, and tell them to stop the general. The Bolsheviks do. The general's troops melt away. And suddenly the Bolsheviks are the ones with guns, and the government looks weak.

This is the moment where the story turns. The Bolsheviks gain members quickly. The soviets start voting for them. And Lenin, back from hiding, starts pushing hard for an armed takeover.

Now, the October Revolution. This is the one people picture. A famous scene where a huge crowd storms the Winter Palace. The reality is much less cinematic. The Bolsheviks seize key points in the city: bridges, telephone exchanges, train stations, and government buildings. The Provisional Government's guards are few, and the palace falls with surprisingly little fighting. The big storming scene you've seen in movies was later recreated for the cameras. So it looks like an epic storming, and it was really more like a coup done by a small group of organised people with a lot of guns.

Here's the part I want you to keep in mind. The Bolsheviks were a minority. Most Russians were not voting for them. In fact, when the Constituent Assembly elections happen in November, the Bolsheviks lose badly. Most people vote for the Socialist Revolutionaries, a rival party with a lot of rural support.

So what do the Bolsheviks do? They shut the Assembly down in January 1918. They say the old system is over and the soviets are the real voice of the people. From the inside, they say it's democracy. From the outside, it looks like a party that just decided to stop counting votes it didn't like.

Next, the peace. Lenin promised to get Russia out of the war, and he does, but the price is enormous. The treaty of Brest-Litovsk, signed in March 1918, gives Germany huge chunks of western territory, including Ukraine, Poland, and the Baltics. It is a humiliating deal, and a lot of Bolsheviks hate it. But Lenin argues that they need the breathing room, and they take it.

And then the country falls into civil war. The Reds, the Bolsheviks, are fighting against the Whites, a messy coalition of monarchists, liberals, and anti-Bolshevik socialists. Foreign armies pile in too, from Britain, France, the United States, Japan, and others, supporting the Whites or just trying to make sure the Bolsheviks don't spread the revolution. It's a huge, brutal war. It has fronts everywhere. It has armies that switch sides. It has a lot of local fighting that doesn't really fit into one clean story.

Sources on the total casualties are patchy and disputed, so I'm going to be careful with numbers here. What's clear is that the war is savage on all sides, and the Bolsheviks respond with their own terror. The secret police, the Cheka, get started in December 1917, and they become a tool for crushing opponents, real and imagined. The Red Terror is the name for this campaign of arrests and executions. Lenin's government says it's necessary. Many people in the countryside, and in the cities, just experience it as fear.

The Tsar and his family are killed in July 1918 in Yekaterinburg. The circumstances and the exact orders are still argued over, so I'd treat any tidy version of that story with a bit of suspicion.

Meanwhile, the Bolsheviks need to feed the Red Army and the cities. So they adopt a policy called War Communism. In practice, it means the state takes grain from the countryside by force, and it runs industry through central control. Peasants who are told to hand over their grain often hand over nothing, and the result is a collapse in production and then a terrible famine. Millions suffer, especially along the Volga.

By early 1921, things are so bad that sailors at Kronstadt, a naval base that had once been a Bolshevik stronghold, revolt. They want free elections and an end to the requisitioning. The Bolsheviks crush the revolt. It is one of the most painful ironies of the whole story. The people who had helped put the Bolsheviks in power are now the ones the Bolsheviks shoot.

That same year, Lenin retreats. He introduces the New Economic Policy, which allows some private trade and small businesses again. Think of it as a tactical retreat. The party is still in charge of politics, but the economy gets some breathing room.

The Reds win the civil war. By 1922, the fighting is mostly over, and the Soviet Union is formed at the end of that year as a union of republics. Officially, it's a federation of equal peoples. In practice, it is one party, with its own internal hierarchy, controlling almost everything.

So here's the payoff. A revolution started in the name of workers and peasants. Along the way it became a civil war, a famine, a secret police state, and a one-party government. None of that was the plan on paper. But every choice along the way made the next one easier.

Next time, Lenin is gone, and the question of who takes over is the big one. Spoiler: it is not who most people expected. Thanks for watching.
