# Narration

<<<NARRATION
Nothing escapes a black hole. Not dust, not stars, not even light. That sentence sounds like science fiction, but it is one of the most solid facts in physics. And it raises a question that has fascinated people for more than two centuries. What happens to a thing once it crosses the line of no return?

To answer that, let's follow a black hole through its life. It is born, it lives and eventually, it dies. Along the way, it will stretch our sense of space and time.

Start with what a black hole is. Every object with mass pulls on everything around it, and to escape its pull you need to move fast. The speed you need is called the escape velocity. On Earth, it is about eleven kilometers per second. Now imagine squeezing an object more and more until its escape velocity reaches the speed of light. The boundary where that happens is called the event horizon. Inside it, nothing can get back out. Not because the black hole grabs and holds, but because there is no outward direction that leads anywhere, at least not one that stays outside the horizon.

How do you get something that dense? Nature does it through death. A star like the Sun spends most of its life fusing hydrogen into helium. That fusion pushes outward, and gravity pushes inward, and the two balance. When the fuel runs out, the balance breaks. A very massive star, many times heavier than the Sun, collapses in on itself. Its core crushes down into an object so dense that, if the Sun were squeezed to that size, it would fit inside a city. The outer layers blow off in a supernova, and what remains can be a black hole.

These are called stellar black holes. The Sun, squeezed to the point where it becomes a black hole, would have a horizon only about three kilometers across. A black hole with the mass of the Sun is not a giant hole in space. It is a very compact object, with a very strong grip.

Now, the common misconception: black holes are not cosmic vacuum cleaners. From far away, a black hole pulls on things exactly the way the star that formed it did. If the Sun were replaced by a black hole of the same mass, Earth would keep orbiting it without any change. The difference appears only when you get close.
Imagine a clock falling toward a black hole, and an observer far away watching it. To the observer, the clock seems to slow down as it approaches the horizon, and its light becomes redder and fainter. It never quite seems to reach the boundary. Meanwhile, for the clock itself, nothing special happens as it crosses.
For small black holes, there is another effect. The gravity is so strong that the pull on your feet can be much stronger than the pull on your head. The result is a stretching force, sometimes called spaghettification. For a star-sized black hole, that stretch would tear you apart long before you reached the horizon. For giant black holes, the tidal forces at the horizon are weaker, and a traveler could cross it without being stretched at all.
Then there is life. Black holes grow. A stellar black hole can feed on a companion star, drawing material into a glowing disk that heats up and shines brightly in X-rays. Two black holes can also spiral together and merge, releasing ripples in spacetime called gravitational waves. Detectors on Earth first measured those ripples in 2015.

The largest black holes are in the centers of galaxies. These supermassive black holes can contain millions or billions of times the mass of the Sun. Our own galaxy has one at its center, with a mass of several million suns. In 2019, a global network of radio telescopes produced the first image of a black hole's shadow, the dark region surrounded by glowing gas, in a galaxy many millions of light-years away.
Now for death. In the 1970s, the physicist Stephen Hawking showed that black holes are not perfectly black. Quantum effects near the horizon cause them to slowly emit radiation, now called Hawking radiation. Over time, the black hole loses mass and shrinks. The rate is extremely slow for big black holes. A black hole with the mass of the Sun would take far longer than the current age of the universe to evaporate. So the death of a black hole is a very long goodbye.

And here is a deep puzzle. When a black hole evaporates, what happens to the information about everything that fell in? Quantum physics says information cannot simply vanish. General relativity seems to say it does. This is called the information paradox, and physicists still have not settled it.
So what does the far future look like? Stars will burn out, one by one, over trillions of years. Some galaxies will merge, and many of their stars will drift away into the dark. In that very distant era, black holes may be among the last large objects left in the universe. They will hold on, quietly, for a remarkably long time, before fading into the faint glow of radiation.

So, back to where we started. Nothing escapes a black hole. That is true, as long as the black hole exists. But even a black hole is not forever. It is born from a dying star, it grows by eating and merging, and it fades through the strange glow of quantum physics. Its birth, its life and its end are all part of the same story.
NARRATION>>>
