<<<NARRATION
If you drop a hammer and a feather at the same time, which one hits the ground first? Most people say the hammer, and in air they're right. But that's not the whole answer, and the reason is the first thing most people get wrong about gravity. Here's a hint: it isn't really about weight. Let's find out what gravity actually does.

Here's the route. We'll clear up the old idea that heavier things fall faster, follow Galileo's approach, see how Newton's law makes the answer clean, look at why weight is really a push from the ground, and then meet Einstein's picture, along with the places where these ideas run out.

Start with the old idea. For nearly two thousand years, the standard view, going back to Aristotle, was that heavier objects fall faster in proportion to their weight. It sounds sensible. A stone falls quicker than a leaf. But air explains most of that difference. A leaf catches the air and floats down. Remove the air, and the story changes.

Galileo challenged the idea in the late 1500s and early 1600s. He rolled balls down gentle slopes, which slowed the motion enough to measure with the tools of his day. He concluded that objects of different weights speed up at the same rate, once air resistance is set aside. A popular story says he dropped balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa to prove it. That story is probably not true, and it first appears in a biography written long after his death. But the reasoning he used is real, and that's what matters.

The clearest test came on the Moon. In 1971, during the Apollo 15 mission, the astronaut David Scott stood on the lunar surface, held up a hammer and a feather, and let them go together. With no air to slow the feather, they hit the dust at the same moment. It's one of the most vivid demonstrations in the history of physics, and it shows that gravity treats different masses the same way.

Now the maths, because it makes the reason clear. Newton said that force equals mass times acceleration. He also said that the pull of gravity between two objects is proportional to their masses, and falls off with the square of the distance between them. For an object falling toward Earth, the gravitational force is Earth's mass times the object's mass, divided by the distance squared, times a constant. Set the two expressions equal. The falling object's mass appears on both sides, and it cancels. What's left is an acceleration that depends on Earth's mass and the distance, and not on the object at all. Every object accelerates the same way, about nine point eight metres per second squared at the surface. The feather and the hammer each follow the same rule, and their masses drop out.

Here's the next piece, and people often get it backward. Why does weight exist at all? When you stand on a floor, gravity pulls you down, but you don't fall through. The floor pushes back up with an equal force. Your weight is the pull you feel as the floor resists it. When you sit in a chair, the seat holds you up, and you feel that push. That's also why, in free fall, you feel weightless. Nothing pushes back, so you don't feel the pull. The gravity is still there. The sensation depends on the push.

Let's test that with a thought experiment. Picture a lift in a tall building, with its cable cut. As the lift falls, everything inside falls at the same rate. A coin you're holding seems to float beside you. A ball you release doesn't drift toward the floor. Inside that falling box, you can't tell whether you're floating in deep space or falling in a gravitational field. Einstein built one of his foundations on this observation. In a sense, gravity is locally the same as being in free fall.

That observation led Einstein to a different picture. In 1915 he proposed that gravity isn't a force reaching across space. It's a property of space and time themselves. Massive objects curve spacetime, and objects moving freely follow the straightest possible paths through that curved geometry. The Earth isn't reaching out to grab the Moon. The Moon is following the path that the Earth's mass carves out.

Why does that matter beyond textbooks? One test was the orbit of Mercury. The point of Mercury's closest approach to the Sun slowly shifts, and Newtonian gravity, with all the planets included, leaves a small gap that the calculations couldn't close. Einstein's equations accounted for that gap, about forty-three arcseconds per century. It was one of the first striking confirmations of his theory.

Now a warning about the picture most people use. You've probably seen a rubber sheet with a heavy ball on it, and a marble rolling toward the ball. It's a helpful image, but it's misleading. The sheet sags because gravity pulls it down, so the analogy uses gravity to explain gravity. The picture also hides that space and time are curved together. It's a sketch, not a model. Use it to build intuition, then put it down.

The same ideas matter in everyday technology. GPS satellites orbit high enough that their clocks run slightly faster than clocks on the ground. Relativity predicts that, and the net effect is about thirty-eight microseconds a day. Without correcting for it, a position calculation would drift by roughly ten kilometres in a day. Every GPS receiver is quietly relying on general relativity.

Now the limits. General relativity works extremely well for planets, stars and galaxies. But it runs into trouble inside a black hole, where the mathematics predicts points of infinite density. It also doesn't fit with quantum mechanics, the theory of the very small, and physicists have not yet worked out how the two should be joined. So gravity is very well described across huge ranges of scale, and there are still open questions at the extremes.

So go back to the hammer and the feather. They hit the ground together, because gravity gives every object the same acceleration regardless of its mass, and the masses cancel out of the equation. What we feel as weight is the push of the ground, not the pull of gravity itself. And what we call gravity is, in Einstein's picture, the shape of space and time. The next time something falls, ask whether the weight is what matters, or the fall itself.
