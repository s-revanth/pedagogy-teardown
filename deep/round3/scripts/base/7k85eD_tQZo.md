## Narration

<<<NARRATION
The rain stops, the sun comes out, and suddenly there's a band of color hanging in the sky. Why does a rainbow look like an arc, and why do the colors always come in the same order? And while we're at it, why are the earbuds in your pocket always a knot? It turns out both questions involve the same kind of tangle, one made of simple rules repeated many times.

Start with the rainbow, since it's the more beautiful puzzle. The short answer is this. Every raindrop sends sunlight back to your eye at a fixed angle, about forty-two degrees measured from the point directly opposite the sun. Only the drops at that angle are sending light your way, so they line up into a ring around that point. You see an arc because the ground blocks the rest of the ring.

Let's build that answer from the inside of a single drop. Sunlight enters the drop and bends, because light slows down in water. That bending is called refraction. Then the light reaches the back of the drop and reflects off the inside surface. Then it bends again as it leaves. Two bends and one internal reflection. Along the way the light also gets split into colors, because different wavelengths bend by slightly different amounts. Red bends the least, and violet bends the most. That split is called dispersion, and it's what turns white sunlight into a spectrum.

Picture one drop as a tiny glass ball. Shine a white beam into it, and the light fans out into colors, with each color leaving at its own angle. Red leaves at about forty-two degrees. Violet leaves at a slightly smaller angle, around forty. So from where you stand, red sits on the outside of the bow and violet sits on the inside. That single fact is why the order never changes. It's also why the rainbow sits opposite the sun. The anti-solar point moves as the sun moves, so a low sun gives a high rainbow and a high sun gives a low one.

Now you can see why the rainbow is a circle, not a flat stripe. Each drop only sends light at one particular angle to you. Drops higher or lower in the sky are sending their light at other angles, so their light doesn't reach your eye in this direction. Only the drops at the right angle fill in the ring. Turn around, and a different set of drops would make a ring around the same point. The rainbow is really a cone of light, and the ground cuts it into an arc.

Sometimes you get a second rainbow above the first. That one comes from light that bounces twice inside each drop. It leaves at a bigger angle, around fifty-one degrees, and the colors are reversed, with red on the inside. The dark band between the two bows is where light from raindrops doesn't reach you at all. It's a useful reminder that the rainbow isn't painted on the sky. It's a particular set of drops at a particular angle, and nothing else.

Now the headphones. You pull them out of your pocket, and they're a knotted mess. You didn't tangle them on purpose, but the physics isn't mysterious. A cord is long, flexible and thin, and it has to be wound, unwound, and dropped into a bag over and over. Every time you wind it, you put a small twist into it. Twist builds up along the cord, and when the cord slackens, that stored twist can turn into loops. A cord behaves a bit like a rope that's been pulled and released many times, storing twist until it kinks.

It helps to think about a random walk. Imagine each short piece of cord turning in a slightly random direction as you handle it. Over a long length, the cord takes a jumbled path, crossing over itself many times. Once it has crossed itself, the loops can catch on each other, and a simple tug can pull them into a tighter knot. The longer and thinner the cord, the more chances it has to cross itself. That's why earbuds, which are long and thin, are so prone to it. Shoving them into a pocket is the worst case, because the cord gets pushed and crossed in a hurry. Hanging the cord loosely lets it relax, and that's gentler on it.

Here's a fair caveat. Tangling depends on the cord's stiffness, its thickness, its coating, and how you store it and pull it out. Some cords tangle less because they are stiffer, or because their coating is slicker, or because they're wound in a certain way. Physicists have studied how cords and ropes knot under shaking and tugging, and the results depend on details. Some people hang the cord loosely over one hand, which lets the twist come out instead of building up. Even so, there isn't a single perfect fix. It's a messy physical problem, and the mess is real.

So the rainbow and the knot have one thing in common. Both come from simple rules repeated over and over. Each drop follows the same bending rule and sends light at one angle. Each segment of cord follows the same twisting rule and crosses itself. The large pattern, the bright arc or the tangled ball, comes from the repetition, not from any single piece.

Next time you see a rainbow, look for the ring around the point opposite the sun. Next time you pull out a tangled cord, you'll at least know the physics behind it. Either way, nature is doing exactly what the rules say, just in very large numbers.
NARRATION>>>
