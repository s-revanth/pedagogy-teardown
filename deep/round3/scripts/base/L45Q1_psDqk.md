## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Is anything real? It sounds like a trick question, the kind you ask in a dorm room at two in the morning. But it's worth taking seriously, because the answer changes depending on what you mean by real, and the attempt to pin it down goes somewhere surprising.

Start with the simplest version. Look at the table in front of you. It feels solid. You can press on it, and it pushes back. Now zoom in. The wood is made of atoms, and atoms are mostly empty space, with tiny nuclei and even tinier electrons moving around in a volume that dwarfs them. So the table is not solid in the way the word suggests. What your hand feels is electrical repulsion between the electrons in your skin and the electrons in the wood. The solidity is a feeling produced by forces. It is real in the sense that it happens, but it's not what the feeling suggests. A useful picture is two magnets held close together with like poles facing. You feel the resistance clearly, but nothing solid passes between them. The push is a real effect of forces, and the table is a huge bundle of those effects.

That's the first crack. Here's a second one. The table looks brown, or dark red, or whatever colour you think it is. But colour is not a property sitting on the wood like paint. The surface absorbs some wavelengths of light and reflects others. Those reflected wavelengths enter your eye, and your brain builds the experience of a colour out of them. Change the lighting, and the reflected wavelengths change, and the colour you experience changes too. The same is true for sound. The air is pushing and pulling in pressure waves, and the sound is what the brain makes of that pattern. The object doesn't contain the sound. It contains a set of vibrations.

If the things we see and hear are partly constructed, how do we know anything is out there at all? This is the old question, and it goes back a long way. René Descartes tried to answer it by doubting everything he could. His senses sometimes lie, dreams feel real, and even mathematics might be a trick played by some deceiver. He stripped away every belief he could possibly be wrong about. What was left? The fact that he was doubting. Something was doing the thinking. That is the one thing that doubt couldn't remove, and he built a whole philosophy on it. It's a clever move, but it leaves you with a mind and nothing obvious to set beside it.

Optical illusions are a good way to feel how this works. Two lines of the same length look different because the brain is making a guess about depth and perspective. Two patches of grey look like different shades because of the surrounding light. In each case, you are not looking at a clean recording of the world. You are looking at the brain's best interpretation of the world, and it is an interpretation that usually works. It only shows its seams when the inputs are tricky.

That leads to a thought experiment. Imagine a brain floating in a tank, wired up to a computer that feeds it exactly the signals a real body would send. Everything it experiences would feel real to it. From the inside, nothing would be different. Philosophers sometimes call this a brain in a vat, and a close cousin is the simulation argument, where the whole world is a computer program. Neither scenario can be ruled out from inside the experience. Which is exactly why they're useful. They show that the content of your experience can't, by itself, prove where it came from.

So what can we do? Here's a practical proposal. Instead of asking whether something feels real, ask whether it behaves like something real. Does it stay there when no one is looking? If you leave the table in the room and walk out, does it still exist when you come back? Does it hold up when other people check it? Can you see it, photograph it, measure it, and get the same answer as someone standing somewhere else? And does it predict what happens next? If you push the table, it moves in a way you can predict. A mirage fails the first test, because it disappears when you walk toward it. A dream fails the second, because nobody else sees it.

By that standard, reality is less a substance and more a pattern of stubbornness. It's the thing that keeps answering back when you poke it, in ways that agree with other people's pokes. That's not a complete answer to the metaphysical question, but it's a workable one, and it's how science operates.

Now a warning. Physics has its own version of this problem. In quantum mechanics, the act of measuring a particle seems to change its state, and the mathematics describes outcomes in terms of probabilities until a measurement happens. Some people read this as proof that reality depends on observers. Physicists disagree about what it means, and the disagreement is not settled. The equations work spectacularly well, but interpretations of what lies underneath them are still open. So science gives us an excellent test for what behaves in consistent ways, and it doesn't give us a finished picture of what is ultimately there.

Which brings us back to the table. It's still in the room. It still feels solid, even though it's mostly empty space. The same invisible pushing holds up your chair and the floor under it. Its colour is a conversation between light and your brain. And if you want to check whether it's real, you can do what physics does: walk out, come back, ask a friend, push it, and see if it answers the way you expect. Each check is a small test, and the table keeps passing them.

So here's the question to take with you. What would you check? Pick one thing you're sure of right now. What's the test that would convince you, and who else would have to agree?
NARRATION>>>
