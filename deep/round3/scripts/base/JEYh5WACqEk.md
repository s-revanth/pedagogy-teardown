## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Why did smallpox and measles cross the Atlantic mostly in one direction? Ships went both ways. People went both ways, at least for a while. And yet the disease traffic was lopsided, and the consequences were enormous. Europeans carried a set of pathogens into the Americas, and the people who had never met them had almost no defence. Things flowed the other way too, but far less, and the question is why.

The short answer is animals, people, and time. The long answer has several moving parts, so let me take them one at a time.

Here is the outline. Three filters decide which diseases can make the crossing and survive on the other side. The first is which animals were nearby, because many human diseases began as animal diseases that learned to live in people. The second is how many people were packed together, because some diseases cannot survive in a small population. The third is how long the contact went on, because the more connected two regions are, the more pathogens they swap.

Start with the second filter, because it is the easiest to see in a small case. Imagine a village of one hundred people. A sick traveller arrives carrying measles. Most of the village catches it. Each person who recovers is now immune. Within a few weeks the infected people are either recovered or dead, and the disease has nobody left to infect. The outbreak simply stops. It is not defeated by a cure. It runs out of people.

Now the village has a new problem. Babies are born, and some of them have no immunity. But in a village of one hundred, the number of new susceptible children each year is small, so the next arrival might find a few sparks and nothing to catch fire. The disease keeps coming back only when a new carrier arrives from somewhere else. Between visits, it vanishes.

This is the idea of critical community size. It is the number of people below which an infection cannot keep itself going. For measles, the figures people quote are in the hundreds of thousands. Estimates vary, and they depend on how connected the population is, but the basic point holds. A big, dense, connected population can keep measles circulating all year. A small one cannot.

Think of it like a candle and a forest. Light the same spark in both places and the result is completely different. In the candle the flame goes out. In the forest the fire can run for miles. The spark is the same. The difference is how much fuel is close together.

Small islands show the same pattern again and again. A disease arrives on a ship, burns through the population, and disappears. Then the next ship brings it back. Whether the disease survives depends on whether enough people are there to keep it going, and on how often someone new arrives to restart it.

Now the first filter. Many diseases that spread among people started out in animals. A pathogen that jumps from a cow, a pig, a bird, or a rodent into a person has to become good at living in people, and there are a lot of animals in Eurasia that gave that chance.

The old world had a long history of domestication. Cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, horses, and poultry were tamed, kept in large numbers, and kept close to people for thousands of years. Every one of those close relationships was a chance for a pathogen to cross over. Measles is widely thought to have come from an animal disease related to cattle plague. The origins of smallpox are less clear, and the evidence points to a link with rodents, but the details are still disputed. The broad picture is solid even where the specifics are not.

Count the animals on the other side of the ocean. The Americas had dogs, llamas, alpacas, guinea pigs, and turkeys. Those are real animals, and they were important. But there were far fewer large domesticated mammals, and none of the big herd animals that Eurasia had in such numbers. That means far fewer sources of new diseases. The Americas did not lack pathogens. They lacked the particular ones that Eurasia had been collecting from its animals.

The third filter is time and connection. Eurasia was not just big. It was linked. Trade routes ran across the continent for centuries, and armies and merchants carried goods, people, and germs with them. Diseases moved through a network. Each region added its own pathogens to the pool, and the whole pool got mixed. The Americas had been cut off for thousands of years. When contact finally came, it came suddenly, and it came all at once.

Before going on, I want to deal with a tempting explanation that is simply wrong. It goes like this: one group was biologically weaker, so they died. That is not what happened. Nobody's immune system is bad at fighting infections in general. The difference was that each side had met a different set of germs. A population that has lived with a disease for a thousand years has a sort of shared history with it, and that history shows up in survival. A population that has never met it has no such history, and it can be hit very hard the first time.

The reverse direction is more complicated, and I want to be careful. The most common candidate for a disease moving from the Americas to Europe is syphilis, which appeared in Europe soon after contact. Some researchers think it came from the Americas. Others think it was already present in Europe and simply became more visible. The evidence is thin, it is disputed, and anyone who gives you a confident answer is ahead of the data. So the honest summary is that the pattern is clear, but a single reverse example is not.

So, the payoff. The one-way pattern is not destiny, and it is not evidence that one group was simply better or worse. It is the product of three things. Which animals were nearby and how close people lived to them. How many people were packed together in one place, which decides whether a disease can keep going. And how long and how widely the populations were connected, which decides how many pathogens got mixed.

Now go back to the question. Why did smallpox and measles cross the Atlantic mostly one way? Because Eurasia had the animals, the crowds, and the connections, and the Americas had been apart from all of it for a very long time. Remove any one of those factors and the story changes.
