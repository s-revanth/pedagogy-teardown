## Narration

<<<NARRATION
I want to start with a confession. Most of the games I made in the first few years were bad. Some were not even games in any real sense. They were experiments that I was too proud to throw away, and I still have a few of them. Today I want to show you some of that early work, and talk about what those years actually taught me, because it was not what I expected at the time.

The story has three parts, roughly. There was a stage of copying, where I was doing exactly what the tutorials said and not really understanding much of it. Then a stage of building my own things, which was messier and much more useful. And then a stage of finishing, which took me far longer to learn than I would like to admit. I'll go through them in order, and I'll try to be honest about where I was getting it wrong.

It started on an old home computer. It was not powerful, and it did not have much in the way of tools, but it could run a simple programming environment, and that was enough. I found a tutorial and followed it line by line. I typed in every line, got the result, and felt amazing. Then I would move on to the next tutorial. What I did not do was stop and ask why any of the lines worked. I could make the thing happen, but I could not make a new thing happen.

That is a very common way to start, and I don't regret it. Copying is how you get your hands on the basic pieces. But it has a limit, and I hit that limit hard. The moment a tutorial ended, I had no idea what to do next. I had a folder full of almost-identical little programs, each one a slightly different copy of the same example, and none of them was mine.

The real change came when I got a book. It was dry, and it asked me to do things that were not in a tutorial. Make a small thing move. Make it react to input. Make it stop when it hits something. The exercises were not very exciting, but they forced me to think about what each line was doing. That is when I made my first prototype that was actually my own idea, and I remember it as the first time the work felt like mine.

Let's look at some of that footage. This is the prototype. It is crude. The movement is jerky, there is no proper feedback when something goes wrong, and the whole thing would bore anybody after thirty seconds. But it worked. It did one thing I had decided on, and I had designed the rule for it myself. Looking at it now, I can see every problem, but at the time it was the most exciting thing on the screen.

After that came the stall, and this is the part I am least proud of. I started project after project. Each one was bigger than the last. I had a plan for a huge game with lots of levels, complicated systems, and a story. I would work on it for a few weeks, hit the first real difficulty, and then start something new. Looking through my old folders, there are dozens of these, each one with a few files and nothing playable. I was not short of ideas. I was short of finishing.

For a long time I believed the problem was my tools. If I had a better engine, or a better language, or a better setup, then I would finish things. So I kept changing the environment, looking for the one that would make it easy. That was a myth, and it cost me years. The tools were never really the blocker. The blocker was that I kept choosing projects that were too large to end, and then blaming whatever was in front of me.

The breakthrough was almost embarrassingly small. I made a little game with one screen, one goal and one way to lose. I decided in advance what it would include, and then I refused to add anything else until it was finished. It took a couple of weeks. It was not good, but it had a beginning, a middle and an end. When it was done, I played it from start to finish, and I realised I had never done that before. That was the first game I had actually finished.

Here is a comparison, so you can see the gap. This is a later project, one I worked on with a clearer plan. It is still not perfect, and I would change a lot if I started it again. But the difference is that it has a clear goal, the systems fit together, and it reaches an end. The distance between that and the earlier prototypes is mostly a result of finishing lots of small things, not of getting dramatically more talented in one leap.

I find an instrument is a useful way to explain this. Nobody learns to play the piano by sitting down and playing a full concerto. You play scales. You play simple pieces with one hand. You repeat the same few notes until your fingers know them. Making small games is the scales. Each one teaches you a single skill, and each one ends, so you get the satisfaction of completing something, which keeps you going.

The same idea applies well beyond games. Writing, drawing, building things, even learning a language. In every case, the people who improve fastest tend to finish lots of small pieces rather than starting one huge piece and never completing it. Small scope is not a limitation. It is how you get the feedback you need to improve. A finished small thing teaches you more than an unfinished large one, because you can see what actually happened.

So here is the advice I would give to someone at the start. Finish something small before you start something large. Scope down on purpose, even when it feels like you are cheating yourself. Decide what the project includes before you begin, and then stop adding to it. Keep a list of things you will do later, so the ideas are not lost, but do not let the list become the project. And when you finish, look at what you made, even if it is bad. That is where the learning is.

I'd like to be clear that I still start big things. The difference now is that I try to keep a small finished version as the first milestone, so there is always something complete on the screen. That one habit has done more for me than any tool or any course I have taken. It is not a glamorous answer, but it is the one that worked.

So to recap. In the copying stage, I could make things happen but could not make new things. In the building stage, I started making my own rules, and that was the real beginning. In the finishing stage, I learned that tools were not the problem, scope was. Small complete projects were what turned all of that into skill.

If you are at the start right now, I hope this is useful. Your early work will look embarrassing later, and that is fine. It means you have improved. Make something small, finish it, and make the next one. That is really all there is to it. Thanks for listening, and take care.
NARRATION>>>
