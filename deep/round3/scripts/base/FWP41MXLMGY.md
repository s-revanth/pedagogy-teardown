## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Picture a road in central London during a normal weekday. It is jammed. Buses crawl, taxis nudge forward, and the cyclists are weaving between lorries at every junction. Now picture the same road in the first spring lockdown, with almost nothing on it. That is the image this ride is built around. It is the most unusual version of the city most people have ever seen, and it tells you a lot about how the city works when the traffic goes away.

Here is the answer up front. A bike ride through an empty city shows you the space that is normally taken up by cars, and it shows what it takes to keep some of that space once the emergency is over. The rest of the video is the ride itself, with a few detours to explain what I am looking at.

Here is the outline. I will go along the route in stretches. Then I will talk about the layout of the city, the experiments that appeared while the roads were quiet, the reasons cycling feels hostile in normal times, and the limits of what the empty streets can tell us.

Start at a station. The bike is unlocked, the seat is set, and the route is a line on a map that runs from the station, through the side streets, and out toward the river. On a normal day, this start would be a long wait at a light. This morning, there is hardly anyone at the junction.

The first stretch is quiet side streets. The houses are close together, the pavements are narrow, and the parked cars are where they always were. But the road itself is nearly empty. Every few minutes a bus goes past, and it is almost empty too. Once in a while, a delivery van turns into a side road. Otherwise, the street belongs to the people who are out walking, or to the rider.

Now a detour, because you cannot understand the ride without the layout. London is not a planned grid. Many of its streets follow old lanes, footpaths, and tracks that were there long before anybody drew a plan. The Romans laid out roads that still shape some routes today. The medieval city added lanes and yards, and the Victorian city added the wide roads and railway lines that cut through older neighbourhoods. The result is a tangle. Radial roads run into the centre from every direction, ring roads sit around it, and the river runs through the middle as a barrier with a limited number of crossings.

That tangle matters for a bike ride. A straight line on the map is rarely a straight line on the ground. You take a turn to avoid a major road, and then another turn to avoid a junction, and before long the route has doubled its length. The river is the biggest single obstacle. Every crossing is a bridge, and every bridge is a place where the bike lane narrows or ends.

The middle of the ride is where the empty roads really show something. On screen, the traffic counts for the city appear as bars. In the spring, the drop in car traffic is steep. The bars for buses fall too, but less. The bars for cycling go up, though from a low starting point. The difference in the bars is the story of the lockdown. The city did not stop moving. It moved differently.

While the roads were quiet, something else was happening. The city began to put in temporary cycle lanes, often just a painted line and a row of plastic bollards, and low-traffic neighbourhoods appeared on residential streets. A low-traffic neighbourhood is a street or set of streets where through traffic is discouraged, usually with barriers that let bikes and buses pass but stop cars from using the street as a shortcut. On this ride, those features appear one after another. Some look good. Some are rough. Some end at a junction without much explanation.

Now the caveat, because it matters. The quiet was not a free experiment. People were not allowed out, most offices were closed, and many shops were shut. The empty streets were the product of an emergency. They are not a preview of what the city would look like if everyone agreed to drive less. Anyone who looks at the empty roads and says the city would simply work better is missing the reason the roads were empty in the first place.

Still, the ride does show something useful. The space is there. On a quiet road, you can see how wide the carriageway is, how much room a lane could take, and how many parked cars sit in lanes that could be used for something else. The question is not whether the city has the room. The question is who gets to use it, and for what.

Now the junctions, which is where the ride stops being pleasant. This one is a long, wide junction. It is open, the tarmac is huge, and the lights are timed for cars. The cycle lane ends just before the junction, and for a few seconds the rider is simply mixed in with the traffic. A lorry pulls up on the left. The driver cannot see a small rider low down by the wheel. Nobody is hurt, but the moment is tense, and that tension is the reason many people do not cycle at all.

Here is the derivation, in plain words. Why does cycling feel dangerous in London? Three things. Junctions are wide, so a rider has to cross a lot of space with traffic coming from several directions. Large vehicles like lorries have blind spots on their side and front, and a rider close to the wheel is easy to miss. And many cycle lanes stop without warning, so the rider has to move into traffic at exactly the point where it is most dangerous. Each of those is a design choice, and each can be changed.

Other cities have tried something similar. Some ran temporary experiments during the same period. The ones that lasted tended to combine physical barriers that keep cars out of a lane with clear junctions where the lane is continuous and visible. The ones that failed tended to be paint on a road that cars could still use. The difference is not the idea. It is the design, and whether the design is good enough to make someone feel safe on a real road with real traffic.

So what did the ride show? It showed that the city has the space. It showed that the space is not fixed. Some of it can change quickly when the rules change. And it showed that the hard part is not finding room for bikes. The hard part is building junctions and lanes that a nervous rider can trust, on a busy weekday, without a single empty road to help.

The ride is nearly over now. The route curves back toward the river, and the last stretch is a long, straight road with a lane that has been painted fresh. There are a few people on it, and a couple of bikes, and the traffic is light but not gone. It looks like the city in the opening image, but it is also clearly the same road that was jammed a year before. The quiet is still there, a little, at the edges.

So go back to the road from the start, the one that was jammed on a normal weekday. Empty, it showed you the space. Full again, it shows you the choices. The city does not have to stay empty to be different. It only has to decide who the space is for.
