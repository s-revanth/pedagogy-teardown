## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Which large American city still does not have a voting member of Congress? It is a question with a slightly awkward answer, and the answer is Washington, D.C. The capital has a population bigger than some states, it has its own local government, and it still does not have a full vote in the body that makes national law. That gap is the subject of this video, and it points at a bigger story about how cities get power at all.

Here is the answer up front. Washington, D.C., is the case the title points at. And the reason it stayed the way it did is not a single decision. It is a chain of legal choices, each one made for its own reasons.

So, to set the scene. Over the last century, many American cities became more democratic in the ordinary sense. They got elected mayors, where previously the mayor had been appointed or chosen by a small group. They got councils elected by district, which gave neighbourhoods a direct voice. And they got broader voting rights, so that more residents could take part. Not every city went the same way, and not every change stuck. But the general direction was toward more residents choosing their local leaders.

That is the trend. Now the constraint, because it explains why the trend was not universal.

Under a legal principle often called Dillon's rule, a city is a creature of its state. It can do what the state has allowed it to do, and nothing beyond that. The city does not have an inherent right to write its own charter or set its own structure. The state writes the rules, and the state can change them. Think of it as a tenant. The city lives in the building, but the lease is written by the state, and the landlord can change the terms.

That is a simple model, and it has a lot of consequences. For most cities, the state is the landlord, and it decides whether residents vote for mayors, how councils are shaped, and what powers the city has. For a city that is not a state, the landlord is the national government, and the arrangement is much more unusual.

Now the history of Washington, D.C. It has a complicated path, so let me go through it in order. In 1961, the Constitution was amended to give residents of D.C. the right to vote in presidential elections. That amendment was first used in 1964. So residents could choose a president, but still had no vote in the House or the Senate. In 1971, D.C. got a delegate to the House of Representatives. The delegate can speak and serve on committees, but cannot cast a vote on the final passage of a bill. That is a real representative, but not a full one.

Then came the Home Rule Act of 1973, which gave the District more control over its own local affairs. In 1974, residents elected a mayor for the first time under that framework. That is a big change, and it was a real step toward self-government. The city got its own elected council and its own mayor, and it began to make many of its own decisions.

Now the misconception, which is the important part. Home rule sounds like full self-government. It is not. Congress keeps the power to review local laws and can overturn them. The federal government also reviews the District's budget, and the city's spending has to go through a process that includes Congress. So D.C. governs itself in many practical ways, but it does so under a set of rules that Congress can rewrite at any time. The gap is not only a missing vote. It is also a set of powers that stay outside the city's hands.

I want to be careful about how far this goes. D.C. is one case. Other cities and territories have their own arrangements, and different states give cities very different powers. Some states give their cities a great deal of freedom, and some keep them tightly bound. So Washington is a clear example of one kind of arrangement, not a summary of every American city.

So what is the payoff? The gap in D.C. is not a mystery. It is a set of legal choices, made at different times, for different reasons, that can be changed by the same people who made them. A vote in Congress is one such choice. A change in the home rule framework is another. Both are possible, and both have been debated for decades.

That leaves an open question. Will the remaining gap close, and if it does, who will decide? The answer depends on the people who make the law, and on the residents who keep asking for the same thing. It is one of those questions where the history is clear, and the future is not.
