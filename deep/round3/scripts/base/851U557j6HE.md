## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Here is a formula that looks like it should be a bug. Take the function sine of x over x. Integrate it from zero to infinity, and you get pi over two. Now multiply it by sine of x over three, divided by x over three. Integrate again, and you still get pi over two. Keep going. Multiply by sine of x over five, over x over five. Still pi over two. Keep adding factors with odd denominators, and the answer keeps coming out to the same number, through seven factors in total.

Then, at the eighth factor, it breaks. The result drops by a tiny amount. People who first saw this assumed they had made an error, or that the computer had. That is a reasonable reaction. A pattern that holds for seven steps and then fails looks like a bug.

But it is not a bug. There is an actual reason, and it says something real about how these integrals behave. The short version is this: the break happens when a certain sum of widths crosses a threshold. I'll build up to that slowly.

Let's start with the simplest piece. The function sine of x over x is called the sinc function. It starts at one when x is zero, wiggles, and dies away as x grows. If you integrate it over the positive numbers, the area under the curve comes out to pi over two. That is the first fact everything else rests on.

Now the second factor. Sine of x over three, over x over three, is the same shape, squeezed. It wiggles three times faster and dies away faster. Multiply it by the first sinc, and the product is a new curve. Its shape looks different, but its area still comes out to pi over two. Look at what happens on the screen. The product is smaller everywhere than the original. So the area should drop, and yet it does not.

Let's test this numerically rather than trusting intuition. Compute the integral for the product of two factors, then three, then four. Each time you add a factor, the curve gets narrower and lower. Each time, the integral appears to stay at pi over two, to many decimal places. Factor by factor, the answer holds. That is brute force, and it is convincing, but it does not explain anything. It only shows the pattern is stable up to the point we have checked.

So let's address the instinct that is tempting you. Each new factor is a number between minus one and one, and it squeezes the curve. It seems obvious that the integral should shrink, because the curve is shrinking. That is the misconception. The curve gets smaller in height, but also narrower in width, and the two effects balance. The area is what matters, and the area is not simply the product of the factors' sizes.

To see why, we need a different picture of each sinc function. Think of sine of x over x as the frequency view of a box. A box is a simple shape: flat at a fixed height over a range of frequencies, and zero outside. Each sinc function corresponds to a box, and the width of that box depends on the denominator. Sine of x over three corresponds to a box one third as wide. Sine of x over five corresponds to a box a fifth as wide. So the sinc functions are really stories about boxes, and the widths are the important data.

Why do boxes help? Multiplying two functions in the x world corresponds to a convolution in the frequency world, which is like sliding one box across another and asking how they overlap. Integrating the product over all x picks out the value of the combined frequency shape at zero. So the question about the integral turns into a question about the height of a stack of boxes, sitting at zero.

Now let's compute. Add up the widths of the boxes, leaving out the first one. For the first seven factors, those widths are one third, one fifth, one seventh, one ninth, one eleventh, and one thirteenth. Add them up and you get about zero point nine five five. That number is less than one, the width of the first box. The stack at zero stays flat on top, at exactly the level that gives pi over two. The boxes slide around, but as long as the combined spread never reaches past the edge of the first box, the height at zero is set by the first box alone.

That is the entire reason for the first seven steps. The combined width of the other boxes is smaller than the first one, so there is always a flat region where the answer is locked in. Nothing changes the result.

Now add the eighth factor, with denominator fifteen. Its width is one fifteenth, about zero point zero six seven. Add it to the others and the total becomes about one point zero two. That is just over one. The stack can now reach past the edge of the first box, and the flat top at zero is no longer perfectly flat. The height at zero dips, a little. The integral drops by a tiny amount. There was never a bug. There was a threshold, and the eighth box crossed it.

Let me pause here, because this is a good moment for you to check it yourself. Pause the video, and add up one third, one fifth, one seventh, one ninth, one eleventh, and one thirteenth. Compare your total to one. Then add one fifteenth and compare again. If your sum crosses one exactly where I said, the explanation holds up. If it doesn't, something in my picture is off, and that would be worth finding.

Here is the broader lesson. This kind of argument has a familiar form. When you add up independent pieces with bounded spreads, the density of the total at a point stays fixed as long as the spreads are small enough, and changes once they are not. The same mechanism appears in probability, when you sum uniform random variables and ask about the density of their total. The integrals are one clean case of a general phenomenon.

So go back to where we started. The first seven factors are not a coincidence and not a numerical artifact. They are a consequence of a width condition. The eighth factor is not a mistake either. It is the first moment when the combined spread exceeds the first box, and the curve has room to tip.

To sum up, the three ideas are these. Multiplying sinc functions with odd denominators corresponds to stacking boxes of decreasing width. The integral is the height of the stack at zero. And that height stays exactly at pi over two until the small boxes together reach past the big one. Once you see the widths, the break at the eighth factor becomes the most natural thing in the world.

It looked like a bug. It turned out to be a threshold, and a threshold is a very different thing.
