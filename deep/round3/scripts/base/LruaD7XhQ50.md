## Narration
<<<NARRATION
The federal government owns more land than Alaska. That is a surprising sentence, because Alaska is the biggest state by a very large margin. You picture the federal government as a building in a capital city, a handful of offices, and maybe a few military bases. The reality is that a huge part of the country is land the federal government holds directly, and it is bigger than the largest state.

Here is the answer up front. Federal land in the United States is roughly 640 million acres. Alaska is about 365 million acres. So the federal total is larger than Alaska's entire area. Those are rough figures, and the exact number depends on what you count, but the comparison holds.

Here is the outline. Where the land is. Why it is there. Who manages it. And what the numbers do and do not mean.

Start with the map. If you put the federal land on a map of the United States, shaded in, a clear pattern shows up immediately. The West is heavily shaded. The East is mostly not. Large blocks of federal land sit across the mountains, the deserts, and the Pacific states. In much of the East, almost all of the land is in private hands or owned by states.

Now the common picture, because it is the one most people carry around. When you think of federal land, you probably imagine buildings: offices, courthouses, military installations, and national capitals. Those exist, but they are small in area. Most federal land is open country. Forest, grassland, desert, mountains, and wetlands. It is land that is owned by the government and is mostly not built on at all.

To make the word clear, let us define it. Public domain is land owned by the federal government that has not yet been given or sold to anyone. That is the origin of most of this land, and it is the key to the rest of the story. The question is how the government came to own so much of it in the first place.

The short version is that the government acquired a lot of land all at once, and then gave away or sold a lot of land in some regions but not others. Look at the big acquisitions. The Louisiana Purchase in 1803 added a huge block of the interior. The Mexican Cession in 1848, and the Gadsden Purchase in 1853, added land across the Southwest. The purchase of Alaska in 1867 added the territory that became the state. In each case, the land came to the federal government. Much of it stayed with the federal government, especially in the drier West, where settlement was harder and farming was less productive.

Now the other side of the story, because it explains the East. The Homestead Act of 1862 let people claim land for a small fee, if they farmed it and lived on it for a set period. Sales and grants moved land out of federal hands across the Midwest and East, and that land became private farms, towns, and cities. The land there was good for farming, close to markets, and easy to settle. So the government sold or gave it away, and it became private.

The West was different. The land was dry, and farming a quarter section of desert did not work the way it did in the Midwest. Large areas were better suited to grazing, to forests, or to nothing much at all. So a lot of it was never handed out in the same way. It stayed in federal hands and was managed as public land.

Here is a quick tour of the numbers, state by state. Nevada is one of the most federal states in the country, with roughly four-fifths of its land owned by the federal government. Utah is also heavily federal, with around three-fifths of its land. And Alaska itself is about three-fifths federal, which is a neat wrinkle. The biggest state is majority federal too. So the federal total is not a freak of geography. It is the sum of several very large western states.

That is the detail that surprises people. Most maps of the country show every state as the same kind of block, with a state name and a border. The federal share is hidden inside those blocks, and it only shows up when you shade the land by who owns it. Once you do, the western states look like they belong to a different country.

Now the agencies, because the land is not managed by one office. The Bureau of Land Management holds the largest share, a quarter of a billion acres or so, much of it in the arid West. The Forest Service manages national forests and grasslands. The National Park Service runs national parks and monuments. The Fish and Wildlife Service manages refuges. And the military holds large training and testing areas. Each has its own rules, its own budgets, and its own history.

So far I have been using the big number. Now the caveats, because the total depends on definitions. What counts as federal land? Does it include water, such as lakes and offshore areas? Does it include tribal reservations, which are held in trust but are not quite the same as federal land managed by agencies? Different sources add up different pieces, and so the totals vary. The general picture is solid, and the Alaska comparison holds, but the exact figure is a matter of definitions as well as surveying.

So what is the payoff? The map is a record of how the country was acquired and settled. Land bought or taken in the nineteenth century stayed in federal hands in the regions where settlement was hardest. Land in the regions where farming paid off was handed or sold to private owners. The result is a map where the West looks like it belongs to the federal government and the East looks like it belongs to individuals and states.

Go back to the surprise. The federal government owns more land than Alaska, which sounded like a trick. It is not a trick. It is what happens when a country buys huge territories, settles some of them quickly, and leaves the rest in public hands.
