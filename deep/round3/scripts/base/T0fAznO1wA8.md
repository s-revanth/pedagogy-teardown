## Narration
<<<NARRATION
Picture a pirate captain. You probably see a man with a hat, a sword, and a voice that nobody argues with. He points at a ship, the crew jumps, and what he says goes. He is the boss in the most complete sense of the word. That picture is a little bit true and mostly wrong.

The captain was the boss during a fight. Not during the whole voyage, and not in every decision. That split is the whole story, so let me unpack it.

Here is the outline. First, the picture we have, and where it comes from. Second, what the crews actually wrote down. Third, the reason the arrangement made sense. And at the end, we come back to the word boss.

Start with the myth. A captain who could do anything he liked, whenever he liked, to whoever he liked. That image is powerful, and it has plenty of help from stories written long after the fact, and from trial records and accounts of victims. Those sources tend to describe the most dramatic moments. The moment of attack. The moment of punishment. They do not tell you much about an ordinary Tuesday on board.

When you look at what crews actually agreed to, the picture changes. Many pirate crews drew up written articles when they joined a ship. These were short agreements. They covered who got what, how the loot was divided, and what happened to people who were injured. They also covered how the crew made major decisions. Those decisions included where to sail, whether to attack, and what to do with a prize. The rules were not a formality. Everyone signed, or at least everyone was expected to.

Picture the ship as a small committee. There is a set of rules everybody has agreed to. There is a way of voting on big questions. There is a fixed share of the loot for each person, with a larger share for certain officers, and sometimes for the captain. The captain has real authority, but it comes from the crew, and it can be withdrawn.

Here is the key part. In a fight, a ship cannot afford a debate. If you have forty people trying to shout orders during a boarding action, the boarding action goes badly. So during battle the captain gives orders, and the crew follows them. That is the captain as commander. It is narrow, specific, and temporary. When the fighting stops, the authority goes back to being a shared arrangement.

Ships also had a second officer who mattered a great deal. The quartermaster was usually elected alongside the captain. He looked after the stores, kept track of shares, and settled disputes between crew members. In many accounts, the quartermaster was the one who spoke for the crew when the captain's decisions were questioned. He was a counterweight. So in port, the captain did not simply decide what happened next. There was a check on him that was built into the ship.

Think about what an ordinary week looked like. Most days at sea were not fights. They were long stretches of sailing, cleaning, mending, and waiting. Those quiet hours are when the articles mattered most. Who took which watch? Who got the extra ration? Who was responsible for the stores? The answers were not left to the captain's mood. They were agreed in advance, and the crew could check them. That is part of why the captain's power in battle was accepted without much grumbling. The crew knew the rest of the ship was not his to command.

Now the question that matters. If a captain can be voted out, why would the crew accept him in the first place? The answer is about incentives. A crew of people who have all escaped bad captains, bad owners, or bad pay on other ships is not going to hand one man unlimited power. A captain who could take everything would be a captain whose crew would not stay. So the captain had to be useful, and useful captains were the ones who kept the crew happy.

There is a modern parallel that is easier to picture. A merchant ship has a master, and in a real sense the master is in charge at sea. Nobody argues with the navigation order in a storm. But the master answers to the owners in port, and the owners answer to the law. The authority is real and bounded, and it is shaped by the people it is supposed to serve. Pirate ships were not identical to merchant ships, but the logic of a leader who needs a crew to keep working is similar.

Now the caveat, because it matters here. Historical sources about pirates are uneven. Most of what we know comes from trials, from accounts written by people who were attacked, from newspapers, and from later books. Some of those accounts were written to make the pirates look as frightening as possible. Some were written to make them look like brave rebels. The articles that survive are a real source, but a small one, and any neat summary of how every pirate ship worked is too neat. Different crews did different things.

Still, the general lesson holds up. A crew that can remove its leader in peacetime is a crew that will follow that leader in a fight. The vote is not a sign of weakness. It is the reason the orders work. People who chose their captain are more likely to obey him when it counts, and people who could remove him are less likely to feel trapped under him.

So go back to the word boss. A pirate captain was a commander, a leader, and in a fight, the one whose word settled things. But he was not the owner of the ship, and he was not the owner of the crew. Those people had a say. They could vote him out, and they sometimes did.

That is why the picture in your head is almost right, and why the word boss is the wrong word for the job.
NARRATION>>>
