# Narration

<<<NARRATION
Here is a video with a number on it. Billions of views. Someone watched the clip, and the platform recorded it. A view is not a natural fact, like the weight of a rock. It is a definition, and someone decided what it would be. That decision shapes the number you see, and sometimes it shapes the whole story around it.

So what counts as a view? On many platforms, the answer is more flexible than you might expect. In a feed, a video can start playing automatically as you scroll past it. If it plays for a few seconds, the platform can register a view, even if you never clicked, never turned the sound on and never looked up from your thumb. Under some definitions, a few seconds of autoplay is enough. Each definition is a choice, and each choice produces a different count for exactly the same clip.

Imagine a creator who makes an original video, the kind that takes weeks of work. It goes viral. The views climb. Now imagine a different account, one that did none of that work. It grabs a copy of the clip, maybe trims it, adds a logo in the corner and reposts it. The copy starts spreading in feeds, and some of its views are counted by the platform. The reposting account earns attention, and sometimes ad money, from work it did not do. The original creator sees the views flow somewhere else.

This is what people mean when they say views are being stolen: a system where copies are easy, attention is measured loosely and the rewards go to whoever gets the numbers first.

Now the incentives. Platforms want big numbers, because big numbers attract advertisers, creators and more users. Advertisers want evidence that their money reaches people. Creators want proof that their work is being seen. A loose definition of a view is a very convenient way to produce a bigger number, and nobody has to lie outright to benefit from it.

Here is the honest caveat. Most of the data behind these numbers is private. Outsiders cannot check every figure, and the companies do not publish the full details of how each view is counted. So it would be unfair to say that every view is fake, or that every platform is cheating. Some counts may be real under a broad but reasonable definition. The problem is not only fraud. The problem is that a definition can make a number look more impressive than it is, and most viewers never see the definition at all.

This is not unique to one platform. Each app has its own rules. One counts a view after three seconds. Another waits longer. A third counts a play every time a clip restarts. So the same clip, uploaded to several apps, can produce very different totals. A headline view count tells you about the platform's rules as much as about the audience. If you compare numbers across apps without knowing the rules, you are comparing different rulers.

Think of it like a shop that counts everyone who walks past its window as a customer. The number would look impressive. But the customers who actually walk through the door are the ones that matter. Attention on a feed works the same way. A passing glance is not the same as a watch. Someone who watches the whole clip, returns to it, or shares it with a friend is giving far more value than someone whose thumb paused for a moment.

So what should you look at? For creators, the most useful numbers are watch time, retention and where viewers came from. Watch time tells you how long people stayed. Retention shows you where they dropped off. Source tells you whether the views came from your own audience, from search, or from a reupload that is quietly taking your reach. For viewers, the lesson is simpler. A big number on a clip does not prove that the clip is good, or that the person who posted it made it.

And there is a bigger point underneath this. A view count is a measurement of attention. Attention is limited, and it is the most valuable resource on a platform. When measurement gets loose, attention can be redirected from the people who made something to the people who copied it fastest. That is worth fighting over, and the fight is most honest when the definitions are public and the numbers can be checked.

So the next time you see a view count with a lot of zeros, do the quick check. What counts as a view here? Who uploaded it first? How long did people actually watch? The number may be impressive, but the story behind it is the part that tells you what it really means.
