<<<NARRATION
This is a war fought over a group of islands with more sheep than people, and it happened in 1982. The Falkland Islands. Or the Malvinas, depending on who you ask. And the answer to who wins is up front: Britain wins the islands back, but the cost is heavy on both sides.

Let's set the scene. The Falklands are a cluster of islands in the South Atlantic, a long way from Britain and a long way from Argentina, too. They're cold, windy, and mostly farmland, with sheep, peat, and a small population of British settlers. For a long time, Britain had controlled them, and Argentina had claimed them. So you have a dispute over a remote patch of land that doesn't seem worth a fight until somebody decides it is.

Now think about Argentina in 1982. The country is run by a military junta, a group of generals who took power in a coup in 1976. They're in trouble. The economy is a mess, there is growing anger over human rights abuses, and the government is struggling to hold together. And when a government is in trouble at home, it sometimes looks for a foreign enemy to rally people around.

So in early April 1982, Argentina sends forces to the islands. On 2 April, Argentine troops land and take Port Stanley, the small capital. There's a small garrison of British Royal Marines there, and they can't hold off a larger invasion force. The governor is taken into custody, and the Argentine flag goes up.

For Argentina, it's a big moment. The junta gets a burst of popular support. And for Britain, it's a shock. Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher decides that the islands are going to be retaken, and she commits to sending a task force.

Here's the problem. The Falklands are roughly eight thousand miles from Britain. That's an enormous distance to sail a fleet, and it takes weeks. The task force has to be supplied the whole way, and it needs ships, aircraft, troops, and a huge amount of planning. It's a logistical nightmare, and it is also a test of whether Britain can still project force on that scale.

Now a caveat, because war stories get messy fast. Early claims about who was winning were often shaped by propaganda from both sides. So if you hear a very clean story about a quick victory, that's a sign you should check the timeline and the sources.

The first British success is South Georgia, a remote island in the South Atlantic that Argentina had also occupied. British forces retake it in late April. It's a small operation, but it's a sign that the task force can actually do something.

Then the naval war heats up. Britain declares an exclusion zone around the islands, and the navies start hunting each other. In early May, a British submarine sinks the Argentine cruiser General Belgrano. It's a controversial attack, because the ship was outside the zone, and it is one of the deadliest moments of the war. A few days later, an Argentine missile hits the British destroyer Sheffield, and the ship sinks. So the sea war is no longer a bluff. It's real, and people are dying.

Then the landings. In late May, British forces land at San Carlos, on the northwest side of East Falkland. It's a risky move, because the landing area is exposed to air attack, and the Argentine air force is determined. But the British get ashore, establish a beachhead, and start moving toward Stanley over land.

Think about the logistics for a second. Every bullet, every tent, every tin of food has to get to the islands by sea and then move across boggy ground with few roads. Helicopters are stretched thin, ships are vulnerable, and the weather can turn on you in an hour. The war is as much about getting stuff to the right place as it is about fighting once you get there.

The ground fight is hard. At Goose Green, British paratroopers fight a tough battle against Argentine defenders, and it's a costly victory. Then the British push toward Stanley, through a series of hills and ridges, in freezing conditions. The fighting is miserable. Soldiers are cold, wet, exhausted, and fighting in terrain that favours the defenders.

The final assaults are brutal. The British take hill after hill, one at a time, until the Argentine position around Stanley starts to crack. By 14 June 1982, the Argentine commander surrenders the garrison at Stanley. The war is over. The islands are back under British control.

Back home, the war was a huge deal too. In Britain, it split opinion, with some people questioning whether a distant territory was worth the lives, and others rallying behind the troops. In Argentina, the defeat turned a national rally into a national reckoning, and people began asking hard questions about the junta. A short war can have a very long tail.

So what's the cost? Estimates put British deaths at around 255, and Argentine deaths at around 649, along with a few Falkland civilians. Those are numbers you can check, but keep in mind that every figure has a human story behind it, and many of those people were young conscripts sent into the cold with poor equipment.

For Britain, the war is a political win. For Thatcher, it's a huge boost. For Argentina, it's a disaster, and the junta falls not long after. The war becomes a case study in how a small dispute, mixed with domestic politics, can drag in navies, air forces, and whole armies over a place most people had never thought about.

So here's the takeaway. The Falklands are a small place with a big political story. A war fought over sheep and a few towns ends up being about pride, politics, and a government that wanted a win. The islands are still there. The sheep are still there. And the war still gets argued about.

Thanks for watching, and no, the sheep did not vote.
