<<<NARRATION
How many people do you think die in road crashes every year? Take a guess before I tell you. Most people guess a few thousand, or perhaps tens of thousands. The real number is far higher. Worldwide it's roughly 1.2 million deaths a year, and in the United States alone it's over 40,000. That's a lot of people, every year, from a problem we have mostly learned to accept as normal. So here's a question: could a machine drive better than we do? And if it could, why aren't we pushing harder to let it?

Here's the route. We'll look at why the road death toll is so high, meet the basic idea of a self-driving car, look at the evidence so far, go through the hard cases, and then weigh what waiting costs.

Let's start with the human side. Why do people crash? Driving asks a lot of attention, and attention is limited. We get distracted by phones and passengers. We get tired, especially late at night or on long drives. Some of us drive after drinking, or speed, or misjudge a gap in the traffic. Government safety agencies study thousands of crash reports to work out what went wrong, and they consistently find that human factors are involved in the large majority of crashes. That isn't a judgement on people. It shows how hard the task is for a brain that gets bored, distracted and tired.

Now, what does a self-driving car actually do? The term covers a range. Engineers often describe levels from zero, where you do everything yourself, to five, where the car handles every situation on every road without a human. Most cars on the road today sit at the lower end, with features like adaptive cruise control or lane keeping. The systems that get the most attention are the ones that drive with nobody in the seat, in limited areas.

How does such a car see the road? It uses several sensors that work together. Cameras see colour, signs and lane markings. Radar measures distance and speed, and copes with fog and rain better than cameras do. Lidar builds a three-dimensional picture by bouncing laser pulses off objects. The software combines these streams, a step called sensor fusion, and builds a model of what's around it: cars, cyclists, pedestrians and the road ahead. A planning system then decides how to move. No single sensor is perfect, but together they cover one another's weaknesses.

Let's make that concrete. Picture the car's view as a set of tracked objects, each one a little box with a velocity arrow showing where it's heading. The system keeps updating those arrows many times a second. When a cyclist swerves, the arrow changes, and the car adjusts before a human might have noticed. The question isn't whether the car sees everything. It's whether it sees the right things quickly enough, every time.

Here's the statistic people quote most, and it's one that gets misread. You may have heard that human error is behind about 94 percent of crashes. That number comes from a government study that looked at the critical reason for each crash, the last failure that made it unavoidable. In those crashes, the driver was the critical reason. That doesn't mean removing drivers would remove 94 percent of crashes. Some crashes would still happen to a machine, from bad weather, bad roads or a fault. And some human-related crashes would still happen anyway. The figure is important, but it's a starting point, not a promise. Keep that in mind when you see the headline.

So what's the evidence that these systems do better? Some companies have published crash data from their own fleets in a small number of cities. In those areas, the reported rates of injury crashes per mile look lower than the human baseline. That's encouraging. But the comparison isn't clean. The fleets drive particular routes, at particular times, often avoiding the hardest conditions. Human drivers cover everyone: drunk drivers, older drivers, drivers in heavy snow, and cars with no safety features at all. The company data is also self-reported, and it covers a small number of miles next to the enormous total of human driving.

Then there are the hard cases. Snow that covers the lane lines. Construction zones that move the road. A police officer waving traffic through a red light. A plastic bag drifting across the road that the sensors read as a solid object. A cyclist riding in an unusual way. None of these is rare in the real world, and each one tests whether the system can handle the unexpected. Engineers spend a lot of effort on these edge cases, and they should. But each new case is a reminder that a system which performs well on average can still fail in a strange moment. Human drivers fail in strange moments too, which is part of why the comparison is so hard.

So let's do the arithmetic that matters. Suppose a system is meaningfully safer per mile than the average human driver. Each mile it drives is a mile with fewer injuries on average. If it's adopted across millions of miles, the total number of people hurt goes down, even though crashes will still happen. That's the logic behind the claim you hear. The question isn't whether the machine is perfect. It's whether it's better than the thing it replaces, and whether the margin is large enough to justify the change. The case for change is strong, because the baseline is so bad.

Now the wait. Every year that we delay, the same toll continues. The people who die on roads are not abstractions. They are drivers, passengers, cyclists and pedestrians, often young. Some argue we should wait until the technology is flawless. But nothing is flawless, and the human system isn't either. The honest comparison is between the current system and a better one, not between the current system and a perfect one. Some countries have adopted goals like Vision Zero, which aims to eliminate deaths and serious injuries from traffic altogether. That target is zero, not a small improvement.

There's also a messy middle period. For years, self-driving systems will share roads with human drivers, cyclists and pedestrians who behave unpredictably. That mix creates new problems, and it needs clear rules, careful testing and honest reporting of crashes. Good policy would publish that data openly, so everyone can see whether the systems are actually saving lives. Bad policy would hide it, or let the first high-profile failure decide everything.

So go back to the question at the start. How many people do you think die on roads every year? The answer is about 1.2 million worldwide. The real question is whether a driver that doesn't get tired, distracted or drunk could cut that number meaningfully. The evidence so far is encouraging but incomplete. And the cost of waiting is measured in lives. So ask yourself whether you'd want these cars on the road now, provided they can prove they're safer. I think the honest answer is yes, as long as we measure it carefully and keep the data in the open.
