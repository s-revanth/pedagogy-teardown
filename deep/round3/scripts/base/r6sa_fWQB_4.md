<<<NARRATION
How many people will ever be born? It sounds like a question that should have a clean answer, but the answer depends on what we assume about the future. Let's try to estimate it anyway, carefully, and see how much the answer moves when we change our assumptions.

Here is the plan. We will count what has already happened, then project what might happen, and finally check whether the projection holds up. The number we are after is not how many people are alive. It is how many people will ever be born. Those two numbers are different, and mixing them up is the most common mistake in this kind of estimate.

Think of it this way. A living population is a stock, like the water in a bathtub. Births are a flow into the tub, and deaths are a flow out. The stock at any moment is the result of all the past inflows minus the outflows. To count people who will ever exist, we care about the inflow, the births, summed over time.

Before we go any further, pause for a second. How many people do you think have ever lived? Take a guess, write it down if you like, and keep it in mind.

Let's count backwards. The population has been growing for a long time, but the early numbers are small. Estimates from historians and demographers put the population at a few million ten thousand years ago, a few hundred million at the start of the common era, and around a billion in the early nineteenth century. Combine those population sizes with rough birth rates, average lifespans, and the number of years between them, and you get a total. The best-known estimates land at roughly a hundred billion people who have ever lived. The uncertainty is large, perhaps tens of billions either way, because early birth rates are poorly recorded. But the order of magnitude is solid.

So a hundred billion people have lived. How does that compare with your guess? Many people guess far fewer. That gap is worth remembering, because it shows how hard it is to picture numbers that include the long tail of history.

Now the future. Today there are about eight billion people alive, and roughly one hundred and thirty million are born each year. That is a little over four births every second. This is the number that does most of the work in our estimate. If we know the birth rate and the time we are counting over, we can multiply and get a total.

Let's build a simple scenario. Suppose the population levels off at around ten billion, a figure that many demographic projections consider plausible. Suppose average lifespans stay near eighty years. Then a stable population of ten billion, with lives of about eighty years, needs roughly one hundred and twenty-five million births a year to stay flat. That is close to the birth rate we see today, which is why the plateau scenario is a natural anchor. Over the next century, that comes to about thirteen billion new people. Over a thousand years it comes to about one hundred and thirty billion. Add that to the hundred billion already lived, and the total for this scenario, over a thousand years, is somewhere above two hundred billion.

Now the hard part. How long could the human species last? The sun is slowly getting brighter, and within roughly a billion years Earth's surface will likely become too hot for complex life. That is a rough figure, and it has real uncertainty, but it gives us a time horizon. So let's ask: what if we kept today's birth rate for that whole billion years?

Try the arithmetic. One hundred and thirty million births a year, times a billion years, comes to about one hundred and thirty quadrillion people. That is more than a million times the total so far, and it is obviously absurd. Nobody believes the rate will stay flat for a billion years. Of course it won't. A population cannot grow without limit on a finite planet, and the wall in this calculation is the reminder that the rate must change.

That is the most useful part of this exercise. A constant rate looks harmless for a few centuries, and it turns into nonsense over long periods. So any honest estimate has to bend the curve somewhere. The question then becomes: where does it bend, and how hard?

Think of the bathtub again. If the inflow stays high while the outflow shrinks, the tub fills. If births fall, as they already have in many countries, the inflow shrinks. A total that depends on the whole future is really a bet on the shape of that curve. That is why the answers you will see range so widely. Some estimates keep births near today's level for a few hundred years and then stop. Others assume births fall quickly and the species shrinks. Others imagine a far larger civilisation spread across many planets.

Let's compare a few scenarios. In a plateau scenario, the total is in the low hundreds of billions over the next thousand years. In a shrinking scenario, where births fall below deaths within a century or two, the total barely exceeds the hundred billion already lived. In a scenario where the population keeps growing for a long time and settles at a much larger level, the total could reach into the trillions. None of these is a prediction. They are sensitivity checks, showing which assumption matters most.

It is worth being honest about what can move the answer. Extinction risk is one. A catastrophe that ends the species early cuts the total dramatically. Technology is another. Medicine that extends lifespans changes the number of births needed to hold a population steady. Settling other worlds could add a very large number of people, though that is speculative. Each of these changes the answer by an order of magnitude or more, which means our confidence in any single figure should be modest.

But that does not mean the exercise is useless. The estimate tells us something solid even with the uncertainty. The number of people who have ever lived is around a hundred billion. Future totals are likely to be measured in hundreds of billions, and could reach trillions only under assumptions that require sustained growth and expansion. Whatever the exact figure, the people alive today are a meaningful fraction of everyone who will ever exist, and that is a striking thing to realise.

Let's recap. We counted the past, roughly a hundred billion people. We projected the future from a birth rate of about one hundred and thirty million a year, and we saw that holding that rate for a billion years gives an impossible result. We compared scenarios and found that the total depends mostly on how the birth rate changes. And we noted the big uncertainties, extinction, technology and expansion beyond Earth, that could push the answer up or down.

So how many people might ever exist? Probably hundreds of billions, and possibly far more if we manage to thrive for a very long time. But the more interesting takeaway is the method. Find the flow, multiply by the time, check the extreme case, and notice where the curve has to bend. You can use the same steps on almost anything that has a rate and a duration.

That is all for this estimate. Thanks for watching.
