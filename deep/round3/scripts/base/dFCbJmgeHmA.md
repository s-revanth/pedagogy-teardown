# Narration

<<<NARRATION
Imagine an ordinary day about sixty-six million years ago. The sky is clear, the forests are green and the seas are warm and shallow over what is now the Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico. Dinosaurs are everywhere. Some are the size of buses, some are the size of chickens, and none of them have any idea what is coming.

Somewhere far above, a rock is falling. It is roughly ten kilometers across, and it is moving at about twenty kilometers every second. That is faster than any bullet, faster than a rocket can take you out of Earth's grip. In a few minutes, it will change the planet.

This is the story of that day, told as a clock. We will go through the first minutes, then the hours, then the days, months and years that followed. It is one of the best-documented catastrophes in Earth's history, and every step of it leaves a trace.

First, the world as it was. The late Cretaceous was a warm planet. There was no ice at the poles to speak of, the sea levels were high and shallow seas covered large parts of the continents. Forests covered much of the land, filled with flowering plants, insects and birds' ancestors. The dinosaurs were not fading. They were thriving. Tyrannosaurs, duck-billed herbivores, horned giants and tiny feathered hunters shared the land with mammals that were still small and mostly nocturnal. The planet looked stable, the way it has looked to every generation that did not know what was about to happen.

Now, minute zero. The asteroid hits the shallow sea where the rock is rich in sulfur and carbonate. The energy release is staggering. The impact releases something like a hundred million megatons of TNT, millions of times the largest nuclear weapon ever tested. In an instant, a crater nearly two hundred kilometers wide opens in the seabed and the crust under it.

Within the first minutes, that crater blasts hot rock and vapor high into the sky. Much of it leaves Earth's atmosphere, and then falls back in a blazing rain. This is the ejecta, and it will spread across the globe. Some of it lands in the Americas, some in Europe and in Asia. The rock that falls back down is not just dust. It is molten and glowing, hot enough to heat the surface far away.

Between about ten minutes and an hour, the most dangerous effect begins. As the ejecta re-enters the atmosphere, it glows, and that glow is a massive pulse of infrared heat falling on the surface of the planet. Think of an oven door opening over the whole world. Anything exposed to open sky gets a fast, intense burn. Forests catch fire far beyond the crater. Animals that were in the open, under the sky, have seconds to find cover. Some survive by burrowing, hiding in water or crouching in shadow. Many do not.

Within the first hours, the shock waves have done their work. Earthquakes ripple out from the crater. Tsunamis, waves of enormous height, sweep across coastlines near the impact and surge across the seas. The shoreline changes. Sediment is torn up and deposited in layers far from where it came from. And the wildfires that began with the heat pulse spread across continents. Smoke rises into the sky in columns that can be seen for huge distances.

Then comes the day. The fires keep burning, and the smoke thickens. Sunlight is weakened. The air fills with dust and fine particles, and the sky starts to darken. The heavy rock blasted out of the impact also contains lots of sulfur, and that sulfur is lofted high into the upper atmosphere. There it forms tiny droplets of sulfate aerosols that reflect sunlight away from Earth. This is the start of what scientists call an impact winter.

Over weeks and months, the darkness deepens. Plants that depend on sunlight begin to die. Leaves in the forests wither and fall. In the oceans, the microscopic plankton at the base of the food web, which depend on sunlight too, collapse in huge numbers. Animals that eat plants go hungry, and animals that eat those animals go hungry next. The food chain does not break at the top. It breaks at the bottom, and it breaks everywhere at once.

Over months and years, the planet cools. The surface temperature drops, and the cold lasts long enough to kill many of the animals that had survived the first burn. Scientists debate exactly how long the darkness and cold lasted. Estimates range from months to a few years, and different models produce different timelines. But the direction is clear. The world that had been warm, green and busy became dim, cold and hungry in a very short span of geological time.

So how do we know all this? Three kinds of evidence come together. The first is the crater itself, buried under the Yucatán. It was found through geophysical surveys that measured an enormous circular structure of rock and gravity. The second is a thin layer of clay found in rock layers around the world, which is dense with the metal iridium. Iridium is rare on Earth's surface, but common in asteroids. A layer rich in it, laid down at the same time everywhere, is a signature of something from space. The third is the fossil record. Above that layer, the fossils change dramatically, and many groups simply disappear.

It is worth saying clearly, though, that the dinosaurs did not all vanish at once in a single heartbeat. Some groups had already been in decline, and the wider ecosystem was changing for millions of years before the impact. But the impact was the decisive blow. Roughly three quarters of all species on Earth were wiped out in this event. Non-avian dinosaurs, the big ones and the small ones, were gone. The clock stopped for them.

And volcanism may have added stress before the impact. Around the same time, huge volcanic eruptions in what is now India released enormous amounts of gas and lava. Scientists still debate how much that contributed, and how the two events interacted. The impact was a massive disaster on its own, but the planet may already have been under strain.

Yet not everything died. Many animals survived, and the ones that did had certain traits. Small animals that could burrow, hide or live on very little food had an advantage. Animals that could eat seeds, or that could survive without much green food, had a better chance. Some birds, descendants of the theropod dinosaurs, made it through. And the small mammals, which had been living in the shadows of the dinosaur era, survived in large numbers. The planet would take millions of years to recover, but when it did, the mammals began to diversify into the forms we know today.

So the dinosaurs did not entirely vanish. Birds are dinosaurs. The sparrow on your windowsill and the eagle in the sky are living descendants of a lineage that stretches back to the Cretaceous. The rest of the dinosaurs were erased, but the story did not end there.

That is the day the dinosaurs died. Not in an instant, but in a sequence of minutes, hours, months and years. A rock arrived from space, and the world changed. Every step left a mark, and we can read them all in the layers of the Earth.
