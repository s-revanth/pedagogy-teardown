## Narration

<<<NARRATION
Here's a question that sounds like it should have a vague answer. How much of the Earth can you see at once? Standing on a beach, you might think you can take in a huge chunk of the planet, because the ocean goes on forever. But the honest answer is that it depends on how high you are, and the numbers change faster than most people expect.

The short version is this. From the ground, you see a tiny fraction of the surface. From the Moon, you see almost half of it. In between, there's a clean geometric rule that tells you exactly how the view grows as you climb. Let's build it step by step.

To make the problem simple, pretend the Earth is a perfect ball, and pretend your eye is a single point. Hold a pin above the ball and imagine a line from your eye that just grazes the surface. Anything beyond the point where that line touches is hidden behind the curve. Everything closer is visible. So the question becomes a question about tangent lines to a sphere.

Here's the first useful result. Call the radius of the Earth R, and call your height above the surface h. The distance from you to the horizon, along the surface, is roughly the square root of two times R times h. That's the formula for a sensible height, where h is much smaller than R. Let's check it with something you can picture.

Stand on a beach with your eyes about 1.7 metres above the water. Plug that in, using a radius of about 6,371 kilometres, and you get a horizon roughly 4.7 kilometres away. That's a little more than a few miles. Beyond that point, the sea curves away from your line of sight, and the ship on the far side of the horizon is hidden, its hull disappearing first.

Now, that horizon distance tells you how far you can see along the surface. But we want area, the amount of the planet inside your view. Imagine the visible part of the surface as a cap, a dome-shaped patch on the ball. The area of a cap like that is two pi times R times h. Divide that by the total area of the Earth, which is four pi R squared, and the R's cancel. What's left is h over two R. So the fraction of the planet you can see is simply your height divided by twice the radius.

Let's test that on a mountain. Take a summit around 8,850 metres high, which is roughly the height of Mount Everest. The horizon from the top is about 330 kilometres away, which is a large distance on the ground, and it sounds impressive. But the fraction is still under a tenth of a percent of the planet. A few hundred kilometres of horizon is a tiny sliver of a sphere more than twelve thousand kilometres across.

Now move up to an airliner. At about 10 kilometres of altitude, the horizon is roughly 350 kilometres away. The visible fraction is around a twelfth of one per cent, which is still very small, even though the view from the window looks enormous. This is a good moment to notice how the human sense of scale is fooled by the flat-looking land below. The curve is hard to see locally, so your brain assumes the cap is much bigger than it is.

Next, the International Space Station, which flies at around 400 kilometres. Plug that in and the horizon is roughly 2,300 kilometres away. The visible cap is about 16 million square kilometres. That's around twice the size of Australia. As a share of the whole planet, it comes to a few per cent, which is still less than a twentieth of the surface. So from the station you see a broad, round region of the planet at once, but never close to all of it.

Here's a way to picture why the growth is slow. The cap is like a lid on a ball. Make the lid taller, and it covers more, but the lid only spreads gradually, because the surface keeps curving away from the edge. You need a lot of height before the lid covers a large share of the ball.

That's where the Moon comes in, and it requires a different formula. When you are very far from the Earth, the tangent-line approach still works, but now the distance to the Earth's centre dominates. Let d be the distance from your eye to the centre of the Earth. The visible fraction of the surface is one half of one minus the ratio of R to d. For a distant observer, the cap becomes close to a hemisphere.

Plug in the Moon's distance, which is about 384,000 kilometres from the centre of the Earth. The ratio of R to d is tiny, a little under two per cent. So the visible fraction is one half of about 0.98, which is a little under half. From the Moon, you see almost half of the planet's surface at any one time, and the rest is hidden on the far side. Think about that for a moment. The whole Earth is a huge object in the sky, but even from there, one glance covers less than half of it.

Now let's be careful, because some details shift the numbers a little. Atmospheric refraction bends light near the surface, so the real horizon is slightly farther than the geometric one, by a few percent. Terrain blocks things. A mountain range might hide the ocean. Clouds, haze and the curvature of your local landscape can make a big difference in practice. And the cap is an idealised shape, so the figures here are best read as clean estimates rather than survey results. But the big picture holds: the geometry sets the upper limit, and the real world only reduces what you can see.

The same mathematics applies in other places. A ship on the open sea disappears hull first, because the curve of the water hides the lower part before the upper. The horizon for a lookout on a mast is farther than the horizon for someone on the deck, and that difference is exactly the square-root rule at work. Put a camera on a moon orbiting another planet, and the same formula tells you what fraction of that planet's surface it can see. The rule doesn't care what kind of ball you're standing on.

Let's recap the ladder. A person on a beach sees a few kilometres of horizon and a tiny share of the surface. An airliner sees a few hundred kilometres. A mountain summit sees a bit more. The space station sees a cap roughly twice the size of Australia, a few per cent of the planet. And the Moon sees almost half, because it is far enough away that the whole disc of the Earth fits into a single view.

So go back to the question you started with. Did you guess a lot? Most people do. The surprise is not that the cap grows, it's how slowly it grows when you're close, and how fast it grows when you're far away. Height matters, but the planet is much larger than your intuition.

Next time you look out across a flat expanse of water or land, and it seems to go on forever, remember the lid on the ball. Your eye is a point above a sphere, and the horizon you see is the exact spot where the surface starts to turn away from you. The whole planet is out there, curving quietly past the edge of your view.
