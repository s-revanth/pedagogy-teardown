<<<NARRATION
Last time, Rome and Carthage went to war over Sicily, and Rome had one big problem. It was a land power. It had armies that could march anywhere. And it had almost no ships. Carthage owned the sea. So this time, the question is simple: how does a land power fight a war at sea?

Here's the answer up front, so you can relax a bit. Rome wins, but it's going to take a lot of wrecked ships and a lot of stubbornness to get there.

Quick recap, because we need the stakes. Carthage is a big trading empire with a strong navy and a long coastline. Rome is pushing into Sicily, and that means a fight with Carthage over the island, which is valuable for farming and trade. Rome has been winning on land, but Carthage keeps the seas and can keep supplies flowing. So as long as Rome stays on the ground, it can keep winning cities and still not win the war.

So Rome needs ships. And the Romans make an interesting decision. They don't invent a navy from scratch. They copy one.

The story goes like this. A Carthaginian warship runs aground near Rome's forces. It's a fancy quinquereme, a five-row galley, and it's basically a gift. The Romans take it apart, study it, and build their own copies, quickly. Think of it as reverse engineering on a deadline. They don't have skilled sailors, and they don't have a tradition of naval battles. So they have to train their crews while they build the ships. It's a bit like building a race car and learning to drive it on the same weekend.

Now here's the clever part. Rome doesn't just try to out-sail Carthage. Romans know their strength is fighting in close quarters, like infantry. So they come up with a device called the corvus. The corvus is a boarding bridge. It's a long plank with a spike on the end that swings down and pins the enemy ship to the deck. Roman soldiers then run across and fight on the enemy ship as if it were a field.

Think about what that does. The Carthaginians are good sailors. They can ram, dodge, and out-manoeuvre a clumsy fleet. But if the Roman boarding bridge hooks you, the sea battle becomes a land battle. And a land battle is exactly what Rome is good at.

The first big test is Mylae. Rome's fleet sails out and meets a Carthaginian navy, and the corvus does its job. Ships get hooked, and Roman soldiers swarm across the decks. It is Rome's first major naval victory, and it's a shock to Carthage. Carthage was supposed to be the sea power. Now the Romans have just taken a few of its ships in a way that a pure sailing expert would not expect.

Then comes Ecnomus, which is one of the largest naval battles ever fought in the ancient world. We're talking hundreds of ships on each side. It's a massive clash, with formations and wings and a lot of chaos. Rome wins again, and it's a big win. After Ecnomus, the Romans have control of the sea long enough to do something bold: invade Africa.

This is where Marcus Atilius Regulus enters the story. He lands near Carthage with an army and starts taking towns. Carthage panics. It's not supposed to be possible to invade the homeland. But Regulus is making real progress. Then Carthage hires a new commander, a Spartan mercenary named Xanthippus, who reorganises the army and beats Regulus badly near Carthage. Regulus is captured.

Now, the legend. The usual story says that Carthage lets Regulus go to Rome to negotiate peace, and that he comes back to Carthage, because he promised to, and is then executed in horrible ways. It's a great story. It's also very likely embellished. Ancient writers loved stories about honour and sacrifice, and this one has everything. So I'd treat the details with caution, even though the broader defeat is well attested.

Meanwhile, back at sea, things go wrong. Rome loses two entire fleets to storms. Think of it as a series of bad weather events that turn a winning war into a series of expensive mistakes. Hundreds of ships, a lot of men, and a lot of money, gone in a few bad storms.

But Rome does not quit. It rebuilds the fleet. And part of the way it does that is through private donations. Wealthy citizens chip in to fund new ships. It's a kind of wartime fundraising that you rarely see in ancient Rome, and it shows just how serious the government is about winning.

Next, Drepana, around 249 BC. This is a painful Roman defeat. The Roman commander Publius Claudius Pulcher attacks a Carthaginian fleet near the port, and it goes badly. The Romans lose a lot of ships and men.

And now, the sacred chickens. The story goes that before the battle, Claudius wants to check the omens. The sacred chickens are supposed to eat, and if they eat eagerly, that's a good sign. But the chickens refuse to eat. So Claudius, reportedly, gets fed up and says something like, then let them drink, and throws the chickens into the sea. Then he attacks anyway, and loses.

Now, I am going to be honest with you. This story comes from ancient sources, and it's probably as much a moral tale as a record of what happened. The Romans like the idea that a reckless commander ignored the gods and paid for it. So it makes a great story, but it may tell you more about how Romans wanted to think about their failures than about what really happened on the day.

Meanwhile, on land, the Romans start a long siege of Lilybaeum, a major Carthaginian stronghold in western Sicily. This goes on for years. Carthage has a commander named Hamilcar Barca, who holds out on Mount Eryx, a strong position overlooking the area. The siege is slow, boring, and brutal. It's a war of attrition, where patience and money matter more than brilliant battles.

Then comes the end. In 241 BC, Rome sends another fleet, funded and built with that same mix of state money and private donations. It sails to the Aegates Islands, off the western coast of Sicily, and it catches a Carthaginian fleet that is trying to resupply its forces on Sicily. The Carthaginian ships are loaded with supplies and are not built for a real fight. Rome wins decisively.

After that, Carthage has no way to keep its army supplied in Sicily. It sues for peace. The deal is that Carthage gives up Sicily, pays a large indemnity to Rome, and agrees to stay out of certain areas. It's a heavy price, and it leaves Carthage with money problems and angry soldiers.

So here's the lesson. Rome was a land power that had no navy, and it learned to build one fast. It turned sea battles into land battles with the corvus. It paid for its mistakes by rebuilding. And it outlasted a power that was ahead at sea on paper. Adaptation beat advantage, and that's a pattern that keeps showing up in history.

But this war is not the end of the story for Carthage. There are angry mercenaries coming home from Sicily, unpaid and furious, and Hamilcar Barca has his own plans. Next time, we're going to see what happens when a defeated power has to pay its own soldiers, and what that does to the Mediterranean.

Thanks for watching, and remember, don't let the chickens drink.
