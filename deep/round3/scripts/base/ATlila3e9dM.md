<<<NARRATION
Let's start with a guy who wanted to be a painter. He applied to art school, got turned down, and tried again, and got turned down again. Nobody at the time would have guessed that this would end with a dictatorship and a world war.

Here's the big answer up front, so we know where this is headed. This part covers how Adolf Hitler moves from a rejected artist to the leader of a small political party, and how that party tries to seize power in 1923. This is the setup for everything that comes next, so it's worth taking seriously.

Quick roadmap. We're going to cover his childhood in Austria, the years in Vienna, the First World War, the chaos after the war, and then the first big attempt at taking power.

Hitler was born in 1889 in Braunau am Inn, a town on the border of Austria and Bavaria. His father, Alois, was a customs official. He wanted his son to follow him into a stable civil service career. Hitler had other ideas. He liked drawing, he liked dreaming about art, and he did not like being told what to do. The family moved to Linz, and the conflict between father and son kept going.

By his late teens, Hitler wanted to study art in Vienna. He applied to the Academy of Fine Arts, and he was rejected. He tried again the next year, and was rejected again. So the dream of a painter's career basically ended there. What followed were years in Vienna, mostly broke, scraping by on small jobs and selling paintings and postcards. It's a lonely, bitter period, and his own later accounts of it are heavily shaped by his own story. So be careful with the romantic version.

In 1913 he moves to Munich. And then the First World War breaks out in 1914. Hitler volunteers for the German army, which is a big moment in his personal story, because he gets a sense of purpose that he never really had before. He serves on the Western Front as a dispatch runner, carrying messages between units under fire. It's dangerous work. He is decorated for bravery, including an Iron Cross, which makes him proud for the rest of his life.

Now, a quick caveat. A lot of what we know about his wartime self-image comes from his own writing and speeches, and he had every reason to make himself look heroic. So when you hear a story about how brave he was, check where it came from before you believe it.

In the last months of the war, Hitler is gassed, probably with mustard gas, and temporarily loses his sight. He's sent to a hospital in Pasewalk, and it's there that he hears about the armistice in November 1918. For a lot of German soldiers, that feels like a humiliating collapse, and the mood in Germany is bitter.

This is where a myth gets its start. The story goes that the army didn't lose the war on the battlefield, but was stabbed in the back by traitors at home, like socialists and Jews. It's a powerful story, because it lets people avoid facing the real reasons for defeat. And it's wrong. The German army was exhausted, short of supplies, and facing an enormous Allied force. But the myth spreads anyway, and it becomes a foundation for a lot of the resentment in the years after the war.

Hitler returns to Munich in 1919, and the army hires him to do political education work. Part of his job is to watch small political groups and report on them. In September 1919, he goes to a meeting of a tiny party called the German Workers' Party. He's supposed to be a spy. Instead, he joins.

And here's where the speaking skills come in. Hitler turns out to be a very good public speaker. He's loud, emotional, and he knows how to get a crowd going. He starts drawing people in, and he takes over the party's publicity. Over the next few years, the party gets a new name, the National Socialist German Workers' Party, which everyone shortens to Nazi, and it starts building its own street fighting group, the SA.

Those years matter because they show how a man with no office, no army, and no real money can build a movement. He had a message that blamed others for Germany's problems, a gift for the stage, and a party that needed someone to take charge. Nobody handed him power. He took the room, one speech at a time.

By 1923, the economy is in chaos. Inflation is out of control, and the German currency is nearly worthless. Hitler sees an opportunity. In November 1923, he and his allies try to take over the government of Bavaria in Munich, in what's called the Beer Hall Putsch. It's a clumsy attempt. The plan falls apart quickly, police open fire, and several people are killed. Hitler is arrested and put on trial.

The trial becomes a stage for him. He uses it to attack the Weimar Republic, the government in Berlin, and to present himself as a patriot. He's sentenced to five years in prison, but he serves only a short time before being released.

While he's in prison at Landsberg, he dictates a long book that mixes autobiography and political ideas. It's called Mein Kampf. At this point, it's not a bestseller, and most people don't read it. But it lays out a lot of the ideas that will matter later: hatred, expansion, and a racist worldview. It becomes a blueprint for what comes next, and it's one of the most important, and most disturbing, books of the twentieth century.

So here's where we are. A rejected artist becomes a soldier, then a myth-builder, then a party leader, then a failed revolutionary, and then a prisoner with a manifesto. The next part is where the party starts growing, and where the economic crisis changes everything.

Thanks for watching.
