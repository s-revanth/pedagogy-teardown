<<<NARRATION
Picture a small drone, about the size of a dinner plate, hovering over a field. It's cheap. It has a camera, a battery and a little software. Now imagine that nobody is holding the controls. The drone looks at what's in front of it, decides what counts as a target, and acts. Would you be comfortable with that? Most people say no right away. But the question is moving from theory to practice, so it's worth thinking it through carefully.

Here's the plan. We'll pin down what people mean by an autonomous weapon, compare a simple automatic door to the new capability, look at why cheap drones change the maths, trace the diplomacy since about 2014, and end with the honest uncertainty about what is happening on battlefields.

First, a definition, because the word autonomous gets used loosely. A drone piloted by a person from a distance is not autonomous. A human watches the video feed and chooses each strike. Researchers usually sort the rest into three levels. In the loop means a human approves every target. On the loop means the machine selects and engages targets, but a person can watch and stop it. Out of the loop means the system picks targets and fires, and no human can step in in time. Most of the debate is about the last category and the middle one.

Here's an everyday analogy. An automatic door has a sensor and a rule. When someone steps near, the sensor fires, and the door opens. Nobody decides to open it each time. The rule does. That's a very simple autonomous system, and it's harmless because the stakes are low. Now replace the door with something that can move, see and carry a payload. The structure is similar: sense, decide, act. The difference is the stakes, and the rule has to recognise a person, a vehicle or a target in a messy world.

Now add cost and numbers. Imagine a swarm of a few thousand small drones, each cheap enough to be disposable. Suppose one operator can supervise many of them. The old constraint was that every weapon needed a trained person at the controls, and that limited how many could be used at once. Remove that constraint and the economics change. One person can, in principle, launch a large number of machines, and each one can pick its own path. A cheap machine that doesn't need a pilot is a very different thing from an expensive aircraft that does. The cost of mass is no longer the limit.

Compare this with older shifts in warfare. Each new technology, from the longbow to the aircraft, changed who could do harm and from how far away. Autonomy continues that story, with one difference. The machine can make the decision as well as carry it out. That is what makes it feel new, and it's why the usual analogies only go part of the way.

That's where the problem hits a wall. Scale creates situations that no single human can track. If a swarm misidentifies a target, you may not find out until much later. If every machine shares one flaw in its recognition software, the same mistake repeats across the whole swarm. And if a machine makes a bad decision, who is responsible? The manufacturer, the programmer, the commander who launched it, or the operator who wasn't watching? The laws of armed conflict assume that a person is accountable for decisions to use force. Machines complicate that chain, because the decision is spread across hardware, code, training data and orders. Militaries and courts already assign responsibility for mistakes made by people under orders, but the chain gets much longer when a machine sits in the middle.

So let's ask the question directly. If a machine chooses a target and gets it wrong, who answers for that? Pause on it, because it's harder than it sounds. Some argue that machines could reduce certain harms, because they don't get tired, frightened or angry. Others argue that taking the human out of the decision lowers the threshold for starting a fight, because the cost in lives on your own side goes down. Both points deserve a hearing.

The diplomacy has been running for more than a decade. Starting around 2014, states began talks on lethal autonomous weapons under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, a United Nations framework for weapons that can cause excessive injury or harm civilians. Those talks produced guiding principles, including that humans must keep meaningful control and that existing international law applies. But as far as I know, there is still no binding treaty that bans or restricts these systems. Many states want one. Others argue that existing rules are enough, or that a ban is premature while the technology changes so quickly.

Now the battlefield question, which is where the caveats matter most. A 2021 United Nations panel report on the conflict in Libya described a drone that may have attacked retreating forces without a remote operator directly choosing the target. The report's wording was careful, and the details are disputed. Reports from other recent conflicts describe drones with varying levels of automation. The problem is that much of this evidence comes from partial sources, from the parties involved, or from accounts that can't be independently checked. It's easy to overstate, and it's easy to dismiss.

So what does a sensible view look like? I think autonomy in weapons is not a single switch. It's a set of choices about how much a person decides, how much a machine decides, and how the two are checked against each other. The real policy question is which of those choices we want made by a person, and which ones we are willing to hand over. Those choices are not only technical. They touch law, military doctrine and public trust, and they will be made by someone, whether or not we make them deliberately.

So go back to the small drone over the field. Whether it's the first of its kind or the thousandth, the design choices are already being made, one component and one line of code at a time. Ask yourself the question we started with. Who decides, and who answers for it?
